# Risk and compliance

> **Area:** `risk-and-compliance` · owned by the COO skill. **Use when:** Price the duties a business must meet and the external risks it carries: the landed cost of anything imported or cross-border, eligibility to enter or sell to a government, licences and permits, product certification, data protection, ESG and green claims, carbon-credit and other environmental-attribute revenue, and a risk register with owners. Use for ANY plan, budget or model with an imported or cross-border input cost (hardware, components, finished goods), even when nobody asked a trade question; and when entering a market, launching a product, making an environmental claim, selling carbon credits, or when a tariff or rule change is in the news.

**Outcome:** (1) for each imported input or product, a landed cost per unit (a range when no quote exists) and an eligibility verdict, with the legal source and date beside every rate; (2) a compliance register of every duty the business must meet, each with an owner and evidence; (3) a risk register that prices each external risk and names who watches it.

## When to use
- "What does this product really cost delivered to our warehouse in country X?"
- Choosing between sourcing countries, plants or shipping lanes.
- Quoting a foreign customer: which incoterm, who pays duty, who carries the risk.
- Bidding for government, military or public-sector contracts with domestic-content rules.
- A new tariff, trade-remedy case or rule change is announced: "does this hit us, and from when?"
- **Any** business plan, budget or unit-economics model with an imported or cross-border cost line (devices, solar kits, machines, components, spare parts), even if the question is about value, pricing or funding. Imported-cost errors can flip a plan's value.
- Entering a market or launching a product: which licences, permits and certification marks gate the sale.
- Making an environmental, social or "green" claim, or reporting ESG data to a customer, lender or regulator.
- A plan earns revenue from carbon credits, renewable certificates or other environmental attributes.
- Building or reviewing a risk register, or a lender or investor asks for one.

Related skills: `market-entry-and-go-to-market` (which mode and where), `disruption-exposure-mapping` (route and supplier risk on the same lanes), `pricing-strategy` (passing duty through to price), `supply-contracts-and-coordination` (who bears cost changes in supplier contracts), `relevant-cost-decisions` (which landed-cost items are relevant to a make, buy or source choice), `risk-decisions-under-uncertainty` (which assumptions are critical; act now or wait), `disruption-response-planning` (triggers and responses).

## The rule that governs this skill
Trade rates, thresholds, regulations, standards and carbon prices change often, sometimes weekly, and are litigated. **Never state a rate or threshold from memory or from this file as a fact.** For every figure used:
1. Look it up in the primary source (list in step 3).
2. Record beside it: the source (document, section or heading), the date published or effective, and the date you checked.
3. Check for anything published after that date that amends it, and for effective dates and transition rules (goods in transit, contracts signed before a date).
4. If only secondary commentary (law-firm notes, news, broker summaries) is available, label the figure "unverified" and say so in the answer.

All rates, prices and thresholds in this file are illustrative.

## Method, part A: imported and cross-border costs

### 1. Classify the product
- Every import is classified under the Harmonized System (HS): 6 digits shared worldwide, extended nationally (the US uses a 10-digit HTS; the EU an 8-digit CN plus TARIC codes). The code drives duty rate, origin rule, quotas, trade remedies and licensing.
- Classify by the legal notes and the General Rules of Interpretation, not by the product's marketing name. Material, construction, use and (for garments) gender and knit versus woven all matter.
- Look for binding rulings on similar goods: the US CBP rulings database (CROSS); EU Binding Tariff Information (EBTI). When the stakes are high, request your own binding ruling before committing volume.
- One product can have several plausible codes with very different rates. Record the chosen code, the reasoning, and the alternatives rejected.

### 2. Determine origin and marking
Origin is not where the goods shipped from; it is where they were made under the applicable rule. There are two kinds of rule:
- **Non-preferential origin** (for normal duties, extra tariffs, marking, government buying): in the US mainly "substantial transformation" (a new article with a new name, character or use); textiles and apparel have their own rules, generally keyed to where the garment is wholly assembled or the fabric formed.
- **Preferential origin** (to claim a free-trade-agreement rate): rule set out per product in the agreement, usually a change in tariff classification, a regional value content percentage, or a specific process. Textile rules are often "yarn forward": the yarn, fabric and sewing must all happen in the member countries.
- Keep the proof: bill of materials with origin of each input, supplier declarations, production records. Preference claims are audited after the fact.
- **Marking:** most imports must show the country of origin legibly on the article or its container (in the US, 19 USC 1304 and its regulations). Mismarking is a separate violation.

### 3. Build the duty stack, as of the decision date
Duty on a line is often several layers. List each one separately:

| Layer | What it is | Where to check (US example; use the equivalent elsewhere) |
|---|---|---|
| Base (MFN) duty | Normal rate for the HS code | National tariff schedule (US HTS, published by the USITC) |
| Preference | Reduced or zero rate under an agreement, if origin rules are met | The agreement's text and the schedule's special-rate column |
| Additional tariffs | Extra duties by country or product (national-security, retaliation, reciprocal or emergency-powers tariffs) | Official gazette (US Federal Register), the schedule's chapter 99 headings, customs guidance messages |
| Trade remedies | Anti-dumping and countervailing duties, specific to product, country and sometimes producer | Orders and scope rulings in the gazette; the trade-remedy authority's case list |
| Fees | Customs processing and port fees, usually ad valorem with floors and caps | Customs regulations and the current fee schedule |
| Import taxes | VAT or GST in most countries (not in the US); excise on some goods | National tax authority |

Also check, for each layer: whether it stacks with others or replaces them; exclusions and exemptions; whether it applies by country of origin or country of export; and whether its legal basis is under court challenge. Additional tariffs imposed under emergency or executive powers have been challenged in court; check the current status, not last month's.

Customs value differs by country: the US generally values goods excluding international freight and insurance; the EU includes them (CIF value). Duty on the same shipment therefore differs by more than the rate.

### 4. Check government-buying rules, if selling to the public sector
Outline only; **thresholds change; verify** every one in the current regulation.
- **US Berry Amendment** (10 USC 4862, implemented in DFARS 225.70 and clause 252.225-7012): Defense Department purchases of food, clothing, textiles, fibres, tents and some other items must be wholly grown and produced in the US, down to the fibre. Exceptions include purchases below the simplified acquisition threshold, items not available domestically in sufficient quality or quantity, and some purchases abroad. Specialty metals have a separate rule.
- **Buy American Act** (41 USC 8301-8305; FAR Part 25): federal supply purchases must be domestic end products made in the US with a minimum share of domestic component cost. The share has been rising on a set schedule; check the current figure and the price evaluation preferences.
- **Trade Agreements Act** (19 USC 2501 and following; FAR 25.4): above a threshold set for each period, agencies may buy only from the US or designated countries (agreement partners), and the Buy American preference is waived for those countries. Origin uses substantial transformation.
- **EU defence and security procurement** (Directive 2009/81/EC and national laws): member states may restrict or favour European supply for defence and sensitive contracts; newer EU defence-industry programmes add European-content conditions. Check the specific tender and programme rules.
- For each contract: which rule applies, which exception, the threshold as of today, and the clause in the solicitation. The solicitation's clauses are the binding text for that bid.

### 5. Check low-value parcel (de minimis) regimes
Many countries let low-value shipments enter duty-free or with simplified entry. These regimes have changed sharply and repeatedly in recent years (suspensions, removals, per-item fees, country-specific carve-outs) in the US and the EU among others. Do not build a direct-to-consumer model on a de minimis assumption without checking the rule in force on the ship date and any announced end date.

### 6. Fix the incoterm and title
Incoterms (current edition, 2020) fix where cost and risk pass from seller to buyer. They do **not** transfer title; title passes when the sales contract says so. Write both.
- **EXW / FCA:** buyer arranges main carriage; buyer is normally importer of record and pays duty.
- **FOB / CFR / CIF** (sea only): seller loads on board; risk passes at loading; under CFR and CIF the seller pays freight (and under CIF minimum insurance) but risk is still the buyer's from loading.
- **DAP / DPU:** seller delivers to the destination; buyer clears import and pays duty.
- **DDP:** seller pays duty and acts as importer of record in the buyer's country: needs a local registration or agent and carries every tariff change until delivery.
- A seller quoting CIF or DDP at a fixed price carries freight and duty increases until the price resets. Add a cost-adjustment clause or a validity date.

### 7. Use duty deferral and recovery
- **Bonded or customs warehouses** and **free or foreign-trade zones:** duty is paid only when goods leave for the home market; re-exports pay none. Useful for regional hubs and uncertain demand.
- **Duty drawback:** most of the duty paid can be refunded when imported goods (or goods made from them) are exported or destroyed, within time limits and with records.
- **Inward processing** (EU) or zone manufacturing: import inputs duty-suspended, process, re-export.
- Check whether additional tariffs or trade-remedy duties are excluded from these reliefs; recent tariff measures have limited them.

### 8. Keep the supply-chain and forced-labour file
Several jurisdictions bar goods made with forced labour: the US by 19 USC 1307, enforced by detention orders and, for one region of China, a legal presumption of forced labour that the importer must rebut with evidence; the EU by a forced-labour regulation phasing in. Keep, per product: supplier map to raw material (for cotton, to the farm or gin), purchase orders, invoices, production and transport records, audits, and traceability test results. Without the file, goods can be held at the border indefinitely. Unseen subcontracting is where this risk hides.

### 9. Build landed cost per unit
Landed cost = ex-works price + inland freight to port + international freight + insurance + duty (all layers, on the correct customs value) + customs brokerage and fees + destination port and handling + inland freight to warehouse + carrying cost of inventory in transit and at destination (unit value × annual holding rate × weeks in pipeline ÷ 52) + compliance cost (testing, audits, bonds), allocated per unit.

Then compare lanes in one table: origin, incoterm, each cost line, total per unit, lead time, eligibility (preference, government buying, forced-labour risk), and the source and date of every rate. Recompute when any rate, fee or freight quote changes.

### 10. Fallback landed cost when no supplier quote exists
A plan still needs an imported cost before quotes arrive. Build it as a **range**, never a point, and label it "estimate, no quote":

| Line | How to estimate | Typical range (illustrative; verify) |
|---|---|---|
| Ex-works benchmark | Lowest credible of: B2B marketplace listings at the plan's volume, a competitor's published bill of materials or teardown, import-record unit values for the HS code | Low = best listing; high = listing × 1.3 for spec, certification and small volume |
| Freight and insurance | Container or air rate ÷ units per container (by volume or weight, whichever binds); insurance about 0.3–0.5% of value | Dense sea goods 2–8% of ex-works; bulky or air 10–30% |
| Duty | Full duty stack (step 3) on the destination's customs value; if the code is uncertain, use the higher plausible code | Base plus any additional tariff; run with and without |
| Clearance | Broker fee per entry ÷ units per entry, plus port, inspection and customs fees | 1–3% of value for full containers; far more for small lots |
| Inland | Port to site, plus last-mile for remote or rural sites | 1–5%; remote sites higher |
| FX buffer | Allowance for currency moves between quote and payment | 3–10% of the foreign-currency share, by currency volatility and the payment lag |

- Landed low = sum of lows; landed high = sum of highs. Carry both into the model and test the decision at the high end (`risk-decisions-under-uncertainty`, step 5b).
- Replace each line with a quote as quotes arrive; keep the date of every figure.

## Method, part B: duties the business must meet

### 11. Keep a compliance register
One row per obligation. Columns:

| Obligation | Source (law, regulation, regulator, contract) | Applies because | Owner | Evidence held | Review or renewal date | Penalty if missed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- Build it by walking the business: each country, site, product, activity (import, sell, employ, collect data, make claims, discharge, sell credits) and each customer or lender contract with compliance clauses.
- Rank rows by penalty × chance of a miss; the top rows get a named owner and a calendar reminder before the review date.
- Review quarterly and on every new product, market, site or law change. A register with no owners or no evidence column is a list, not a control.

### 12. Licences and permits
- Typical families: business registration and tax numbers; sector licences (food, health, finance, energy, telecoms, transport, alcohol); import and export licences for controlled goods (dual-use, chemicals, radio equipment); site permits (planning, building, fire, environmental discharge, waste); employment and work permits.
- For each: issuing body, lead time, fees, conditions, renewal date, and whether it transfers on a sale of the business (many do not). Lead time belongs in the launch plan: permits are a common critical path.

### 13. Product standards and certification as a market gate
- Many products cannot be sold without a conformity mark or approval: CE or UKCA marking (Europe and UK), FCC (radio equipment, US), UL or other safety listings that retailers and insurers demand, national marks such as BIS (India) or CCC (China), and sector approvals (medical devices, food contact, electrical safety, solar and battery standards such as IEC).
- Treat the mark as a gate: no mark, no sale in that market or channel. Record which standard, which test lab or notified body, the cost, the lead time and whether a change to the product or supplier voids it.
- Certification cost and time belong in landed cost (step 9, compliance line) and in the launch plan. A cheaper supplier whose product is not certified for the market is not cheaper.

### 14. Data protection, in outline
- If the business collects personal data: which laws apply (by where the people are, not where the business is), the lawful basis for each use, a record of processing, retention limits, security, breach notification deadlines, contracts with processors, and rules for sending data across borders.
- Examples to check: the EU and UK GDPR, US state privacy laws and sector rules (health, children, finance), and national laws elsewhere. Get counsel for anything beyond the outline.

### 15. ESG reporting and green claims
- **Claims:** every environmental or social claim ("carbon neutral", "eco-friendly", "recyclable", "ethically sourced") needs evidence that matches its exact wording, scope (product or company, full life cycle or part) and date. Regulators have tightened rules on generic and offset-based claims (for example the EU's rules on green claims and greenwashing, the UK's Green Claims Code, the US FTC Green Guides); check the current text in each market.
- Prefer specific, measured claims ("40% recycled content, third-party verified") to broad ones. Disclose when a claim relies on offsets.
- **Reporting:** larger firms and their suppliers face disclosure rules and customer questionnaires (emissions by scope, supply-chain due diligence). Keep the data, method and boundaries written down so a number can be reproduced.
- Each claim and each report becomes a row in the compliance register with its evidence file.

### 16. Carbon credits and environmental-attribute revenue
When a plan earns money from carbon credits, renewable certificates or similar, follow `coo/references/risk-and-compliance/carbon-credits.md`. In short:
- Pin the standard (for example Verra's VCS or Gold Standard), the methodology version and the crediting period.
- Model revenue when credits are **issued and delivered**, after the monitoring, verification and issuance lag; deduct buffer pools, levies and a performance haircut.
- Price on a low, base and high range, each with its source and date.
- Check host-country authorisation and corresponding adjustment (Article 6), and buyer concentration.
- Book pre-sold credits as a delivery liability until delivered; model the shortfall clause.
- Run the carbon-cliff check: what happens to revenue and to the business model when each crediting period ends.

## Method, part C: the risk register

### 17. Link every risk to a probability, an impact and an owner
| Risk | Cause and trigger | Probability (by when) | Impact ($, days, or both) | Expected loss | Owner | Response (avoid, reduce, transfer, accept) | Signpost to watch | Review date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- Sources of rows: the compliance register (each obligation that could be missed), tariff and rule changes on the lanes in part A, supplier and route exposures from `disruption-exposure-mapping`, carbon risks from step 16, and the critical assumptions found in `risk-decisions-under-uncertainty` (step 5b).
- Size probability and impact from base rates and judgment; expected loss = probability × impact. Keep a separate flag for rare, severe events, which an expected-loss ranking hides.
- For the top risks, choose the response with `risk-decisions-under-uncertainty` (act now, buy an option, or wait) and write the trigger and response into `disruption-response-planning`.
- Every row has one named owner and a review date. Recompute when a signpost moves.
- For a project, add a contingent action (the prepared fast response) to each row, and when risks run to hundreds, prioritise the sub-projects by value at stake against implementation risk instead of ranking every risk (`project-management`, step 8).

## Worked pattern
A brand imports a cotton knit shirt into the US. Ex-works $8.00. Two lanes. **Every rate below is illustrative; verify against the current schedule and gazette.**

| Line (per unit) | Lane A: Asian country, sea | Lane B: Central American FTA partner, sea |
|---|---|---|
| Ex-works | 8.00 | 9.50 |
| Freight + insurance to US port | 0.45 | 0.20 |
| Base duty (illustrative 16.5% of $8.00 / $9.50) | 1.32 | 0 if yarn-forward met, else 1.57 |
| Additional tariff (illustrative 20% on Lane A origin) | 1.60 | 0 |
| Fees, brokerage, port | 0.12 | 0.12 |
| Inland to warehouse | 0.15 | 0.15 |
| Carrying cost (value × 20%/yr × weeks ÷ 52) | 0.18 (6 weeks) | 0.07 (2 weeks) |
| **Landed cost** | **11.82** | **10.04** (11.61 if origin fails) |

- Lane B wins only if the yarn-forward rule is met: the yarn must be from the region too. Fabric from Asia cut and sewn in Lane B's country fails it, and duty returns.
- Lane A's figure depends on an additional tariff whose legal basis must be checked on the decision date; record the gazette notice and date.
- Both lanes need a forced-labour file tracing cotton to origin.
- Record: "HTS code X; base rate from HTS edition dated Y, checked Z; additional duty under notice N."

**Carbon and compliance example (illustrative; standard practice).** A firm plans to sell 20,000 subsidised clean cookstoves a year in an African market, imported from Asia, funded partly by carbon credits.
- *Imported cost, no quote yet:* ex-works benchmark $25–32; freight 4–8%; duty 0–20% (code uncertain, take the higher); clearance 2%; inland 4%; FX buffer 5%. Landed $29–45 per stove. The plan had used $26 (ex-works only); at the high end the subsidy gap per stove nearly doubles.
- *Gate:* the stove needs the national standards mark; 4 months of testing put first sales after the planned launch. Register row: owner, lab, renewal date.
- *Carbon:* design estimate 2.0 t per stove per year → 40,000 t. After a 25% performance haircut, 30,000 t; at $6 (low) to $12 (high), $180–360k a year, first issuance 18 months after deployment. A buyer prepays $150k for 25,000 t: a delivery liability until issued; the shortfall clause makes the firm buy replacements at market if it misses.
- *Cliff:* the crediting period ends in year 7; renewal requires the newer methodology, which may cut credits per stove by a third. Carbon pays 40% of gross margin, so the stove price or subsidy model must change before year 7.

## Common mistakes
- Trusting secondary commentary (news, blogs, broker notes, an AI answer) over the legal text.
- Using rates, thresholds or exemptions from before the decision date, or ignoring effective dates and transition rules.
- Classifying by product name; never checking rulings.
- Assuming "shipped from" equals origin, or claiming a preference without proof of origin.
- Computing duty on the wrong customs value, or forgetting additional and trade-remedy layers.
- Quoting DDP or CIF at fixed prices with no cost-adjustment clause.
- Treating incoterms as title transfer.
- No forced-labour file until goods are detained.
- Comparing lanes on ex-works price alone; leaving out lead-time carrying cost.
- Treating an imported cost as a domestic one, or leaving the trade method closed because nobody asked a trade question.
- Using ex-works price as a placeholder for landed cost when no quote exists; a point estimate where a range is needed.
- Launching before the certification mark or permit is in hand; assuming a mark survives a supplier or design change.
- A compliance register with no owners, no evidence or no review dates.
- Green claims broader than the evidence, or offset-based claims not disclosed as such.
- Booking carbon revenue when tonnes are reduced instead of when credits are issued; ignoring buffers and haircuts.
- Counting prepaid carbon as income; counting the same tonnes as forward-sold and in base revenue.
- No carbon-cliff date in the plan; assuming crediting renews on the same terms.
- Ranking risks by expected loss alone, so rare severe events fall off the register.
