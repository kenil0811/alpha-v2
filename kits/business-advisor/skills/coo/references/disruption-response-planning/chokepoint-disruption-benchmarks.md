# Shipping chokepoint disruption: benchmarks and how to use them

From published reporting on a closure of a major Gulf shipping strait (as of October 2026). Use these as
order-of-magnitude anchors when sizing a chokepoint scenario; replace them with current sourced figures
before presenting.

## What moves, and by roughly how much
| Item | Order of magnitude in that episode |
|---|---|
| War-risk insurance premium | From about 0.25% to about 6-10% of hull value per transit |
| Spot freight rates on affected lanes | Up roughly 3.5x to nearly 6x (+256% to +479% across sources) |
| Overland bypass by truck (Red Sea port to Gulf port) | About 4.5-5x the cost of the sea leg it replaces |
| Bypass pipeline capacity | About 4.7 million barrels a day, far below normal strait flow |
| Government responses | Caps on any single supplier's share (e.g. 30%), and strategic reserves sized at 4-6 months |

## How to use them
- Build three scenarios (short, base, long closure) and apply cost multipliers per lane, not one average.
- Price the bypass options (other ports plus trucking, pipeline, air for small high-value parts) against
  waiting; bypass capacity is limited and fills fast, so assume rising prices and allocation.
- Insurance can make a route unusable even before it is closed; check cover, not only the physical route.
- Expect policy: single-supplier caps and reserve mandates change the long-run answer toward dual sourcing
  and more stock; include them in the after-crisis plan (section 9).

## When sources conflict
Reports in a live crisis disagree, often widely (the freight range above is one example). Cite each figure
with its source and its as-of date, show the range rather than picking a favourite, say which number the
plan uses and why, and rerun the plan at both ends of the range.
