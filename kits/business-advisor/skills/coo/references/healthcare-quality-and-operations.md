# Healthcare quality and operations

> **Area:** `healthcare-quality-and-operations` · owned by the COO skill. **Use when:** Improve how a healthcare provider delivers care - solve the right problem with cause maps, run Lean/PDSA improvement, set quality aims and measures that don't distort care, build a continuous-improvement culture, improve patient experience, and manage clinical staff and data. Use when a practice or hospital has safety or quality problems, long waits, falling patient satisfaction, staff burnout, or needs to lead a change.

**Outcome:** a specific improvement aim, a root-cause picture, a small set of weekly measures that show
progress before outcomes move, and a plan people will actually adopt.

**When to use**
- A safety event, a poor quality rating, high mortality or readmission in one area.
- Bottlenecks: emergency department crowding, long waits for appointments, slow discharges.
- Patient satisfaction, retention or NPS falling.
- Launching a quality campaign or continuous-improvement program.
- Staffing, retention or motivation issues with physicians, nurses or other professions.
For general (non-healthcare) process tools, see `quality-control-and-lean-operations` and `process-capacity-and-cycle-time`.

## Method

### 1. Solve the right problem: build a cause map
- Start from the effect on a goal (for example, "sepsis deaths above expected"). Ask why, write the causes, repeat.
- Mark **AND** links (several causes all required) and **OR** links (any one possible; useful when evidence is missing).
- Keep going until you reach causes you can act on. Five Whys is the quick version.
- Check whether a "supply chain" or "IT" problem is really a clinical or process problem, and vice versa.

### 2. Improve the process
Goals: lower cost, better safety, efficiency, smoother workflow. Frameworks: Lean, Six Sigma, PDSA (plan-do-study-act), root-cause analysis. None is perfect; the core is the same: find the root cause, define the outcome, test a change with data, iterate.
Lean steps: define value from the patient's view; map the value stream; create flow; respond to actual demand; pursue consistency; remove waste.
Lean tools: 5S (sort, set in order, shine, standardise, sustain); value stream mapping; kaizen; kanban (visual workflow signals); standard work; poka-yoke (design that prevents errors); 5 Whys; gemba (go see where work happens); A3 problem sheets; rapid improvement events; visual management.

### 3. Write an aim and measures
- **Aim:** specific number and date ("some is not a number, soon is not a time"). Bold is fine if credible.
- **Public message:** one sentence that makes people want to join.
- **Hard counts:** 3-5 process or participation measures counted weekly (units enrolled, bundle compliance, audits done), because outcomes like mortality take months to show.
- **Graduated involvement:** define full participation and an easy on-ramp for those not ready.
- **Nodes and mentors:** respected local champions in each unit who coach peers.
- **First visible win in 30 days**, publicised.

### 4. Measure quality without distorting it
Quality is a balance of clinical outcomes, processes, experience, access and equity; satisfaction and
safety can conflict (patients may rate more opioids or antibiotics highly).
- Metrics compress reality: risk adjustment is imperfect, attribution is unclear, survival is not quality.
- Compare observed to expected (O/E), with expected based on age, background conditions and acute problem; accurate documentation and coding drive the expected value.
- Before comparing two clinicians or units: case volume, case mix and severity, procedure mix, and the care around the procedure.
- Metrics get gamed: optimise what is counted, avoid risky patients, do "just enough" to cross thresholds. Public reporting amplifies this.
- Different raters disagree (government stars, independent safety grades, rankings, accreditation). Know which audience each serves: attracting patients, meeting regulators, earning payor money, or actually improving care. Only the last justifies most internal effort.
- Culture decides whether improvement happens: blame-free reporting of events, leaders who respond.

### 5. Build a continuous-improvement culture (lessons from leading health systems)
1. Build a whole management system, not a separate improvement team.
2. Senior leaders commit to changing their own behaviour.
3. Go slower to go faster: a multi-year plan, not months.
4. A common language about patients, quality and safety.
5. Tailored training and ongoing coaching.
6. A few priorities, held steady.
7. Support middle managers; they make it stick.
8. Align frontline work to the organisation's "true north".
9. Involve patients.
10. Fix management and culture before buying digital tools.
To make it outlast its champion: put improvement
experience in the criteria for choosing the next chief executive and new board members, and take the board
to the front line; build the behaviours into every manager's development plan (going to where work is done,
asking open questions, removing barriers); rotate who leads the daily huddle, including nurses and front-desk
staff, so it does not stop when one doctor is busy and so juniors speak up; win on a problem in the team's own
unit rather than citing famous systems; and run it as a daily operating system: shift huddles flag problems,
and results go up to managers each day in visual form.
Two more: show early impact to staff and patients, and expect no quick fixes. The full list and a 0-3 maturity self-check by dimension: `coo/references/healthcare-quality-and-operations/continuous-improvement-maturity.md`.

### 6. Lead the change
People feel losses more than equal gains (loss aversion), so they over-weight what change takes away.
- Acknowledge the past and why earlier choices made sense.
- Shift from "what is" to "what if"; describe the end point clearly so the why is clear.
- Agree on milestone metrics to show progress and signal when to pivot.
- Listen and adapt; give grace for frustration; aim for motivation people internalise.

### 7. Improve patient experience
- Separate the symptom metrics (access, wait, responsiveness, NPS) from what patients say they lost (feeling known, continuity).
- Choose one primary change and state its tradeoff in income, time, expectations or care model. Options include adding staff to absorb coordination, shrinking panels, longer visits, raising price and repositioning, or better expectation-setting.
- Five principles: treat every patient as a consumer
  too, whose choices (whether to come back, take the medicine, switch doctor) affect outcomes and pay under
  value-based contracts; technology is necessary but not enough (patients judge access, the technical and
  human sides of care, and billing); cover the whole journey, not just finding a doctor (visiting family,
  home care, remote monitoring support); use data (records, social needs, patient-reported outcomes) to
  tailor contact and suggest times and visit types; and frame it as a "journey to health", not a "digital
  front door" that waits for the patient to arrive.
- Social needs: screen patients for food, housing, transport and money problems at routine visits and refer
  them to partner services; an unmet need can undo a care plan (a patient with no stable home cannot keep
  insulin cold). The investment case is in `healthcare-business-economics`, step 1b.
- What drives patient choice and referrals, and how providers position and market: `coo/references/healthcare-quality-and-operations/patient-acquisition-and-marketing.md`.
- Difficult patients and other ethical conflicts: reason with the four principles in step 7a. Decide continue vs discharge with one clinical or ethical reason, one organisational reason, and an explicit statement of whether patient welfare or organisational performance takes priority and why.

### 7a. Reason through an ethical conflict (four principles of biomedical ethics)
Four principles give rough, shared ground; in real cases they often point different ways, so the work is to
weigh them for this situation and say which wins and why.
| Principle | What it asks | Rules it supports |
|---|---|---|
| **Respect for autonomy** | Let a capable person decide for themselves, and help them understand enough to choose | Tell the truth; keep privacy and confidences; get consent; help with big decisions when asked |
| **Nonmaleficence** | Do not harm, and do not impose unreasonable risk of harm | Due care; negligence is falling below the professional standard, deliberately or carelessly |
| **Beneficence** | Act to benefit the patient, not only avoid harm | Prevent and remove harm, help, rescue; a large benefit can outweigh a small harm |
| **Justice** | Distribute scarce care fairly | Pick and state the rule: equal shares, need, effort, contribution, merit or market exchange; no one gains or loses care for traits they did not choose |
Steps: name the decision and who is affected; list which principle each option serves or breaks; check law,
policy and professional standards; choose, stating the principle that takes priority; record the reasoning and
tell the patient or family. Use an ethics committee for hard or repeated cases (standard practice). Typical conflicts: a patient refusing advised care (autonomy versus beneficence), rationing beds or staff
time (justice), and business pressure such as discharging a costly patient (justice and nonmaleficence versus
the organisation's interest).

### 8. Manage the workforce
For each profession ask: what they do, what attracts them, who they are, and what motivates or demotivates them beyond "good care" (autonomy, scheduling, pay relative to peers, documentation load, team respect). Salary strongly shapes which specialties attract new physicians. Nursing turnover and shortages drive cost and safety; hiring, scheduling and retention are operating levers, not HR side issues.

### 9. Join the data
Clinical data (EHR: notes, results, orders, codes), operational data (staffing, beds, schedules, throughput) and financial data (codes, contracted rates, cash) live in separate systems built for other purposes. Most real questions need all three. Example: ED at 150% capacity needs staffing, beds, inpatient availability, acuity, discharge steps and expected arrivals together.

## Worked pattern
A 180-bed hospital's sepsis mortality O/E ratio is 1.3.
- Cause map: deaths <- late antibiotics AND late recognition; late recognition <- screening done only at triage OR vital signs not re-checked on the floor; late antibiotics <- pharmacy verification delay AND no pre-stocked doses in ED.
- Aim: "Cut sepsis deaths 25% (from 64 to 48 a year) by 31 December." Message: "Every sepsis patient gets antibiotics within one hour."
- Hard counts weekly: % of positive screens with antibiotics under 60 minutes; floor re-screens done per shift; units live on the protocol; champion huddles held.
- On-ramp: units start with the screening tool only; full participation adds the antibiotic bundle and weekly review.
- Nodes: one nurse and one physician champion per unit, mentored by the ED team that already hits 80%.
- First win in 30 days: pre-stocked first doses in the ED, cutting median time-to-antibiotic from 95 to 55 minutes. Post it on every unit.
- Risk: staff "do just enough" to pass the 60-minute line; also review cases at 61-90 minutes and check for over-treatment.

## Common mistakes
- Fixing a symptom (adding coders, buying software) before mapping the cause.
- Vague aims with no number or date; measuring only lagging outcomes.
- Comparing clinicians or hospitals on raw rates without risk adjustment.
- Chasing a rating instead of the care it is meant to measure.
- Launching many priorities at once; leaders exempting themselves from the change.
- Improving a satisfaction score while losing the personal connection patients actually valued.
