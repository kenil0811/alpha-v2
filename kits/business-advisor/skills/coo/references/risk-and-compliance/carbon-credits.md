# Carbon credits and environmental attributes

Depth for `risk-and-compliance` step 16. Everything here is standard practice. Standards, rules and prices change; **check the registry's current rules and a dated price source before using any figure.** All numbers are illustrative.

## What is being sold
- **Carbon credit:** a certificate for one tonne of CO2-equivalent avoided or removed, issued by a registry against a standard and a methodology after independent verification. It is revenue only once issued and delivered.
- **Other environmental attributes:** renewable energy certificates (RECs in the US, Guarantees of Origin in Europe, I-RECs elsewhere), biodiversity and plastic credits. Same logic: a registry, a standard, an issuance event, a buyer.
- **Compliance markets** (EU ETS, California cap-and-trade, national schemes) have allowances set by law and offset rules of their own. **Voluntary markets** are where most small projects sell.

## The players (outline)
- **Standards and registries:** Verra (the Verified Carbon Standard, VCS), Gold Standard, and others such as ACR, Climate Action Reserve, Puro.earth (engineered removals) and Isometric. Each publishes methodologies, rules for project registration, and a public registry of issued and retired credits.
- **Integrity labels:** the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market (ICVCM) assesses methodologies against its Core Carbon Principles (CCP label); buyers increasingly ask for it. CORSIA (aviation) lists which credits airlines may use.
- **Validation and verification bodies (VVBs):** accredited auditors who validate the project design and verify each monitoring period. Their fees and queues are a real cost and a real delay.

## Method

### 1. Pin the methodology and crediting period
- The methodology sets the baseline (what would have happened anyway), additionality tests, leakage deductions and the monitoring plan. Credits per year come from it, not from the engineering estimate.
- The **crediting period** is the window in which credits can be issued. Typical shapes: a short period renewable a limited number of times, or one fixed longer period; land-use projects run longer. Renewal usually means re-validating against the **current** version of the methodology and a fresh baseline, which can cut volumes sharply.
- Methodologies get revised or withdrawn (cookstove and some avoided-deforestation methods have been tightened in recent years). Record the version and check whether a revision is pending.

### 2. Model the issuance lag
Cash follows a chain: project start → validation and registration → monitoring period → verification report → registry review → issuance → delivery → payment.
- First issuance often lands one to three years after the project starts; each later issuance six to eighteen months after its monitoring period ends.
- Model revenue in the year credits are **issued and delivered**, not the year the tonnes are reduced. Put the lag in the cash-flow model and the working-capital need it creates.

### 3. Haircut the volume
Start from the methodology estimate, then deduct:
- **Buffer pool:** land-use (and some other) projects deposit a share of each issuance in a non-permanence buffer to insure against reversal (fire, logging). The share is set by a risk tool; often 10 to 20 percent or more. Never sold.
- **Registry and share-of-proceeds levies:** some standards take a cut in credits or fees per credit (for example Article 6.4 and some Gold Standard projects carry a share for adaptation).
- **Performance haircut:** real projects under-deliver against design (usage drop-off, downtime, monitoring gaps). Plan on 70 to 90 percent of design until a track record exists.

### 4. Price with a dated range
- Prices vary by type, vintage, label, co-benefits and host country. As a rough order of magnitude in 2024–2025 (illustrative; check a current price index or broker quote and date it): older avoidance credits a few dollars a tonne or less; cookstove, biogas and community credits roughly $5–15; nature-based removals (reforestation) roughly $15–40; engineered removals (biochar about $100–200, direct air capture several hundred dollars and up).
- Use a low, base and high price and say which index or quote, and its date, each came from. Older vintages and credits without a label trade at a discount.

### 5. Check buyer and host-country risk (Article 6)
- Under the Paris Agreement's Article 6, a credit used toward another country's target or under CORSIA needs a **letter of authorisation** from the host government and a **corresponding adjustment** (the host removes the tonne from its own tally so it is not counted twice).
- Authorised, adjusted credits usually sell at a premium; unauthorised credits suit only "contribution" claims. Risks: the host delays or never issues the letter, changes its rules, takes a share of credits, or bans export of certain types.
- Buyer risk: concentration in one offtaker, a buyer's changed climate policy, reputational screens. Check counterparty credit as for any receivable.

### 6. Treat pre-sold credits as a liability
- A forward sale or prepaid offtake gives cash now for credits to be delivered later. Until the credits are issued and transferred, the cash is a delivery obligation (deferred revenue or a liability), not income.
- Read the shortfall clause: if issuance falls short, the seller may owe replacement credits bought at market price, a refund with interest, or damages. Model the shortfall case using the haircuts in step 3.
- Do not count the same future tonnes twice (sold forward and also in the base revenue line).

### 7. Run the carbon-cliff check
For each project, list: crediting period end date, renewals left, what renewal requires, and the share of revenue or margin that carbon provides.
- When the period ends without renewal, carbon revenue goes to zero; a renewal under a newer methodology may cut it.
- If carbon pays for a subsidy (free or cheap devices, below-cost service), the cliff hits the core business model, not just a revenue line. Plan the price change or replacement funding before the cliff year.
- Flag any year where carbon is more than about a third of gross margin as a concentration risk (standard practice threshold, a judgment call).

## Checklist for a carbon line in a business plan
Standard and methodology version · registration status · crediting period and renewals · first issuance date · annual issuance after buffer and haircut · price low/base/high with source and date · authorisation and corresponding-adjustment status · offtake terms and shortfall clause · cliff year and plan.
