# Leading change

> **Area:** `leading-change` · owned by the CHRO skill. **Use when:** Plan and run an organisational change (new system, process, policy, restructure or culture shift) so people adopt it, using the change formula, adoption stages and a sequenced set of change levers. Use when a business is introducing something new that staff must start doing differently, or a change is meeting resistance.

## Outcome
A change plan that builds enough dissatisfaction, a clear model and a workable process to outweigh the cost of change, with levers sequenced so credibility is built before disruptive moves.

## When to use
- "We're moving to a new booking system / pricing / shift pattern. How do we get people on board?"
- "Staff are resisting the new process."
- A small firm is professionalising (new reporting, new roles) or after an acquisition.

## Method

### 1. Test whether the change can succeed: D × M × P > C
- **D, dissatisfaction**: do people feel the status quo is not good enough?
- **M, model**: is there a clear, compelling picture of where we are going and why? It should be desirable (meets stakeholders' needs, motivates), feasible (realistic stretch, room for quick wins) and relevant (fits the context).
- **P, process**: is there a credible plan for getting there?
- **C, cost of change**: what people lose (rewards, power, competence, relationships, identity, time and energy).
Because the left side is a product, if any one factor is near zero the change fails, however strong the others. Score each 0 to 10 with the leadership team and work on the weakest.

**Raising D**: show the performance and opportunity gap (comparisons, benchmarks, market trends, staff survey results), the cost of not changing, and involve key people in the analysis so they discover the gap themselves.

### 2. Expect resistance and read it
Signs: anger, active or passive aggression, withdrawal. Causes: fear of loss, uncertainty, protecting an old identity. Treat resistance as information about the cost of change, not just obstruction.

### 3. Move people through four adoption stages
1. **Awareness**: old routines are challenged; they know a change is proposed.
2. **Interest**: curious, open to information.
3. **Trial**: experiment, weigh costs and benefits.
4. **Adoption**: replace old routines; become advocates.
People move at different speeds. Track where key individuals are, and use their social networks: converting a well-connected person brings friends along.

### 4. Choose the pace
| Directive (fast) | Persuasive (steady) |
|---|---|
| Crisis or urgency | Stable situation |
| People dissatisfied, looking for direction | People satisfied with routines |
| Change agent holds key information | Change agent educates and shows a better way |
| Results needed quickly | Slow and steady works |

### 5. Run it in three phases
1. **Mobilise**: make the case; build capacity.
2. **Move**: build momentum.
3. **Sustain**: institutionalise; keep building capacity.
Key process choices across phases: build credibility, build a coalition, a communication plan, pace and involvement, training, metrics and measurement, and organisational capability. A new measure is itself a change: expect resistance, loophole-hunting and people working to the number, and plan for them (see `people-analytics`, step 8). A practitioners' rule of thumb: about two-thirds of people will be indifferent or opposed to a new measurement system, so put your energy into the supportive third rather than trying to convert everyone.

### 6. Sequence the levers: credibility first, disruption later
Levers differ in how much credibility they need and how disruptive they are. Roughly from low to high on both:
| Stage | Levers |
|---|---|
| Early, low credibility needed | consultant or outside expert support; leaders visibly practising the change ("walk the talk"); private interviews to learn who influences whom and how they feel; progress reports |
| Building | organisation-wide email; success stories; internal training; pilot project; coalition of supporters; town hall meeting with two-way dialogue; recognising an early adopter; privately confronting an irrational resister |
| Late, high credibility needed | announce goals and deadlines; clarify organisational values; revise the reward system; restructure |
Match the lever to the stage the targets are in: communication and stories work for people at awareness or interest; training, pilots and coalitions move people into trial and adoption; rewards and structure lock it in. Levers can be enabling (credibility, communication, training) or substantive (technical, political, cultural). Each takes time to work and can't be repeated immediately, so space them out.

### 7. Map the politics of the change
Every change shifts power, status and how people are measured, so it meets political resistance as well as fear (see `power-and-influence`).
- **Before announcing**: list whose cooperation you need, who could delay it, and who loses power, pay or familiar work; win the respected informal leaders privately first.
- **Consult on the process, hold the outcome**: let affected peers design how the change happens; imposing a detailed plan invites bickering over the details.
- **Pre-wire bad news**: units whose past judgment the change criticises should see the findings privately and help shape the fix before any group meeting.
- **Don't count on a sponsor's private protection**: get the sponsor to commit in front of others, and keep them briefed so they are never surprised.
- **Pace against relationships**: a champion who moves faster than the relationships at the top can win every argument and still be removed. Budget time for the politics.
- **Change shifts power**: a new information system, a central function or a new profession in senior roles moves influence between units; name who gains and loses and plan for the losers.
Case lessons (a change champion removed just before launch; analysts who surprised line managers): `chro/references/power-and-influence/case-lessons.md`, sections 1 and 2.

### 8. Lead the team through sudden change (standard practice)
Say what you know and don't know, set the next check-in date, keep routines that still work, give people one thing they can control, and watch workload and stress.

### 9. Go deeper
Culture diagnosis (competing values assessment, now vs future) and the levers that shape culture (selection, socialisation, rewards): `chro/references/leading-change/culture-diagnosis-and-change.md`.

## Worked pattern
A 30-person plumbing firm moves technicians from paper job sheets to a mobile app.
- D × M × P vs C: D = 4 (techs like paper), M = 7, P = 5, C high for older techs (competence, identity). Weakest is D.
- Raise D: show the 9-day average invoice delay and lost parts costs; let two senior techs review the numbers.
- Pace: persuasive (no crisis).
- Mobilise: owner uses the app for her own call-outs (walk the talk); private interviews find the most-respected tech is sceptical but influential.
- Move: a 3-tech pilot including him; internal training; a success story at the Monday meeting (an invoice paid same day); recognise him as he adopts.
- Sustain: from month 4, paper sheets no longer accepted; monthly bonus counts app-closed jobs.

## Common mistakes
- Leading with restructures or reward changes before building credibility.
- Announcing a vision without first making people feel the problem.
- Treating everyone as if they were at the same adoption stage.
- Reading all resistance as bad faith; some of it reveals real costs.
- Repeating the same lever (more emails) when it has stopped working.
- Leaders who don't use the new way themselves.
- Winning the technical argument and ignoring who loses power, so the change stalls in implementation.
