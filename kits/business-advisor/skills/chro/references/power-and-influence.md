# Power and influence

> **Area:** `power-and-influence` · owned by the CHRO skill. **Use when:** Get things done inside an organisation or across partners without relying on formal authority, by diagnosing the politics of a situation, mapping who depends on whom, auditing your power, and building an influence plan with allies, a sequence and checkpoints. Use when an owner, manager or employee needs buy-in from people they don't control (peers, a boss, a board, a bigger partner, a landlord, a council), faces internal politics or a rival, is managing a boss, is new in a role, or wants to build a reputation and network that carry power, and do it without crossing ethical lines.

## Outcome
A written plan for moving a political situation forward: the goal, who matters and what they want, what power each side holds, which influence tactics fit, the order of moves, how you will know it is working, and where the ethical lines are.

## When to use
- "I need my co-founder / the board / another department to agree."
- "Someone is blocking this and I don't have authority over them."
- "My boss and I don't see eye to eye" or "I've just been promoted and the old hands resist me."
- A small firm dealing with a much bigger partner, landlord or regulator.
- Internal politics: credit-claiming, rival factions, a reorganisation, a rumour campaign.
- A successful person whose position suddenly feels fragile.
- Someone uncomfortable with politics who needs to see where avoiding it costs them.

## Method

### 0. Accept that power is part of the job
Good ideas do not implement themselves. Organisations stall on decisions that are technically easy because nobody mobilises enough support, and the side that cultivates allies and frames the issue well often beats the side with better data. There are three ways to get things done: hierarchy (works only while the person at the top is right and obeyed), shared vision or strong culture (works until the culture itself must change), and power and influence (what remains when the first two fail). Many people are uneasy about power; the position here is that it is to be used well, within the ethical limits in step 7, not avoided.

Implementation matters more than the decision: when you choose you rarely know if the choice is right, and what happens after (adjusting, learning, mobilising support) decides the result.

### 1. Set the goal
One paragraph: what you want to happen, by when, what happens if nothing changes, and what you would settle for. Check that each meeting and conversation serves that goal.

### 2. Judge how political the situation is
Power gets used where four conditions meet:
| Condition | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Interdependence | you need others' resources, approval or work; power matters most when interdependence is moderate |
| Different points of view | specialists see the world through their own data and incentives (sales vs cost vs engineering); disagreement about *how* matters as much as about goals |
| Scarcity | budgets, headcount, space or promotion slots are being cut or reallocated |
| Importance | the issue is big, or symbolically big (office location, titles, reporting lines) |
High on all four: expect a contest and plan for it. Organisations often dodge important issues because they are politically painful; a long-delayed decision is a sign of this.

### 3. Map the players and their dependence on each other
Ask (Pfeffer's interdependence questions, in our words):
1. Whose cooperation do I need to decide and to implement?
2. Whose opposition could delay or derail it?
3. Who is affected in their power or status, in how they are judged or paid, or in how they do their job?
4. Who are the friends and allies of each person above?
Overestimate rather than underestimate: the person you forgot is the one who blindsides you.

For each player record: interests, likely stance (supporter, neutral, blocker), power bases, their alternative if they don't go along (BATNA), who they listen to, and what they would lose. Classify allies: interest-based (want the same thing), position-based (agree on this issue only), and indifferent parties whose support can be traded.

**Read real power, not the org chart.** Use several indicators, since any one misleads: who wins contested decisions; representation in senior jobs and key committees; budget and headcount growth; pay relative to comparable roles elsewhere; office location and other symbols; reputation (ask several people who gets things done); who people go to for advice. Distinguish power from foresight: backing what was going to happen anyway is not power. Detail and worksheets: `chro/references/power-and-influence/diagnosing-power-and-networks.md`.

### 4. Audit your power and theirs
Power is the potential to change what happens against resistance; politics is the use of it. It comes from the person and from the position, and you need both: a skilled person in an isolated job, or a central job held by someone without the skills, each realise little of it.

| Source | Kind | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Expertise | personal | what do they depend on me knowing? It must be visible and current |
| Attractiveness | personal | do people like, trust and identify with me? |
| Effort | personal | do I visibly do more than expected, and learn the details others skip? |
| Legitimacy | personal | do I act in line with the organisation's values, so others accept my influence? |
| Centrality | position | am I in the flow of information and work? |
| Criticality | position | does work stop if I am absent? is my task unique? |
| Flexibility | position | how much discretion does my role have? new roles have more |
| Visibility | position | do senior people see my work, face to face? |
| Relevance | position | does my work serve the organisation's most pressing competitive issue right now? |
| Formal levers | position | role authority, rewards, sanctions (sanctions breed resistance; use rarely) |
| Alternatives | situational | what can I do if they say no? |
| Network | situational | whom can I bring in? |
Status and, unfairly, appearance and demographics also shape how power is perceived. Power is situational, partly perceptual, and spent by using it. To build more: take roles close to the core problem, become the person who solves what others can't, give information and introductions before you need them, make your work visible in person, and mentor newcomers (it creates goodwill and an informed network).

**Reputation (personal brand)** turns expertise and liking into something others repeat when you are not in the room: (1) the impact you want and three to five values you act on; (2) the words others use for you against the words you want, and the signals that would close the gap; (3) two or three proof points with numbers; (4) what changes when you are involved; (5) a check that your visible choices fit the story.

### 5. Build the network that carries power
Networks give three things: private information, access to skills you lack, and power through brokerage (linking groups that don't otherwise talk). Two natural habits weaken them: choosing people like you (self-similarity) and people near you (proximity), which together build an echo chamber. The fix is shared activities with diverse people where something is at stake (cross-functional projects, boards, associations, sport or volunteer teams); trust forms by watching each other under pressure. Find and thank the brokers who introduced you to your most useful contacts; they are often not senior. Keep a routine (for example a weekly lunch with someone outside your area) and meet key people before a project starts, not at the kickoff. Worksheet in the depth file.

### 6. Choose influence tactics
**Lateral leadership** (influence over peers): network, consult before deciding (ask their views and let them shape the *process*, while you hold firm on the *outcome*), build a coalition, then persuade and negotiate for mutual benefit, treating the other as a peer rather than a target.

**Coalitions**: several respected voices together count far more than one. Ask whose blessing, whose resources and whose buy-in you need; recruit in order, starting with those easiest to win and best connected; lock support in early. Pre-wire any meeting where a powerful group will hear unwelcome findings (see the case lessons).

**Six principles of influence** (Cialdini): reciprocity (give first; a concession invites one back), social proof (show that respected peers already do it), commitment and consistency (start with a small yes, get it in writing, link to what they already said), liking (genuine similarity, sincere praise, cooperation), authority (show real expertise early), scarcity (be honest about what is lost by waiting). Detail, defences and the ethics test: `chro/references/power-and-influence/influence-principles-and-ethics.md`.

**Tailor the appeal**: study each person; know what they want in general (recognition, security, advancement) and the feeling driving them on *this* issue; frame your ask as what they already wanted. Believe what you argue: conviction persuades more than an elegant chain of logic. Prepare the case, answer objections in advance, and close with what has to happen next.

**Framing and timing**: tie the proposal to a cause people can't easily oppose (customers, safety, the community); push publicly when the need is most felt; work that has visibly started is hard to reverse, but see step 7 on where that becomes abuse.

**From team and change practice**: raise dissatisfaction before presenting the fix (see `leading-change`); build credibility with early wins before disruptive asks; recognise early adopters publicly; talk privately with a resister before any public confrontation; build trust through competence, benevolence and integrity.

**Manage up**: your boss is one of your key dependencies. Understand their goals, pressures, strengths, blind spots and preferred style; know your own style and how you react to authority; agree expectations; keep them informed (no surprises); be dependable and honest; use their time selectively. Detail: `chro/references/power-and-influence/managing-up.md`.

**Who gets heard**: style signals (directness, saying "I" or "we", how you ask questions, apologise or take credit) shape who is judged confident and who gets credit. See `team-conflict-trust-and-feedback`, step 9.

### 7. Hold the ethical line
- Use an influence principle only where it is genuinely present (real expertise, real scarcity, real consensus). Faking it is manipulation, and it costs trust, customers and staff when found out.
- Map a hard decision before acting: for each option, list the good and bad short-term consequences, the longer-term outcomes, and the values each serves or sacrifices. People who go along with wrong acts usually looked only at the upside of complying and the downside of refusing, believed they had no other option, or had fuzzy values. Look for a third option (refuse, escalate, renegotiate) and how to keep the relationship.
- Power shields people from consequences, which tempts them to stretch rules. Watch for it in yourself and in high performers whom others protect.
- A popular cause does not erase the means: methods that bend rules build enemies who wait.

### 8. Sequence, monitor, adjust
- Sequence: who first, through which channel, with what message, by when.
- Signs it is working: supporters act unprompted; blockers ask questions instead of refusing; your item gets onto agendas.
- Signs to adjust: allies go quiet, new objections, a rival coalition, your sponsor turns cool. Re-map and change tactic.
- Protect your base while winning: keep sponsors briefed, don't humiliate opponents in public, share credit, and keep relationships with people who lose.
- Fallback: your BATNA, and whether to use authority, escalate, or let it go.

## Worked pattern
An operations manager at a 60-person distributor wants to merge three regional warehouses into one. She has no authority over the regional heads.
- Political temperature: high on all four conditions (shared trucks and stock, regional vs central views, headcount cut, status at stake).
- Map: the MD (margin; neutral), finance director (cost; likely ally), two regional heads (lose status; blockers), a respected senior regional head (neutral; the warehouse staff listen to him), and the MD's assistant (controls the agenda; easily overlooked).
- Her power: expertise (built the cost model), centrality (sees all stock data), little formal authority, low visibility with the MD. She prefers one-to-ones.
- Plan: (1) share the model with finance and invite challenges (consultation); (2) one-to-one with the senior regional head to hear what he would lose, then offer him the combined site (reciprocity); (3) hold the outcome firm but let the heads design the move plan (process ownership); (4) brief the MD privately before any meeting (managing up, no surprises); (5) joint proposal from finance, her and him (coalition, social proof); (6) a 3-month pilot on one product line (small commitment first).
- Checkpoints: if the senior head declines by week 3, propose a two-site option. Ethics check: the savings she shows include the redundancy cost.

## Common mistakes
- Believing the better analysis wins by itself.
- Relying on a title or threat when commitment is needed.
- Going public before allies are secured, or surprising powerful people in a meeting.
- Taking second-hand views of an opponent as fact instead of talking to them.
- Assuming a sponsor's private support will hold in public; their own interests come first.
- Ignoring the informal network and the gatekeepers.
- Winning a confrontation in a way that humiliates the other side.
- Treating past results as protection: performance buys power, but arrogance and burned relationships spend it.
- Faking scarcity, authority or consensus.
- Using your BATNA when signalling it would have done.
