# Team launch and charter

> **Area:** `team-launch-and-charter` · owned by the CHRO skill. **Use when:** Design a new team and launch it with a written charter (purpose, goals, roles, rules of engagement, task plan, feedback loop) so it performs sooner and longer. Use when a business forms a project team, task force, new department or cross-functional or multicultural group, or relaunches one that is drifting.

## Outcome
A team that knows why it exists, who does what, how it will work together and how it will know it is on track, written down in a five-slide or two-page charter before real work starts.

## When to use
- "We're putting a team together for X. How do we set it up?"
- A project team, task force, new hire cohort or merged group is starting.
- A team is diverse in background, function, nationality or personality, and you want to head off splits.
- An existing team is stuck and needs a relaunch (re-run this skill as if new).

## Method

### 1. Judge success on three dimensions
A team is effective only if all three hold:
1. **Delivers**: output meets or beats what the client or stakeholders expect (quality, quantity, timing).
2. **Grows**: members and the team as a unit build capability.
3. **Feels good to work in**: members would choose to work together again.
Write one measure for each before launch.

### 2. Design the team (before anyone meets): why, who, what, when, where
| Question | Check |
|---|---|
| Why | Why does this team exist? Principal objective? Who are the stakeholders? |
| Who | Which skills and perspectives are needed? Who has them for the taskwork *and* the teamwork? Do they actually have the time? |
| What | Which role does each member play? What commitments come with each role? What does the leader do? |
| When | Final deadline, critical milestones, initial timeline. |
| Where | In person, virtual, or both? Where will relationships get built? |

Design roles so each one has skill variety, a whole piece of work, visible significance, some autonomy and direct feedback (the same five levers as job enrichment; see `leading-and-motivating-people`).

### 3. Map the inputs before chartering
Run a short "team inputs" session where each member shares: strengths, areas of expertise, values and beliefs, and where they are similar to or different from the others. Output: a one-page map of where the team agrees and where friction is likely.

Optional add-ons:
- **Personality grid**: each member plots themselves low, medium or high on the Big Five (extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, openness, need for stability). Look for gaps (no one high on conscientiousness means deadlines slip) and clusters (all low agreeableness means fights). Each person completes: "I am [level] in [trait], so if you see me [behaviour], it's because [reason], not because [misread]. Help me by [request]." Treat scores as conversation starters, not labels: tests capture one moment, reflect the ideal self, and shift with context.
- **Culture comparison**: compare members on power distance, individualism vs collectivism, short vs long-term orientation, tight vs loose norms, direct (low-context) vs indirect (high-context) communication, and one-at-a-time vs overlapping talk. Country scores are averages; ask the person.

### 4. Write the charter: five sections
1. **Shared goals and roadmap**: the collective goals; what each member personally wants (task goals, learning goals, the experience they want); how you will know you are on track; who starts planning on each new project; how you resolve "excellent vs good enough" disagreements.
2. **Human capital**: where expertise is deep, where it is missing, members' outside commitments, and how people who want to learn a skill get to do so without sinking quality.
3. **Rules of engagement**: channels (chat, email, docs); where files live; standing meeting time; a team **service level agreement** (how fast to reply, how fast issues get resolved, blackout times such as weekends); how work is split and who signs off on quality; coordination roles (scheduler, editor, timekeeper).
4. **Initial task strategy**: milestones, when to get stakeholder feedback, project plan and pacing, how you check you will hit the deadline.
5. **Feedback and learning**: when (midpoint and end of every project), the format (see start/stop/continue in `team-conflict-trust-and-feedback`), how direct people should be, and who owns making the team better next time.

Keep it to about five slides and readable by someone who was not in the room. Treat it as a living document: revise at the midpoint.

### 5. Guard against faultlines and low belonging
- **Faultline test**: a team splits most easily when several attributes line up (for example all the senior people are one gender, one function and one age band, and the juniors another). A team where every attribute is mixed is *more* diverse but *less* likely to split. Map attributes by member and look for alignment.
- **Sequence**: early on, lead with the task (clear goals and roles); build relationships deliberately once the work is moving. Put relationship time on the calendar; don't assume it happens.
- **Surface vs deep diversity**: visible differences (age, race, gender) slow integration early; differences in values, personality and work style matter most later. Plan for both.
- **Belonging**: acknowledge and thank people, respect their outside commitments, give practical help (share inside information, make introductions, endorse their ideas in meetings), let quieter or lower-status members speak first, and check in personally before and after meetings. Ask each leader to name one person likely low in belonging and commit to three actions this week.

### 6. Multicultural and multilingual teams
- Fluent speakers: slow down, avoid idioms, don't dominate, invite the less fluent in, check understanding.
- Less fluent speakers: don't withdraw, don't switch to a side language in front of others, ask for repeats.
- Leader: balance speaking and listening, draw out everyone, be ready to restate and interpret.
- When culture-driven friction appears (direct vs indirect speech, accents, attitudes to hierarchy, how fast decisions get made), the options in order of preference: the team **adapts** itself by naming the difference and agreeing a workaround; a **structural** fix (regroup subteams, change who talks to whom); the **manager steps in** (sets a norm; use sparingly, as teams then lean on the manager for everything); or as a last resort someone **exits**.

### 7. Generate ideas without groupthink (when the first task is creative)
Brainwriting in three phases: about 25% of the time writing ideas alone and swapping sheets to build on others; about 50% combining and enriching ideas together with a facilitator making sure all voices are heard; about 25% narrowing to one idea against the problem's constraints and key stakeholders' views.

### 8. Plan the three intervention moments
Teams are most open to change at the **start** (motivational: purpose, energy), the **midpoint** (consultative: are we working the right way? Half the time is gone and often more than half the work remains) and the **end** (educational: what do we take into the next project). Book all three on day one.

## Worked pattern
A 14-person landscaping firm forms a 4-person team to launch a commercial maintenance line in 10 weeks.
- Design: why = £150k signed contracts by week 10; who = ops lead, estimator, salesperson, crew chief; the crew chief has only 4 hours a week, so the charter notes it.
- Inputs map: estimator and salesperson both high extraversion and low conscientiousness, so the ops lead owns the tracker.
- Faultline check: two office staff vs two field staff, with age and tenure lined up the same way, so it is a risk. Pair one office with one field member on each workstream.
- SLA: replies within 4 working hours; no messages Sunday; blockers raised at the Tuesday 8am stand-up.
- Milestones: pricing model week 2, 10 prospect visits by week 5, midpoint review week 5, first contract by week 7.
- Feedback: start/stop/continue at week 5 and week 10; the ops lead owns follow-through.

## Common mistakes
- Skipping launch to "get to work"; teams underinvest in launch and regret it, and a bad launch is hard to undo.
- Assuming everyone shares your goal level (some want excellent, some want done).
- Treating the charter as a one-off document rather than a process that gets revised.
- Mistaking visible diversity for the whole picture, or assuming diversity alone produces performance without integration work.
- Expecting relationships to form on their own.
- Using personality or culture scores as boxes for people.
- The manager solving every cross-cultural spat, which teaches the team not to solve its own.
