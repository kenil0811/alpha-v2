# Group decision-making

> **Area:** `group-decision-making` · owned by the CHRO skill. **Use when:** Help a leadership team or working group make better decisions by spotting individual biases and group failure modes (groupthink, common-information bias, cascades, sunk cost) and applying structural fixes. Use before an important group decision, when a team keeps agreeing too fast, or when reviewing why a decision went wrong.

## Outcome
Decisions that use all the information in the room, survive challenge, and can be stopped when they stop making sense.

## When to use
- A partners' meeting, board or management team must decide something costly or hard to reverse.
- "We all agreed and it turned out badly. Why?"
- One person always dominates; quieter people hold back.
- A project is far over budget and someone says "we've come too far to stop."

## Method

### 1. Pick the right decision process
- **Rational model**: define the problem, list criteria, weight them, generate options, score them, pick the best. Use for big, reversible-at-cost choices.
- **Bounded reality**: people simplify, use a short list of criteria, compare options one at a time and take the first that is "good enough" (satisficing). Fine for small, frequent decisions. Name which one you are doing.
- State the decision rule up front: leader decides after input, majority, or consensus. Say who owns the final call.

### 2. Check for individual biases
| Bias | Sign | Counter |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmation | Only looking for supporting evidence | Assign someone to argue the other side; check every piece of evidence with equal rigour |
| Anchoring | First number or idea frames all others | Think alone before discussing; try other starting points; ask several sources |
| Sunk cost / escalation | "We've invested too much to stop" | Bring in people who weren't part of the original call; judge only future costs and benefits; don't punish good-faith failures |
| Framing | Same facts, different choice when framed as gain vs loss | Restate the choice both ways |
| Overconfidence | Narrow forecasts, tight plans | Give ranges; compare to past projects (reference class) |
| Availability | Vivid recent example dominates | Ask for base rates |
| Story from noise | A confident judgement of a person after one conversation or one rating period | Score against a written scale before discussing; weigh records and work samples; use several raters and periods (see `people-analytics`) |
| Halo / rater effect | One rater's scores on different qualities all move together; a rater's lenient or tough pattern stays the same whoever they rate | Rate one dimension at a time; add a second rater; ask raters about their own reaction ("would I promote this person today?") rather than abstract qualities, since more detailed anchors can make the rater effect worse (see `people-analytics`, step 6) |
| Calibration by consensus | A talent review or calibration meeting argues ratings into a quota or forced curve | Treat the output as negotiated, not measured; settle pay and promotion on counted results plus each leader's own-reaction answers, not on the forced rating |
| Also | hindsight, halo, self-serving, fundamental attribution error, loss aversion, stereotyping, ignoring compound probabilities (A and B and C) | |

### 3. Check for group failure modes
- **Common-information bias**: groups discuss what everyone already knows and neglect what one person alone knows. Unique facts surface late, if at all.
- **Informational cascades**: people follow early speakers because they assume those speakers know something.
- **Reputational cascades**: people stay quiet to avoid disapproval, especially of the boss.
- **Polarisation**: a group ends more extreme than its members started.
- **Groupthink** signs: excessive optimism, warnings explained away, dissenters stereotyped, self-censorship, silence read as agreement, pressure to conform.
- **Burden of proof flipped**: the culture asks "prove it's unsafe" instead of "prove it's safe". (Example: a large engineering organisation's history of treating a recurring anomaly as normal.)

### 4. Apply structural fixes ("make the group smarter")
1. **Silence the leader**: the most senior person speaks last or not at all until others have.
2. **Prime critical thinking**: tell the group the goal is to find what is wrong.
3. **Reward group success**, not being right individually.
4. **Assign roles**: each member is responsible for one perspective or body of facts, so unique information has an owner.
5. **Devil's advocate**, or better, a **red team** that builds the case against.
6. **Delphi method**: people give estimates anonymously, see the spread, revise, repeat.
7. **Collect information before discussion**: everyone writes down what they know and what they think, independently, before the meeting.
8. Ask explicitly: "What does anyone know that hasn't been said?"

### 5. Act as a decision architect
Rather than fixing people's thinking, change the environment so better choices are easier (standard behavioural practice):
1. Understand which systematic error is at work.
2. Confirm behaviour is really the cause (not missing data or incentives).
3. Pinpoint the specific mechanism.
4. Redesign: defaults, checklists, timed pauses before commitment, reframing, planning prompts.
5. Test the change on a small scale and measure.

### 6. Decisions under pressure and trade-offs
- Teams under time pressure and conflicting goals drift from their stated values. Write down the priorities before the crisis and check the decision against them.
- When the given options are all bad, look for a third option rather than accepting the trade-off as framed.
- Set "turnaround" or kill criteria in advance (for example: stop if costs exceed X or the date slips past Y). Commit while calm.

### 7. Go deeper
Debiasing tools for one-off, high-stakes decisions (three estimates, premortem, outside view, objective lists, joint evaluation, vanishing options, trip wires): `chro/references/group-decision-making/debiasing-toolkit.md`.

## Worked pattern
A three-partner accountancy decides whether to buy a £60k practice-management system.
- Decision rule: managing partner decides after a structured meeting.
- Before the meeting each partner and the office manager write their estimate of annual hours saved, privately: 300, 500, 900, 250. The spread is the finding.
- The office manager is given the role of reporting what staff will lose from the current setup (unique information); she reports the old system handles a niche tax form the new one doesn't.
- The managing partner speaks last.
- Red team (one partner) argues the case for staying.
- Kill criteria are set in advance: if migration passes 4 months or £75k, pause and review with someone outside the original project.

## Common mistakes
- The boss opens with their view, then asks for comments.
- Treating silence as consent.
- Discussing only what everyone already knows.
- Throwing more money at a failing project to justify past spend.
- A devil's advocate everyone knows is just playing a role; a genuine red team with a real mandate works better.
- Accepting the problem's framing without asking for other options.
