# Team conflict, trust and feedback

> **Area:** `team-conflict-trust-and-feedback` · owned by the CHRO skill. **Use when:** Keep a working team healthy by diagnosing and resolving conflict, building trust and psychological safety, handling free riders, managing team mood and stress, and running feedback and midpoint reviews. Use when a team is fighting, going quiet, missing deadlines because of one member, burning out, or needs a structured review.

## Outcome
Disagreement that improves decisions instead of damaging relationships, people who speak up, a fair share of the load, and a feedback routine that makes the team better each cycle.

## When to use
- "Two people on my team can't stand each other."
- "One person isn't pulling their weight and the others are angry."
- "Nobody pushes back in meetings."
- "The team is exhausted / morale has dropped."
- "How do I give this person hard feedback?" or "Run a midpoint review."

## Method

### 1. Diagnose the conflict type
| Type | About | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Task conflict | goals, roles, processes, the right answer | Can help in complex or creative decisions, at moderate levels, *if* it stays separate from relationship conflict |
| Relationship conflict | values, personality, how people treat each other | Raises anxiety, lowers trust, motivation and performance |

The two travel together strongly, so the main job is to stop task disagreement from turning personal. Performance peaks at a moderate level of task conflict; none means apathy, too much means paralysis.

Ask: what started it, what made it worse (incentives, workload, cultural differences, unclear roles), who is in it, and whether the root is structural (for example a pay or metric scheme that sets people against each other). Fix structure first when it is the cause.

### 2. Rules for a good fight
- Argue from facts and data, not opinions about people.
- Put several options on the table, not two camps.
- Anchor on a shared goal ("what's best for the business").
- Use humour to release pressure.
- Keep power balanced; no one wins by rank alone.
- Decide without forcing consensus: if talk stalls, the leader decides after hearing everyone (consensus with qualification).
- Keep the process fair so losers can accept the outcome.

### 3. Choose a conflict-handling style deliberately
Two axes: concern for your own outcome (assertiveness) and concern for theirs (cooperation).
| Style | Result now | Effect on trust (competence, benevolence, integrity) |
|---|---|---|
| Collaborating (solve it together) | win-win | raises all three |
| Compromising (each gives some) | partial | raises benevolence and integrity |
| Competing (impose) | win-lose | may show competence, costs benevolence and integrity |
| Accommodating (give in) | lose-win | raises benevolence, can lower perceived competence |
| Avoiding (ignore) | lose-lose | lowers all three |
People default by personality (high extraversion leans assertive, high agreeableness leans cooperative), by culture (collectivists avoid and accommodate more) and by relative status (people accommodate bosses and compete with juniors). Name the team's default and ask whether it is working.

Three rules: accept conflict as normal; respond to the content, not the delivery style; keep task and relationship issues separate.

### 4. Build trust and psychological safety
Trust is the willingness to be vulnerable to someone, based on three judgements:
- **Ability**: can they do it? (cognitive trust)
- **Benevolence**: will they look out for me?
- **Integrity**: do they keep to principles I accept? (benevolence and integrity together form affect-based trust)
Treat trust like a bank account: make deposits before withdrawals, it takes time to build, keep commitments, and reputation earns credit.

**Psychological safety** is a team-level belief that it is safe to admit mistakes, raise odd ideas and disagree. Safe teams learn and adapt faster and decide better. Leader moves (standard practice): admit your own mistakes first, ask "what am I missing?", thank people for bad news, and never punish someone for a well-intended miss.

When information is unevenly held (one person knows something critical), safety decides whether it gets shared. Ask each member directly what they know that others don't.

### 5. Handle free riders fast
Most people start out willing to cooperate. Without accountability, coasting spreads: contributors cut back once they see others coast. Given a way to enforce fairness, people will pay to punish slackers, which distracts them from their own work. So the manager must act.
1. Check the system first: is work fairly distributed, or do policies let some people do less? Fix the rule (a shared closing routine, rotating tasks).
2. Ask the team who sets the floor; the lowest performer sets the real standard.
3. Talk to the person with empathic listening before judging; find out whether it is ability, motivation, outside load or role design.
4. Enrich or redesign the role if it is a motivation problem (see `leading-and-motivating-people`).
5. Set specific, visible commitments and follow up. Escalate if they keep missing them.
6. Spot natural motivators new hires gravitate to, and give them more scope.

### 6. Manage emotions and mood
- Emotions are contagious; a team develops an emotional tone that shapes performance, creativity, cooperation and trust. Map where the team sits on a mood meter (energy high or low × pleasant or unpleasant) and whether the cause is inside or outside the team, ongoing or one-off. Positive is usually good; negative is not always bad (it can sharpen focus on problems).
- Work and home spill into each other, negative more than positive. After-hours messages raise strain; set quiet hours in the charter.
- Watch for burnout: exhaustion, cynicism, falling effectiveness. Responses include workload review, recovery time, and psychological safety to admit strain.
- **Empathic listening**: let the person talk with eye contact and no interrupting; at pauses, restate the essence ("I hear you saying..."); ask clarifying questions; ask "anything else?" until they are done. Only then problem-solve, and only if they want to.

### 7. Give and receive feedback
Giving (four steps):
1. Check your motive: do you want them to improve?
2. Ask for a micro-yes ("Have you got two minutes to talk about the client call?").
3. Give a data point, not a label: "The slides were due at noon and arrived at 4" rather than "you're unreliable."
4. State the impact, then end with a question: "What do you think we should do?"
Receiving: treat it as growth, assume good intent, be curious, ask several people, choose what to act on, and look after yourself before and after. Good leaders ask for feedback rather than wait for it.

### 8. Run a team review (midpoint and end)
- Each person to each teammate: "My strongest impression of you so far is... Something I'm curious about is..."
- Then start / stop / continue for each teammate.
- Team-level: core strengths, what you're proud of, which norms to change, which coming deliverable should shine.
- Individual self-check: own contribution, how to improve, undisclosed personal pressures, stress and coping.
- Revise the charter. Optionally run an anonymous 360 survey the team designs itself.

### 9. Make sure the right people get heard, and handle hostile moves
Ways of speaking learned early (how direct you are, how long you pause, whether you say "I" or "we", whether you ask questions, apologise, or downplay certainty) are read by others as confidence or competence, and they decide who gets credit (Tannen). Typical patterns: one person raises an idea briefly, another restates it at length and is remembered as its author; someone who says "we did" loses credit to someone who says "I did"; asking questions is read by some as not knowing; ritual apologies and softened requests are taken literally; ritual opposition (arguing to test an idea) is taken as attack by people who do not use it.
- **Leader's fixes**: note who first raised an idea and credit them by name; go round the table so people who wait for a pause get a turn; judge proposals on content, not on how confident they sound; tell people privately when their style is costing them credit.
- **Individual's fixes**: state ownership plainly when it matters ("my analysis shows"); restate your idea when someone repeats it; match the room's directness when the stakes are high.
- **A provoking opponent in a meeting** (from a teaching case on a manager facing a hostile peer): stay calm, but don't score a point that humiliates them; the room will remember the humiliation and the battle lines harden. Afterwards, talk to them or their boss privately.
- **Rumours and sabotage**: don't rely on silence. Raise it early with someone who has authority, with facts and dates, and ask your allies to correct the story where it circulates.
- **Embarrassment after a public stumble**: acknowledge it, with humour if you can; it reads as confidence. Then be kind to yourself and move on; replaying it costs more than the moment did.

## Worked pattern
A café chain's three-person marketing team: the designer and copywriter clash; the third member does little.
- Diagnosis: the clash began over who owns campaign timelines (task) but now includes remarks about attitude (relationship). Cause: two people were each told they were "lead".
- Fix structure: one owner per campaign, alternating monthly.
- Facilitated talk: both describe the last missed deadline as data and impact, then build a shared calendar together (collaborating).
- Free rider: empathic one-to-one reveals they are covering a store shift. Formally move 4 hours to marketing, set two named weekly deliverables, review in 3 weeks.
- At the midpoint: start/stop/continue round; quiet hours added after 7pm.

## Common mistakes
- Treating all conflict as bad, or all harmony as success.
- Letting "it's just how they talk" become relationship damage; or the reverse, reacting to tone and missing the point.
- Ignoring a free rider "to keep the peace": it spreads and the best people leave.
- Problem-solving before the person feels heard.
- Feedback that labels character instead of describing behaviour and impact.
- Avoiding conflict with peers while competing with juniors, without noticing the pattern.
- Crediting the person who said an idea loudest rather than first.
