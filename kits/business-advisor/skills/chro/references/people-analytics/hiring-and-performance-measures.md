# Hiring and performance measures

Depth for `chro/references/people-analytics.md`, steps 5 and 6.

## Selection methods, stronger to weaker
| Method | Measures | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Work sample or assessment centre | Person-job fit | Closest to the job; costly to design |
| Standardised tests (ability, job knowledge) | Person-job fit | Check adverse impact |
| Structured interview | Person-job fit, defined values | Same questions, scored answers |
| Relevant biographical data | Person-job fit | Past results in similar work |
| Recruiter impression, unstructured interview, visits, informal contact | Usually "fit" | Weak and easily contaminated |

## Build a structured interview (standard practice)
1. From the job analysis, pick four to six attributes the job truly needs.
2. Write one or two questions per attribute: past behaviour ("Tell me about a time you...") or a job situation ("A client calls with...").
3. For each question, write what a weak, adequate and strong answer contains, on a 1 to 5 scale.
4. Ask every candidate the same questions in the same order; follow-ups only to clarify.
5. Each interviewer scores each answer alone, straight after it, before discussing.
6. Combine scores mechanically (average across interviewers and questions), then add the other evidence (work sample, references) by a pre-set weighting.
7. Discuss only after scores are in; the hiring manager speaks last (see `group-decision-making`).
8. Every year, correlate interview scores with later performance and early turnover; drop questions that predict nothing.

Why the structure: interviewers spin a coherent story from anything, including random answers, and feel they "got to know" the candidate either way. Free-form interviews can make predictions worse than using records alone. Structure makes interviews more reliable and somewhat more predictive.

## Person-organisation fit, done properly
1. Name the values in behaviours ("raises a problem with a client the same day", not "integrity").
2. Ask about those behaviours in the structured interview.
3. Never let an unscored "fit" impression veto a candidate; it is where similarity bias hides.

## Checking an AI screening tool
1. What was it trained on? If on past hiring decisions, it learns past bias.
2. Has it been validated against job performance, ideally in your firm or a similar one?
3. Selection rates by group: compare each group's pass rate with the highest group's. A ratio under about four-fifths is a common red flag for adverse impact (standard practice).
4. Can candidates and managers see what drives a score?
5. Run it alongside the current process for a cycle and compare who each would have hired, before relying on it.
6. Get legal advice; litigation over AI hiring is growing.

When a manager's interview view and the analytics ranking disagree, don't default to either: check what each is based on, whether each has been validated, and what further evidence (a work sample) would settle it.

## Fairer performance evaluation
1. Define high performance for this job in this firm, in behaviours and results.
2. Use goals agreed at the start of the period, plus behaviour ratings with written anchors.
3. Adjust results for inputs: difficulty of accounts, territories, cases or clients. Compare each case with the average for its difficulty band, or with the same person's prior baseline.
4. Use at least two sources: an objective output, a customer or peer view, and more than one human rater.
5. Check for rater effects: does one rater's scores move together across all dimensions? Do two raters agree on the same person?
6. Separate skill from luck: look at several periods, and expect extreme one-period results to drift back toward the average (see `chro/references/people-analytics/validating-a-measure.md`).
7. Explain the method to staff and show the checks; people who feel misjudged leave.

## Can people rate other people? (from rater-reliability research)
Talent reviews, nine-box grids (performance against potential), 1-to-5 ratings, calibration meetings, forced curves, competency ratings with behaviour anchors and always-on 360 apps all assume a person can reliably rate another. The evidence says mostly not:
- **The idiosyncratic rater effect.** In a large study of about 4,400 managers each rated by two bosses, two peers and two direct reports (nearly half a million ratings), the biggest single source of variation, about 54%, was the rater's own rating pattern: lenient or tough, wide or narrow use of the scale. The pattern stayed the same whoever was being rated. The three largest studies of this kind put it at about 60%. The ratee's actual performance explained far less.
- It is separate from bias about gender, race or age, and appears regardless of them.
- **More detailed scales and anchors make it worse**, not better: faced with complex scales, raters fall back on their habitual pattern.
- **Data insufficiency.** Colleagues don't see enough of each other's work to judge abstract qualities such as "strategic thinking" or "business acumen", which have no single agreed definition. Even Olympic judges watching nothing but one jump disagree.
- **Crowds are wise only when well informed.** Averaging guesses works for an ox's weight (everyone knows roughly what oxen weigh), not for qualities people neither observe nor define the same way.
- **Forced range is not real range.** Managers rarely use the bottom two points of a five-point scale, so firms force a curve; the result is bad data.
- Consequence: for most knowledge work, a firm can't reliably say what drives performance (team size, remote work, training), because performance itself isn't reliably measured. Treat confident claims about that with suspicion. Counted output (sales, piece-work) is the exception.

Other evidence agrees in part (the rater effect in step 6 and the multi-trait multi-method check in `chro/references/people-analytics/validating-a-measure.md`), but it treats several independent raters as better than one, where rater-reliability research treats ratings of others as beyond repair. See "The limit of averaging" in that file.

### What to measure instead
People are reliable raters of **their own experience and intentions**, even though they are poor judges of others' qualities (and of their own performance). So turn the question round: ask the leader what they feel and would do, not what the person has.
| Instead of rating... | Ask the team leader (each quarter or end of project) |
|---|---|
| Quality of work, "performance" | Do I always go to this person when I need extraordinary results? |
| Teamwork, collaboration | Do I choose to work with this person as much as I possibly can? |
| Potential | Would I promote this person today if I could? |
| A problem | Does this person have a performance problem I need to address now? |
(Wording paraphrased from published research.)

- The data is humbler (one leader's reaction) but reliable, and strong wording produces natural spread without a forced curve.
- Adjust for each leader's rating fingerprint before comparing across teams: express each answer relative to that leader's own average and spread (one large firm is known to do this). In a small firm, convert each leader's answers to standard scores: (answer minus that leader's average) divided by that leader's standard deviation (standard practice).
- Pair it with measures that can be counted (output, quality, customer outcomes) and with the person's own engagement answers. In one large firm's data, people who said they knew what was expected, used their strengths, were recognised and were growing got higher "go to for extraordinary results" scores from leaders who hadn't seen their engagement answers.
- For a 360 built this way, each colleague rates their own reaction, and you still need to settle who rates and whether they see enough of the work.

### When you receive ratings
Ask where each rating came from, what it was a rating of, and whether the tool allows for the rater effect. Don't let a single rating or a nine-box placement settle pay or promotion on its own.

## Testing an HR change
To learn whether a policy works (a new interview, a bonus scheme, a coaching programme), compare a group that got it with a comparable group that didn't, over the same period, with the outcome and comparison fixed before you start. Prefer natural comparisons (a staggered rollout by site or start date). Randomly withholding a benefit such as a bonus from staff is widely held to be unethical; if you randomise, randomise only the timing of something everyone will receive (standard practice). Limits and sample traps: `chro/references/people-analytics/ethics-and-limits.md`.
