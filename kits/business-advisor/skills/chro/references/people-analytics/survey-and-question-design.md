# Survey and question design

Depth for `chro/references/people-analytics.md`, step 4. How the wording, format, order and sponsor of a question change the answer, and the rules that follow for engagement surveys, pulse checks, exit interviews, customer surveys and any self-report a business relies on.

## The core idea
People answering a survey act as cooperative conversation partners. They assume everything the questionnaire shows them is there for a reason: the answer options, the numbers on the scale, the time period, the questions before, even who is asking. So the questionnaire is not only a measuring device; it is also a source of information people use to work out what you want. Minor changes in wording, format or order can move results by tens of percentage points.

## What a person does to answer
1. **Understand the question**: both the literal words and what the asker seems to want (the pragmatic meaning). They fill gaps from context.
2. **Recall or estimate**: for frequent, ordinary behaviour there is no memory of each instance, so people estimate rather than count.
3. **Form a judgement**: built on the spot from whatever comes to mind most easily, compared with some standard.
4. **Map it to your format**: fit the judgement to your response options.
5. **Edit**: adjust the answer to look good or stay safe (social desirability).

Each step is a place where the instrument can shape the result. The rules below are grouped by step.

## Rules for understanding the question
1. **A list of options changes the question.** Offering an option on a list makes it far more likely to be chosen than when people answer freely: in one study an option picked by about six in ten from a list was volunteered by about one in twenty when the question was open. A list reminds people of things and also tells them what counts, so they leave off anything not on it. Build closed lists from an open pilot, and include an "other, please say" option.
2. **The range of the answer options defines the topic.** Asked how often they were "really irritated", people given low-frequency options (from "less than once a year") assume serious episodes; people given high-frequency options assume minor ones. They are then answering different questions.
3. **The time window does the same.** "In the last week" invites small, frequent events; "in the last year" invites big, rare ones. Never compare answers collected over different windows, or a daily diary with a year-end recall.
4. **The numbers on a rating scale carry meaning.** A scale from -5 to +5 made "not at all successful" read as outright failure; 0 to 10 made it read as no special achievement. The share of people placing themselves in the lower half was 34% on the first and 13% on the second. Use 0 upwards (unipolar) when you measure how much of something there is; use negative-to-positive (bipolar) only when the two ends are true opposites.
5. **Who asks shapes the answer.** People tailor answers to what they think the asker cares about (in one study the letterhead on the form changed how people explained the same event). A survey branded by HR, by the CEO or by an outside firm will draw different answers; keep the sponsor constant across waves and say plainly why the data is collected.
6. **People will answer about things they don't know.** Around three in ten will give an opinion on an issue that doesn't exist, using nearby questions to guess what it means. A confident answer is not evidence the person knows the topic: offer "don't know" and ask about awareness first.
7. Plain, familiar words are necessary but not enough; check what people think the question is for.

## Rules for frequencies and behaviour
8. **Ask for a number, with the unit.** People treat the middle of a frequency scale as "normal" and place themselves relative to it. With low-range options about 16% reported more than two and a half hours of daily television; with high-range options about 38% did. Use an open box with units ("hours per week: ___") instead of ranges.
9. **Never use vague quantifiers** ("often", "sometimes", "rarely"). "Often" means a different count for headaches than for heart attacks, and a different count for each person. They measure frequency against each person's private standard.
10. **The less memorable the behaviour, the bigger the effect.** Frequent, mundane or ill-defined behaviour (helping colleagues, feeling stressed, checking email after hours) is shaped most by the options; rare, important or perfectly regular behaviour hardly at all. Define the behaviour precisely and ask about a short recent window.
11. **Reports about others are shaped more than reports about oneself**, because people know less about others' behaviour. A manager's estimate of a report's overtime is weaker evidence than the report's own.
12. **Unequal memory creates false group differences.** If one group remembers the behaviour less well, the scale moves its answers more, and a gap appears that isn't real.

## Rules for knock-on effects on later answers and on readers
13. **Where people land on the scale changes later answers.** Someone whose answer sits at the top of a low-range scale concludes they do more than most, and that colours later satisfaction questions. Comparative questions ("how satisfied are you with your workload?") and absolute ones ("how much does your workload bother you?") can move in opposite directions from the same scale.
14. **Readers are fooled too.** Experienced doctors judged the same symptom frequency as more serious when it sat at the top of a low-range scale. Managers reading survey dashboards do the same: report the actual number, not only the scale position.

## Rules for attitude questions and question order
15. **Judgements are built on the spot from what comes to mind.** Some information always comes to mind (chronically accessible: a serious illness, a hated commute); some only because an earlier question raised it (temporarily accessible). The second kind is the source of order effects.
16. **Assimilation or contrast.** Information an earlier question raised pushes a later answer **towards** it when people fold it into the thing being judged, and **away** from it when they use it as a yardstick.
   - Broad targets ("the company", "managers here") absorb what was raised: a question about a star leader lifts views of leadership in general.
   - Narrow targets ("your own manager") can't absorb it, so it becomes a yardstick: the same star makes one's own manager look worse.
   - This is why people distrust an institution but trust their own representative, or rate "discrimination against my group" as high but their own experience as mild.
17. **Specific before general inflates the link.** When a specific question (satisfaction with one's marriage) came before a general one (satisfaction with life), the correlation between them rose from about 0.3 to about 0.7; a lead-in saying both questions were coming cut it to about 0.2, because people then left the specific part out of the general answer. So whether "engagement drives overall satisfaction" can depend on question order alone.
   - Ask general questions before specific ones, or state explicitly whether the general answer should include the specific area.
   - Keep the order identical across survey waves; a reorder can look like a trend.
   - When a result matters, split the sample and run two orders to see if it holds.
18. **On paper or online forms, later questions can also affect earlier ones**, because people scroll back.

## Rules for the final, edited answer
19. **Anonymity and confidentiality reduce editing.** Say who will see what, at what group size results are reported, and keep to it.
20. Sensitive questions get more edited answers; ask them later, in neutral words, and never where a manager can see the individual response.

## Make questions that produce usable range (from rater-reliability research)
21. **Bland wording gets bland answers.** "My job fits my abilities" gets nearly everyone agreeing. Wording with an extreme anchor ("I have the chance to use my strengths every day") spreads people across the scale and so tells them apart.
22. **Ask people about their own experience and intentions, not about others' qualities** (see `chro/references/people-analytics/hiring-and-performance-measures.md`).
23. **A scale everyone answers 4 or 5 on is broken**, like end-of-training "this was a good experience" items. Look at the spread before trusting the score.
24. **Beware "driver analysis" inside one survey.** Finding that some items "drive" a summary item asked at the end of the same survey mostly shows that people who were positive early stayed positive; it doesn't show what drives real behaviour such as staying or leaving. Test drivers against an outcome measured separately (later turnover, absence, output).

## Pretest before you launch
- **Cognitive interviews**: five to ten people from the target group answer aloud, saying what they think each question means and how they arrived at their answer; probe anything unclear.
- Code the transcripts for questions that were misread, needed help or got "it depends".
- Pilot the final form on a small group and look at the spread of answers and the "don't know" rate.
(The group sizes are standard practice.)

## Checklist for any survey item
- [ ] One construct, one question; several items per construct for anything important.
- [ ] Frequency asked as a number with a unit, or the options cover the real range evenly.
- [ ] No vague quantifiers.
- [ ] Time window stated, short and recent, and the same across waves.
- [ ] Scale numbers match the construct (0 upwards for amount; minus to plus only for opposites).
- [ ] General before specific; order fixed across waves.
- [ ] "Don't know" offered where knowledge may be missing.
- [ ] Wording strong enough to produce spread.
- [ ] Asks about the person's own experience or intended action.
- [ ] Sponsor, purpose, anonymity and reporting threshold stated.
- [ ] Pretested with cognitive interviews.
- [ ] Linked to a separately measured outcome before anyone claims it predicts anything.

## Worked pattern
A 60-person agency's pulse survey asks: "How often do you work late? Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often" and, next, "Overall, how satisfied are you with your job? 1 to 5". The founder sees "often" at 40% and satisfaction at 3.9.
- "Often" is a vague quantifier: replace with "In the last two weeks, on how many working days did you work more than one hour past your scheduled finish? ___ days".
- The overtime question, placed first, makes long hours easy to recall and drags the job-satisfaction answer towards it. Move the general satisfaction item to the front.
- "Satisfied with your job" bunches at 4: add a sharper item, "I would recommend working here to a close friend in my field", which spreads answers out.
- Before the founder links satisfaction to client renewals, check that the same people are in both datasets (see `chro/references/people-analytics/ethics-and-limits.md`).
