# Linking people measures to business results

Depth for `chro/references/people-analytics.md`, step 9. Drawing on HR scorecard work on principles of good measurement: how to show that a people measure moves a result the business cares about, in units a manager can act on.

## Levels versus relationships
- A **level** says how much of something there is: engagement at 3.5 out of 5, turnover at 15%.
- A **relationship** says how much one thing changes when another does: a 1-point rise in engagement goes with an x% rise in customer satisfaction.
- A level means little alone. A benchmark (better than peers, worse than last year) only gives relative meaning. Managerial meaning needs business units, usually money, or a known link to them.
- Many HR teams pick a few plausible people measures, track their levels, and simply assert they drive performance. A box-and-arrow chart of "people, then customers, then profits" gives an illusion of understanding without measuring any arrow.
- **Benchmarking on levels is risky for strategic measures**: it assumes the link between a people measure and results is the same in every firm. Those links are often firm-specific.
- Even so, good level measures come first: a strong link between A and B is worthless if A and B are badly measured.

## Start top-down, not from available data
Write the value-creation story first, then measure its links. A cautionary example, a manufacturer's pilot, went wrong because:
1. It used data that existed rather than data that mattered (an annual attitude survey, budget variance), and studied a call centre because data was there, not because it was core.
2. It lumped conflicting effects together (cost control and new-sales revenue pull in different directions).
3. It put process measures (call speed, volume) in the customer box instead of as drivers of customer satisfaction.
4. The attitude survey was one large, vague measure that couldn't be split into ability, motivation and focus, so it couldn't say what to change.
5. Other units saw no reason to help, so data came from a few units only.
6. Measures arrived on different cycles (operations monthly, revenue and customers quarterly, survey yearly), so everything was squeezed to annual and fewer than thirty data points remained.

Test for any proposed measure: imagine the first report. What decision would it inform? Would a manager outside HR find a recommendation based on it persuasive? Would it justify a budget decision?

## Correlations are not enough
A correlation (from -1 to +1) is unitless, covers two variables only, and can be produced by a third. Example: stores that gave more sales training also installed faster tills, so training and customer satisfaction correlate partly because of the tills.

What managers need: a multivariate estimate, in business units, of one factor's separate effect. For example, "a 20% gain in this skill adds $300 of cross-sales per employee per week". Published examples:
- A telecom unit built a seven-item engagement index from its staff survey and found that a 1% rise went with nearly 0.5% higher customer satisfaction with service.
- A retailer found that a 1% rise in customers' willingness to recommend went with revenue gains of about 7% in one department and 4% in another, and that the staff-side drivers differed by department (working conditions in one, pay in the commission-paid one), with attentive managers mattering everywhere.

## Handling other causes (confounders)
1. **Most outcomes have a vital few causes.** Knowing the business lets you name them.
2. **A third factor only distorts the link if it moves with both.** If product information is equally available to all staff, it can't bias the skill-to-sales link. If better-skilled staff also get better information, it can.
3. **If you can measure it, you can control for it**: include it in the model (multiple regression or similar; you needn't run it yourself but should know when it's needed).
4. **The hard case is a joint influence you can't measure.** Thinking it through at least tells you how big the problem might be.
5. Exception: if two causes move almost perfectly together, their separate effects can't be separated.

## Look at the shape, not only the slope
Relationships are often not straight lines. In the retailer's data, customers who gave a 10 out of 10 were far more likely to recommend the store (about 82%) than those who gave a 9 (about 33%). "Satisfied" was not enough; enthusiasm drove referrals. Check whether effects jump at a threshold before setting targets.

## Match the level of analysis
Cause and effect must be measured at the same level: person, team, branch, unit or firm.
- **Build a parallel measure lower down**: a parcel firm's company-wide time-in-transit index means nothing to one driver, but off-route miles per driver feeds into it, so training that cuts off-route miles has measurable strategic value.
- **Or aggregate up**: if customer satisfaction exists only per branch, average the individual people measures to branch level.
- Choose the level that answers the decision, not the one that is convenient.

## Leads and lags
People measures lead; customer and financial results lag. You don't need the exact delay, but you need data over several periods so that, say, a people measure two periods back can be compared with a result one period ahead. An annual survey is too infrequent for this.

## Build the case link by link
You rarely prove the whole chain to profit. Proving people practices move some interim drivers (retention, cycle time, customer retention) is real progress: evidence for seven of twenty drivers beats none. In long value chains (manufacturing, R&D) start with the first, easiest links (retention policy, then a stable R&D team, then product cycle time).

## Getting it adopted
- Managers grow attached to legacy metrics and keep them after they stop being useful; the most accessible measures are often the least useful.
- Expect about two-thirds of people to be indifferent or opposed to a new measurement system; a practitioners' rule is to put energy into the supportive third rather than converting the rest (see `leading-change`).

## Worked pattern (own numbers)
A 12-branch tyre-and-service chain wants to know if technician training pays.
- Story: training raises first-time-fix rate, which lifts customer recommendation, which lifts repeat revenue.
- Level: branch (revenue is only reported per branch); training hours averaged per technician per branch.
- Data: monthly, 24 months, so 288 branch-months rather than 12 annual points.
- Confounders that move with both: newer equipment (measured: include it), branch manager tenure (measured: include it), local competition (not measured: note it).
- Lag: test training in month t against first-time-fix in months t+1 to t+3.
- Result expressed as money: 10 more training hours per technician per quarter goes with 3 points higher first-time-fix and about £1,800 more repeat revenue per branch per month, against a training cost of about £900. Report the range of uncertainty and the unmeasured competition caveat.
