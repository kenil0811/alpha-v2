# Leading and motivating people

> **Area:** `leading-and-motivating-people` · owned by the CHRO skill. **Use when:** Help an owner or manager lead people well, by choosing a leadership approach that fits the situation, using the right kind of power, re-engaging unmotivated staff through role design and reinforcement, and articulating a personal leadership purpose. Use for questions about managing a team, an underperforming or disengaged employee, a new manager's style, or a founder's leadership story.

## Outcome
Staff who commit rather than merely comply, roles that motivate, and a leader who knows their own tendencies and can explain why anyone should follow them.

## When to use
- "How should I manage this team?" or "I've just been promoted to manager."
- "My best employee has gone flat."
- "Staff do the minimum."
- "I need to write my leadership story / pitch for investors, hires or a promotion."
- A one-to-one or monthly review with a struggling employee.

## Method

### 1. Know which power you are using
Five bases of power and the response each tends to produce:
| Power | Source | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Coercive | can punish | resistance |
| Reward | can give rewards | compliance |
| Legitimate | position or title | compliance |
| Referent | people like and identify with you | commitment |
| Expert | people depend on your knowledge | commitment |
Lean on referent and expert power for anything needing discretionary effort. Formal authority sets the floor; informal influence through relationships and networks drives change. (For organisational politics, see `power-and-influence`.)

### 2. Choose leadership behaviours, not a personality
Leadership is mostly learned behaviour (twin studies put it at roughly 30% inherited, 70% developed). Some traits help people *emerge* as leaders (extraversion, conscientiousness, openness, intelligence), but effectiveness comes from behaviour adapted to followers.

Repertoire, from least to most effective in most settings:
- **Laissez-faire**: abdicates, avoids decisions. Avoid.
- **Abusive supervision**: ridicule, blame, belittling. Drives turnover, burnout, counterproductive behaviour. Never.
- **Transactional**: contingent reward (pay for effort, recognise results) and management by exception (active: watch for deviations; passive: step in only when standards fail). Good baseline for routine work.
- **Transformational** (four behaviours): individual consideration (know and coach each person), intellectual stimulation (challenge thinking), inspirational motivation (simple vision, high expectations), idealised influence (role model; charisma helps but can mislead).
- **Servant**: removes obstacles, puts followers' growth first; linked to satisfaction, trust, helping, team performance and psychological safety.
- **Paternalistic**: caring parent figure, moral example and decisive authority; fits some industries and cultures with high power distance.
- Also **participative** and **empowering** styles.

Two traits run through good leadership: **empathy** (see others' view, retain talent through coaching) and **self-awareness** (your part in someone's underperformance; not everyone wants to be managed the way you do).

### 3. Manage your own tendencies
Find your outlier traits (very high or very low on a Big Five dimension) and how others read them. A strength in one role can become a liability in the next; most effective leaders have had to rein in or compensate for something. Ask for feedback on specific behaviours and practise a small adjustment.

### 4. Re-engage an unmotivated employee
**Step 1: listen first.** Ask how the role is going; hear and validate their concerns fully before any solution. Opening with accusations, distracted body language and "that's just the job" shuts people down.

**Step 2: enrich the job** (five core dimensions; standard Hackman and Oldham model):
| Lever | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Skill variety | How could you use more of your skills here? |
| Task identity | Which whole piece of work could you own start to finish? |
| Task significance | Who benefits from this work? (show them: customer feedback, impact data) |
| Autonomy | Where could you decide how the work gets done? |
| Feedback | How could you see your own results more often (scores, figures)? |

**Step 3: reinforce the right behaviours** (standard engagement steps):
1. Identify the few behaviours that most matter to the business right now.
2. Say them in plain language.
3. Find each person's preferred reinforcers (public vs private praise, new challenge, time off, mentoring others) by asking and watching.
4. Reinforce quickly after the behaviour; behaviour that is never reinforced fades.
5. Connect daily actions to the mission ("how does today's work move us towards it?").
6. Use frequent reinforcement while a habit forms, then intermittent.
7. Follow up.

**Step 4: check the system.** Unfair workload, a free rider nearby, or burnout can look like poor motivation (see `team-conflict-trust-and-feedback`). So can the measures: a rating or bonus metric people see as invalid or unfair cuts their commitment, and a single pay-linked metric crowds out the work it doesn't count. Check how the person is measured before blaming the person (see `people-analytics`).

### 5. Write your leadership purpose and story
1. **Core values**: pick 3 to 5 you live by regardless of circumstance, and define each in your own words.
2. **Motivators**: rank intrinsic (growth, mastery, autonomy, meaning) and extrinsic (money, status, recognition) motivators; you need both in balance.
3. **WHY statement**, one of these forms: "To [contribution] so that [impact]." Or "To use [skill] to enable [result] for [group] so that [goal]." Test it on three people and refine the words.
4. **Leadership narrative** (about one page or a 3 to 5 minute talk): a defining moment or person; the WHY; a limiting assumption you challenged; how you enable others to act; how you model the way (practices from the Kouzes and Posner leadership model).
5. Reflected best self: ask 10 or more people from different parts of life when you were at your best, find patterns, and redesign your role around those strengths.

### 6. Use expectations, including low ones from others
Being written off can motivate: people who sense others expect them to fail often work harder to prove them wrong, and perform better (Nurmohamed's studies). It helps only when the doubter seems not credible; doubt from a credible source breeds anxiety and hurts performance.
- Never set low expectations on purpose; show each person you believe they can succeed, and back it with training and resources.
- When outsiders (a rival, a sceptical customer, another department) doubt the team, say why their view is not well founded, pointing to times they were wrong.
- When the doubter is credible (a respected senior, a strong track record), shift the team's focus from proving them wrong to the task itself: plan for setbacks, treat nerves as excitement, keep a pre-work routine.

### 7. Lead the values by example
- Define what earns respect from each group: customers (deliver what you promise), staff (fairness and dignity), investors (transparency), regulators (never break the law), the community. Leaders apply the rules to themselves first, visibly, on small matters as well as large (a lesson from the founder of a widely respected IT-services firm).
- Apply the rules to high performers too. Excusing a star's misconduct tells everyone the values are for show; people stop reporting problems and the best leave (as one fast-growing tech firm's leadership crisis showed).
- Personal power grows with visible effort and with legitimacy, meaning acting in line with the values people already hold (see `power-and-influence`).

## Worked pattern
A dental practice owner's hygienist, a top performer for 4 years, now does the minimum.
- One-to-one: owner asks open questions and listens; hears that every day feels identical and patients never hear about outcomes.
- Enrichment: she owns the practice's gum-health programme end to end (task identity, variety); chooses her own recall schedule (autonomy); gets a quarterly report of her patients' improvement scores (feedback, significance).
- Reinforcement: she prefers private thanks plus training budget, not public praise. Owner thanks her within a day when the programme hits its first 50 patients.
- Owner's own check: high conscientiousness had turned into micromanaging schedules; he stops approving every booking.

## Common mistakes
- Relying on position and threats, then wondering why people only comply.
- Solving before listening.
- Assuming everyone is motivated by what motivates you (money, public praise).
- Treating leadership as fixed personality ("I'm just not a people person").
- Charisma without substance; charismatic leaders can lead teams astray.
- A vague purpose ("help others excel"); make it specific and your own.
- Protecting a high performer from rules everyone else must follow.
