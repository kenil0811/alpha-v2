# Influence principles, defences and the ethics test

Use in steps 6 and 7 of `power-and-influence` when choosing how to persuade, when checking whether someone is pushing you unfairly, or when a decision under pressure needs a values check. Built from Cialdini and Sagarin's "Principles of Interpersonal Influence" and Urbany, Reynolds and Phillips' "How to Make Values Count in Everyday Decisions"; wording ours.

## 1. The six principles
People use short-cuts to decide whether to say yes. The short-cuts are usually sensible, which is why they work.
| Principle | Why people respond | Honest use at work | Manipulative version to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | we repay what we receive | help, share information, make introductions before you ask; when refused, step down to a smaller request (a concession invites one back) | the "free gift" that creates a debt the other never wanted |
| Social proof | we do what similar others do | show that peers like them already use it, with real numbers | invented testimonials or claimed popularity |
| Commitment and consistency | we act in line with what we have said or done | start with a small step (a pilot), get the agreement in writing or said publicly, link the ask to their stated values | a chain of leading questions that traps a yes; bait-and-switch |
| Liking | we agree with people we like | find genuine common ground, give sincere specific praise, cooperate visibly on shared problems, meet in person first | flattery and fake rapport |
| Authority | we defer to real expertise | show credentials and evidence early; bring in a recognised expert | trappings of authority without the substance, or experts speaking outside their field |
| Scarcity | we value what is rare or about to be lost | say honestly what is lost by waiting; frame as loss avoided, which weighs more than equal gain | invented deadlines and "one time only" offers |
Combine principles: a respected peer (liking, authority) who already uses the idea (social proof) and asks for a small trial (commitment) is far more persuasive than any one alone.

## 2. The ethics test
Use a principle only when it is a natural part of the situation: the expertise is real, the consensus exists, the deadline is genuine. Making a principle up exploits a short-cut that normally serves the other person well, and it carries hidden costs: lost customers when discovered, and a workplace that rewards dishonest staff and then needs more surveillance.

## 3. Defending against influence
- Accept that you are susceptible; people who believe they are immune are the easiest to fool.
- Check that each principle is real: Is this person an expert *in this*? Is the deadline real? Are the "others" like me?
- Notice when a small yes is being used to lever a bigger one; decide the big one on its merits.
- Separate the gift from the request: you can accept help without owing an unfair deal.

## 4. Decision map for values under pressure
Use when a boss, client or partner asks for something that feels wrong, or when a decision trades profit against a principle.
1. **Options**: list at least two, then push for a third (refuse, delay, escalate, propose an alternative that meets the boss's real goal).
2. **Consequences**: for each option, the good *and* bad short-term results (people's reactions).
3. **Outcomes**: the longer-term effects that follow (career, legal exposure, customers, the firm's reputation).
4. **Values**: what each option serves and what it gives up (security, achievement, integrity, responsibility, loyalty).
5. **Compare the upsides**: what each option gets you, side by side. This shows what you are trading.
The common error is a lopsided map: only the upside of going along and the downside of refusing. Good people say yes to bad acts when values are fuzzy, when they read consequences to suit what they already wanted, and when they see no other option.

**For leaders**: values statements rarely change everyday decisions on their own. Train with decision maps on real, familiar decisions from your own business, and make the values specific to each stakeholder (what earns the respect of customers, staff, investors, regulators).

## 5. Persuasion habits of effective operators (from a study of a master legislator)
- Study the person before the conversation; know what they generally want and what they feel about this issue.
- Make the case, answer objections before they are raised, and close with what must happen next.
- Match tone to the matter: weighty language for principled issues, plain and quick for practical ones.
- Stories and analogies make points stick and make you good company; people agree more readily with someone they enjoy.
- Believe what you argue; doubt shows.
