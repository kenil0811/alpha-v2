# Managing up

Use when a working relationship with a boss is strained, when starting under a new boss, or when an owner wants a manager to work better with them. Built from Gabarro and Kotter's "Managing Your Boss" and two teaching cases (a fast-promoted salesman and a change champion); wording ours.

## Why it matters
Boss and subordinate depend on each other and both are fallible. The boss needs your cooperation, information and reliability; you need their link to the rest of the organisation, resources and priorities. Managing that relationship on purpose is not manipulation or flattery; it is doing the job well.

## 1. Understand the boss and their context
- Goals and objectives: what are they judged on this year?
- Pressures: from their own boss, clients, the board, peers.
- Strengths, weaknesses, blind spots.
- Preferred working style: do they read (want written material first, then discuss) or listen (want a briefing in person, then the paper)? Do they want detail or the headline? Do they want to be involved or to delegate and hear about exceptions?
Find out by watching, by asking directly, and by asking people who have worked for them.

## 2. Understand yourself
- Your strengths, weaknesses and style, and where they grate on the boss's.
- Your reaction to authority. Two unhelpful extremes: resenting and fighting any constraint (counter-dependent), or swallowing your disagreement and expecting the boss to know everything (over-dependent). Both are overreactions; aim for a working partnership.

## 3. Build a relationship that works for both
| Practice | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Fit both styles | adjust to theirs: shorter agendas for a terse boss, a written pre-read for a reader, a call for a listener |
| Clear mutual expectations | agree priorities, standards and deadlines explicitly; with a vague boss, write your understanding down and check it |
| Keep them informed | no surprises; bad news early, with what you are doing about it; more for a boss who likes to be involved |
| Dependable and honest | do what you said; never shade the truth; a boss who can't trust your information can't use you |
| Use their time and credibility selectively | bring them the issues only they can solve; don't spend their goodwill on small battles |
| Use each other's strengths | cover their weak spots (detail, numbers, people) and let them cover yours |

## 4. Disagreeing with a boss
- Disagree privately, with evidence and a proposed alternative.
- Show you understand their goal; argue about the means.
- Once decided, either carry it out properly or escalate openly through the proper route; never undermine it by complaining to peers.
- If your sponsor is above your boss, don't use the sponsor to go around the boss; sponsors rarely overrule a manager they appointed.

## 5. New in role or promoted fast (lessons from teaching cases)
- Your previous success earned the promotion; it does not earn your new boss's trust. Learn their standards in the first month.
- Ask senior colleagues for advice early, as your sponsor likely suggested; it builds allies and shows respect.
- Treat requests for follow-up, calendar updates and preparation as tests of reliability, not trivia.
- Track your own signals: if your boss starts writing summaries of meetings with you and copying others, the relationship is in formal trouble. Act at once: ask for a private conversation, acknowledge what is fair in the criticism, agree a short written plan, and report progress against it.

## 6. A first-month checklist for a new boss relationship
1. Ask for 30 minutes on their goals, pressures and how they like to work.
2. Agree three priorities and how often to update them.
3. Send a short weekly note in the format they prefer.
4. Deliver one visible early result on something they care about.
5. After a month, ask what you should start, stop and continue.
