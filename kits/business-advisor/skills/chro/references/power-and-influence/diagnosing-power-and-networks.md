# Diagnosing power and mapping networks

Use in step 3 to 5 of `power-and-influence` when you need to know who really decides, and whether your own network can carry what you are trying to do. Built from Pfeffer's chapters on when power is used and diagnosing power and dependence, and from Uzzi and Dunlap's network-building article; wording ours.

## 1. Define the political units
Pick the level that matters for the decision: individuals, departments, or groups sharing a background (a profession, the founders' circle, the people from an acquired firm). Groups share interests when they share training, incentives and the information that comes with their position.

## 2. Score the power of each unit on several indicators
Any one indicator misleads (pay may reflect the job market; an office may be a leftover). Score each unit 1 to 5 on as many as you can see:
| Indicator | What to look at |
|---|---|
| Decisions won | outcomes of recent contested decisions (budgets, hires, strategy calls) |
| Senior representation | share of top jobs, board or committee seats held by the unit's people |
| Key committees | presence on product, capital budget, planning and pricing groups |
| Resources | budget share, headcount growth, capital allocated |
| Pay | the head's pay and starting pay compared with similar roles inside and outside |
| Reporting level | how close to the top the unit reports |
| Promotion rate | how fast its people move up compared with others |
| Symbols | location (headquarters or not), floor, office size, who sits near the boss |
| Reputation | ask several people separately who gets things done and whose support matters |
Watch for shifts over years: a profession taking over roles another used to hold (lawyers replacing engineers in senior posts, say) shows power moving.

Two cautions: look for power where people disagree and the issue matters, since nothing is revealed when everyone agrees; and don't confuse predicting the winner with being able to make it happen.

## 3. Interdependence map (one page)
| Player | Need from them | Can they block? | How affected (power, measures/pay, daily work) | Stance | Their power | Their allies | Who they listen to |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
Fill it for everyone the four questions in the main file turn up. Add people affected indirectly. The best surprise is no surprise.

## 4. Personal network worksheet
1. List 10 to 20 key contacts: people you rely on for private information, specialist skill, advice, or ideas.
2. For each, write who introduced you (or "me").
3. For each, write whom you have introduced them to.
4. Read it:
   - If "me" appears in more than about two-thirds of the middle column, your network is probably inbred: you chose people like you.
   - Names that recur in the middle column are your brokers; nurture those ties and thank them openly.
   - An empty third column means you rarely broker for others, which limits what people will do for you.
   - Check diversity: functions, firms, industries, seniority, background. Clusters of people who all know each other add trust but little new information.
5. Plan: two shared activities in the next quarter that put you alongside different people where something is at stake, and one broker to reconnect with.

## 5. Signs your network is failing you
Calls about interesting work stop; you hear of decisions after they are made; your contacts all agree with you; you have dropped the outside activities that used to connect you. The usual cause is pulling back to "focus on the job"; the usual fix is to restart shared activities.

## 6. Reading the informal organisation (standard practice)
- Who do newcomers go to for advice? Who is copied on everything?
- Who is consulted before the formal meeting? Whose objection ends a discussion?
- Which assistants and coordinators control calendars, agendas and access?
- Who eats or socialises together across departments?
Ask these in casual conversations, not surveys.
