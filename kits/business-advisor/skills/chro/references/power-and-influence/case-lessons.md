# Case lessons on power: how it is gained, used and lost

Use when a situation resembles one of these patterns, or to stress-test an influence plan ("which of these traps am I walking into?"). Each lesson is generalised from a teaching case on organisational politics. The facts are summarised in our own words and the lessons are ours.

## 1. The change champion who lost her sponsor
**Case:** A program manager at a technology firm drives a strategically right shift (buy a component outside instead of using the in-house design) against a powerful engineering vice-president. She wins the analysis repeatedly but is attacked in meetings, undermined with customers, the subject of rumours, and finally removed just before launch. The founder who appointed her and promised protection turns on her in public.
**Lessons:**
- A sponsor's private promise of protection is weak; when the fight becomes costly, the sponsor protects their own interests and the person they cannot do without.
- Hearsay about an opponent is not a diagnosis. Talk to the blocker first, learn what they stand to lose, and look for a role for them in the new way.
- Winning the analysis is not winning the decision; each "win" in a meeting that humiliates the other side hardens the opposition.
- Speed creates enemies. Building relationships at the top is part of the work, not a distraction from it.
- Silence about rumours and sabotage lets them spread; raise them early through someone with authority, with facts.
- Watch for allies who go quiet when it counts (the respected expert who "does not remember"); secure their public support before the meeting.

## 2. The analysts who surprised the line managers
**Case:** A task force of bright staff analysts presents forecasting fixes to the market managers whose judgment they criticise. The managers ridicule the work, shout down their juniors and refuse it outright; the meeting is suspended.
**Lessons:**
- Never let powerful people hear criticism of their work for the first time in a group meeting. Pre-wire: meet each one privately, show the findings, and let them shape the recommendations.
- Juniors presenting conclusions critical of their own bosses face retaliation; the sponsor, not the juniors, should carry the message.
- Data from another unit shared in confidence must stay in confidence; a leak destroys the source.
- A cooling-off break is a tactic: use it to re-map who objects and why, then come back with options rather than the same proposal.

## 3. The founder pushed out after decades of results
**Case:** An investment-firm founder with an extraordinary record grows harsh with colleagues, turns on each successor after a few years, refuses a mediator, and loses his chosen heir. When performance slips and partners stop tolerating his style, he is forced out of the firm he built.
**Lessons:**
- Performance is a source of power only while it lasts; a bad year turns tolerance into a case against you.
- Expertise without referent power is brittle: those who were bullied wait for the moment.
- Dependence works both ways; the firm needed him, but the people around him had alternatives and used them.
- Refusing outside help with a relationship (mediation, coaching) signals that the problem is you.

## 4. The star who fell from grace
**Case:** A celebrated academic with national standing, large donations and loyal followers clashes with his superiors, is passed over, accepts a senior post elsewhere, and is then accused of vandalism on thin evidence; the offer is withdrawn and his reputation is damaged before he can answer.
**Lessons:**
- External fame does not protect you inside an institution; the people who decide your fate are the internal power holders.
- Open conflict with your hierarchy (petitioning against your superior, for instance) makes durable enemies who act when you are exposed.
- Leaving a place is a vulnerable moment: you have spent your local goodwill and not yet built new allies.
- When accused, answer fast with facts and an account, and get credible allies to speak; silence lets the first story set.

## 5. The executive who would not conform
**Case:** A brilliant division head at a large carmaker with personal flair and a strong following moves to corporate headquarters, finds his power shrinks, refuses to conform to the corporate style, and resigns.
**Lessons:**
- Promotion can reduce power: running a division gives discretion and visible results; a headquarters role gives neither until you are accepted.
- In a strong culture, legitimacy comes from fitting in; individuality signals disloyalty and costs influence. Choose deliberately which norms to honour and which to challenge.
- Know whether your power is positional (stays with the job) or personal (moves with you).

## 6. The new manager with no authority
**Case:** A young new hire is put in charge of a sales programme that long-serving branch managers must carry out, though they don't report to him and resent it; the most senior one skips the training and he must decide how to respond.
**Lessons:**
- A title from above is not authority over people who don't report to you. Work through their boss and their informal leader.
- The respected old hand is the key: win her or neutralise her privately before any reprimand; a public sanction makes her a martyr.
- Small slights early (requests refused, support delayed) are signals of where real power sits; read them before they escalate.
- Separate friendship from business carefully, but use friends' knowledge of the informal system.

## 7. The rising star who ignored his new boss
**Case:** A high-performing salesman promoted fast by a senior sponsor clashes with his immediate boss over style and forecasts, ignores requests for follow-up, criticises the boss's numbers to others, and within months his career is in crisis with no allies left.
**Lessons:**
- Your sponsor's support does not override your boss's daily judgment; a sponsor who must choose usually backs the manager.
- Adapt to the boss's working style (detail, preparation, prompt replies) even when you think it is unimportant; style failures are read as performance failures.
- Disagree on substance privately and with evidence, never by complaining to others.
- Being right about the forecast does not help if you have no relationships to carry the point.

## 8. The operator who learned the system
**Case:** A national politician's early career and legislative years. A young congressional aide masters how things get done by questioning every experienced aide he can find and charming the gatekeepers; later he studies each senator so closely that he can frame every request as what that senator already wanted, and controls committee seats to reward loyalty.
**Lessons:**
- Learn how the system actually works from the people who run its plumbing (assistants, clerks, experienced staff).
- Know each person's general motives and the specific feeling driving them on this issue, then tailor the appeal.
- Information about who stands where (a vote count) is power in itself.
- Control of rewards (assignments, roles) builds loyalty, but used as a test of personal allegiance it breeds resentment.

## 9. The builder who bent the rules
**Case:** A public-works builder's career. An idealistic reformer becomes a master of power: he drafts laws that quietly give him wide powers, ties his projects to a cause the public loves (parks), starts construction before opponents can stop it, trades favours with local power brokers, and finds that the power he holds shields him from the legal consequences of overreach.
**Lessons:**
- Whoever writes the rules and the first draft shapes the outcome.
- Framing a contest around a popular end can make the means look unimportant to the public, for a while.
- Once work has physically started, it is hard to stop; this is a legitimate tactic for approved projects and an abuse when used to get around the rules.
- Power that insulates you from consequences is a warning sign, not a licence; use the discussion question the case raises (can one have real influence without giving up principles?) as a test of your own plan.

## 10. Values under pressure
**Cases:** an engineer's account of harassment reports at a fast-growing tech firm dismissed because the offender was a high performer, and the leadership crisis that followed; and a founder's interview on building a widely respected IT-services company (values defined as respect from each stakeholder, and leaders setting the example).
**Lessons:**
- When a firm protects high performers from its own rules, it tells everyone the values are for show; the cost arrives later, larger, and often for the leaders themselves.
- HR that serves power rather than fairness loses the people who report problems first.
- Values hold when they are defined per stakeholder (what earns respect from customers, staff, investors, regulators) and when leaders apply them to themselves first, especially on small visible matters.

## Using these lessons
Before a political move, scan the list and write one line for each pattern that applies: "I am at risk of lesson X because..., so I will...". Re-check after any surprise in the situation.
