# Brand and positioning

> **Area:** `brand-and-positioning` · owned by the CMO skill. **Use when:** Build a brand's foundation (vision, mission, values, personality, proof) and a positioning that owns a clear functional and emotional space in customers' minds: a positioning statement, a brand essence statement, and a map of where the brand sits against rivals. Use when launching a product or company, when a brand "looks like everyone else", when customers file the product in the wrong category, or before writing campaigns, a website or a pitch.

**Outcome:** a short set of internal documents the whole business can act on: a brand foundation, a four-part positioning statement, a one-paragraph brand essence, and a perceptual map showing the open space the brand claims and can defend.

## When to use
- A new product or business is months from launch and nobody can say in one paragraph what it stands for.
- Customers compare the product to the wrong thing ("does it integrate with what we already use?").
- The brand is losing distinctiveness: competitors copy the features that once set it apart.
- Choosing a name, a look, a tone of voice or a spokesperson.
- Before `digital-marketing-and-channels`, `strategic-communication-plan` or `business-storytelling`: those carry the message this area defines.

Related: `consumer-behavior-marketing` (why the levers below work: attention, emotion, memory), `market-sizing-and-unit-economics` (which segment the brand is built for), `pricing-strategy` (price must match the position), `competitive-strategy` (CEO: the business-level choice the brand expresses).

## Method

### 1. Treat the brand as four layers, not a logo
- **Observed:** the marks you own (name, logo, colours, sound).
- **Perceived:** what people think and say about you, managed over time.
- **Experienced:** what you deliver at each touchpoint; experience drives perception.
- **Relationship:** the two-way bond built from what you provide, do and say.
A brand is judged on the last three. A logo cannot fix a broken experience.

### 2. Check whether the brand earns demand and price
A strong brand is (the Kantar Meaningful-Different-Salient framework):
- **Meaningful:** meets a need functionally and connects emotionally.
- **Different:** felt to be unique, setting the trends in its category.
- **Salient:** comes to mind quickly when the need arises.
Brands high on all three win more choice and can charge more. A brand that matches rivals on policy and product (fees, seat choice, perks) drifts towards "a sea of sameness" and loses its pricing power even when it is still liked. Audit yearly: list what the brand does that rivals do not; if the list shrinks, the brand is eroding.

### 3. Build the foundation, bottom up
| Layer | Question it answers | What to write |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | Why do we exist, what do we stand for? | Vision (broad, aspirational, long-term outcome), mission (the specific work done now to reach it), 3 to 5 values |
| Personality and appearance | What is our style and presence? | Archetype, 3 to 5 traits, tone of voice, visual identity |
| Credibility | Why should anyone believe us? | Proof points (evidence, people, sourcing, guarantees, results) |
| Unique value | Why should anyone care? | Outcomes the customer gets, functional and emotional |

**Values test.** Each value must pass two checks: **focus** (few, high-impact, can we actually live it?) and **clarity** (would an employee or customer understand it at once?). Values show in everyday decisions, not on the wall. Trust and integrity are the baseline for every segment, not a differentiator.

**Personality.** Ask "if the brand were a person": what relationship (friend, guide, challenger)? what goals, passions, values? Pick a primary archetype (for example innocent, sage, explorer, rebel, magician, hero, jester, caregiver) and express it through traits such as sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication or ruggedness. The archetype may flex by audience (a software brand can be the magician to buyers and the hero to users) but its core stays.

**Appearance.** Logo simple, memorable, scalable and meaningful; no more than about five colours and three typefaces in three sizes; colour choices follow the brand strategy; the same naming, look and voice on every account and property.

**Proof before customers.** A new brand has no reviews. Show, don't claim: ingredient sourcing, founders' expertise, independent tests, certifications, a guarantee, a public commitment with numbers.

### 4. Position on the customer's outcome and tension
Positioning is the space the brand holds in the customer's mind relative to the alternatives. If you do not define it, the market will, using whatever reference point it already knows.
1. **Find the desired outcome**, not the feature. Ask why people use the category and how they feel when it works (a clean home means comfort, hope, esteem and satisfaction, not just "clean").
2. **Find the tension**: what stops existing options from delivering that outcome (functional gap, emotional cost, a cultural conflict such as convenience versus the planet).
3. **Reframe the competition**: position against the real alternative the customer uses (email attachments, doing nothing, a home remedy), not head-on against the category leader.
4. **Place it on two axes**: functional strength (utility, durability, performance) and emotional resonance (how it makes them feel about themselves). The four outcomes:
   - Low function, low emotion: forgettable commodity.
   - High function, low emotion: reliable workhorse (bought, not loved; price-pressured).
   - Low function, high emotion: feels good but flimsy (loved until it fails).
   - High function, high emotion: transformational, the target.
5. **Read the moment**: cultural and competitive shifts (wellness, sustainability, nostalgia) open adjacent positions; claim the one your proof supports, and legally protect names and claims early.

### 5. Write the positioning statement (four parts)
| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Desired outcome and problem | What the target customer wants, and why current options fall short |
| Solution | How the product meets that desire, functionally and emotionally |
| Support | The proof that makes the promise believable |
| Enhanced outcome | How the customer feels, or what identity they express, after choosing the brand |
Test it on these themes: a real customer tension, clear difference from named rivals, both functional and emotional value, all four parts telling one story, plain language. A classic one-line form (standard practice): "For [target] who [need], [brand] is the [frame of reference] that [point of difference] because [reason to believe]."

### 6. Write the brand essence statement
One internal paragraph that captures the heart of the brand: the truth it believes, why it exists in a way no other brand does, and how it makes people feel. Tests: human, easily understood, true to the proof, and useful for saying no to off-brand ideas. It guides decisions; it is not a tagline.

### 7. Map the competitive space
Pick the method by the question (standard marketing analytics):
- **Who competes with whom:** behavioural data. Cross-price elasticities (a price cut by Y takes sales from X) and brand-switching probabilities (bought X last time, Y this time) show the real competitor set. Large cross-elasticity or switching between two brands means they share customers.
- **Overall similarity map:** customers rate how alike each pair of brands is; multidimensional scaling places them on a map. Best at finding the competitor set, but the axes have no labels.
- **Attribute rating map:** customers rate each brand on chosen attributes; factor analysis reduces them to two or three dimensions and draws attribute vectors (a brand's projection on a vector is its standing on that attribute; long vectors name the axes). More actionable, but only as good as the attribute list.
- **Two brands only:** a profile ("snake") plot of average ratings per attribute, side by side, is enough; with more than three or four brands it becomes unreadable and a map is needed.
- Average perception ratings across respondents only after checking they agree (look at the spread, and split by group if users and non-users differ); never average preferences that way, because people see a brand alike but want different things.
- **Three uses:** check that the intended position was achieved and find gaps and rivals' weak spots; test where a new concept will land once people have read or used it; and, with ideal points added, steer product development toward what segments want.
- Limits: a map is a snapshot (repeat it to track movement) and says nothing about the cost or odds of moving.
- Use similarity data (or switching data) to find who competes, then attribute ratings to explain why and to find empty space. A quick workshop version: plot rivals on the two attributes customers cite most, from reviews and interviews (standard practice).
Detail and a worked layout: `cmo/references/brand-and-positioning/perceptual-maps.md`.

### 8. Accept who the brand is not for
Building a brand to connect strongly with some means it will not connect with others. Name the segments it will not chase (see segment readiness in `market-sizing-and-unit-economics`), and keep the brand consistent rather than diluting it to please everyone.

### 9. Carry the brand online
Write an **online value proposition**: what a visitor gets from the brand online that they cannot get offline or from rivals (content, tools, community, personalisation), consistent with the core position. Brand must feel the same on every channel while content is native to each (`digital-marketing-and-channels`).

### 10. Tell it as a story
Statements are not remembered; stories are. Express the archetype and the tension in a narrative (a rebel water brand mocking plastic and purity clichés), backed by proof (sourcing, recyclable packaging, donations). Storytelling structure: `business-storytelling`.

## Worked pattern
A start-up sells refillable, plant-based cleaning tablets to urban renters.
- **Outcome and tension:** renters want a home that feels calm and cared for; mainstream cleaners feel harsh and wasteful, eco brands feel preachy and weak.
- **Map:** on "cleaning power" versus "feels good to use", the big brands sit high-power/low-feel, eco brands low-power/high-feel; the high/high corner is empty.
- **Positioning statement:** Renters who want a calm, cared-for home are stuck choosing between harsh chemicals and eco products that don't cut grease (problem). The tablets dissolve into a full-strength cleaner in the user's own bottle, with a scent designed for small spaces (solution). Independent lab grease tests match the leading spray; one tablet replaces a plastic bottle (support). Cleaning becomes a five-minute reset that makes a small flat feel like a home (enhanced outcome).
- **Foundation:** vision "every small home feels like a sanctuary"; mission "full-strength cleaning without the waste or the guilt"; values: honest results, small footprint, calm by design (each passes focus and clarity). Archetype: caregiver with sage traits (sincerity, competence). Proof before launch: lab test, founder's chemistry background, refill guarantee.
- **Not for:** heavy-duty commercial cleaners and price-first bulk buyers.
- **Check:** the yearly "what do we do that rivals don't" list has three items at launch; if a major brand launches tablets, the scent-for-small-spaces and refill guarantee must carry the difference.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the brand as a logo and colour palette.
- Positioning on features or the category name, so customers compare you to what they already use.
- Fighting the category leader head-on instead of the real alternative.
- Values that are many, vague or not lived; claims with no proof.
- An emotional promise the product cannot deliver (feels good but flimsy).
- Trying to appeal to everyone; changing personality from channel to channel.
- Drawing a perceptual map on attributes the team chose, without checking they are the ones customers use.
- Calling a concept positioned when the map puts it in the pack, or where it promises more than it can deliver.
- Copying rivals' policies until nothing distinctive is left.
