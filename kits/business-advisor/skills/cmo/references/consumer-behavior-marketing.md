# Consumer behaviour and persuasion for marketing

> **Area:** `consumer-behavior-marketing` · owned by the CMO skill. **Use when:** Understand why customers buy and design marketing (offers, menus, ads, stores, sign-up flows) that works with how people actually notice, remember, feel and choose. Use when diagnosing weak marketing, segmenting a market, testing a change, or writing a persuasive offer.

**Outcome:** a business knows who its customers are, why they act as they do, and which
psychological levers (attention, memory, emotion, persuasion, biases, choice design) to pull,
then tests the change instead of guessing.

## When to use
- "Why aren't people buying / noticing / coming back?"
- Choosing a target segment, or checking whether a segment is real.
- Writing an ad, a menu, a landing page, an offer, a price display, a sign-up flow.
- Deciding whether a marketing change worked (or planning a test).
- Auditing a local business's marketing: one thing done well, one thing to fix, each tied to a principle.

Related: `brand-and-positioning` (the brand and position these levers express), `digital-marketing-and-channels` (where the levers are applied), `market-sizing-and-unit-economics` (needs-based segments, readiness and resistance).

## Method

### 1. Start with research that fits the question
Two families of research, with different powers:
- **Non-experimental** (observation, focus groups, surveys, interviews): good for discovering
  what happens and generating ideas. Cannot prove cause.
  - Observation: shows real behaviour; slow, and you see what, not why.
  - Focus groups: rich, quick discussion; loud voices and group pressure distort it.
  - Surveys and interviews: scale and specifics; but people often cannot report their true
    reasons (introspection fails) and misreport what they did (memory and self-image).
    Treat stated reasons as hypotheses.
- **Experiments**: the only way to show cause. Hallmarks: you manipulate one thing (the
  independent variable), measure an outcome (the dependent variable), and randomly assign
  people to versions. Random assignment makes the groups alike on everything else, so a
  difference in outcome is caused by the change.
- Correlation trap: two things moving together (laptop use and grades, ad spend and sales) may
  share a third cause. Do not act on correlation as if it were cause.

**Business experiment checklist** (standard practice for business experiments):
1. A testable hypothesis tied to a decision ("free delivery over $30 raises average order").
2. A control group and a test group, assigned at random (stores, days, customers).
3. A sample large enough to see the effect; run long enough to cover weekly cycles.
4. One change at a time, measured on the metric that matters (profit, not clicks).
5. Leadership agrees in advance to act on the result, either way.
6. Watch for obstacles: contamination between groups, seasonality, staff who "help" the test.

### 2. Segment by what predicts behaviour, not by convenience
- Demographic segments (age, income, gender, family, generation) are common because the data
  is easy to get and media is sold that way. They are often too broad: a generation is not a
  customer type, and generational labels mislead.
- Better: segment on needs, usage and psychological traits that predict buying:
  - **Tightwads vs spendthrifts:** tightwads feel the "pain of paying" strongly and underspend;
    reduce payment pain for them (bundles, flat fees, prepaid, framing as small daily amounts).
  - **Maximizers vs satisficers:** maximizers search for the best and reach objectively better
    picks but feel worse about them; satisficers stop at good enough and feel happier. Give
    maximizers comparison tools and reassurance; give satisficers a clear default.
  - **Personality-based targeting:** match message tone to trait (for example, adventure for
    open people, security for anxious ones). Ethics: be transparent, use consented data,
    never exploit vulnerabilities.
- Do not ignore underserved groups (seniors often have money and time and are overlooked).

### 3. Win attention
- Attention is selective because capacity is limited; while people attend to one thing,
  the rest is processed shallowly or missed (change blindness, multitasking costs).
- Individual factors: **motivation** (people notice what is relevant to their goals; the
  "cocktail party" effect for their own name; perceptual vigilance for needs) and **arousal**
  (moderate arousal sharpens attention, too much narrows it).
- Stimulus ("source") factors that capture attention: size, colour and contrast, motion,
  novelty and surprise, position (eye level, first or last), faces, personal relevance.
- Perception is active: people fill gaps using Gestalt grouping (proximity, similarity,
  closure, figure and ground) and context. Design layouts so the intended grouping is the
  one people see.

### 4. Be remembered and become a habit
- Memory fails in predictable ways (transience, absent-mindedness, blocking and more). Names
  and surface details are forgotten fastest; meaning is kept.
- Get into long-term memory by **elaborative rehearsal**: link the brand to things people
  already know (associative network), use meaning, stories and distinct cues, and repeat with
  spacing.
- **Encoding specificity:** recall is best where learning happened. Put reminders at the point
  of purchase that match the ad (same jingle, colour, character).
- Absent-mindedness comes from divided attention; fix it with cues at the moment of action
  (reminder texts, packaging prompts).
- **Habits:** behaviour reinforced in a stable context becomes automatic. Instrumental
  conditioning: add a pleasant result (positive reinforcement), remove an unpleasant one
  (negative reinforcement), add an unpleasant result (punishment), or remove a pleasant one
  (omission). Build habits with a stable cue, low friction and a reward; break competitors'
  habits by adding friction to them or disrupting the context (moves, new routines).

### 5. Use emotion deliberately
- Emotions are short, intense, about something, and push to act (unlike moods).
- **Feelings as information:** people read their current feelings as a verdict on the product.
  A good mood at the point of judgment lifts ratings.
- Happiness signals "all is well": people process less critically (good for simple offers,
  bad for complex claims that need scrutiny).
- Humour works when it is a **benign violation**: something is wrong, but safe. It backfires when
  the violation hurts someone or the brand's own credibility.
- Anger makes customers certain, blaming and action-prone (complaints, switching, bad reviews).
  Avoid creating it; resolve it fast.
- Fear appeals need two parts: a real threat and a clear, doable response (efficacy). Fear
  without an easy action produces avoidance.

### 6. Persuade
- **Two routes** (elaboration likelihood): the central route (people think about arguments)
  needs both **motivation** (relevance, stakes) and **ability** (time, knowledge, no
  distraction). Without them, the peripheral route rules: cues like attractiveness, number of
  arguments, celebrity, design. Central-route change lasts longer.
- **Pique technique:** an unusual request ("can you spare 37 cents?") breaks the automatic
  "no" and raises compliance.
- **Cialdini's principles:** reciprocity (give first: samples, useful content), commitment and
  consistency (small first yes), social proof (others like me do it), liking (similarity,
  compliments), authority (credible experts), scarcity (limited, losing out), and **unity**
  (shared identity: family, place, joint action, co-creation).

### 7. Design for biases and expectations
- Two systems: fast intuitive (System 1) and slow deliberate (System 2). Most buying is fast.
- Heuristics to anticipate: **affect** (feelings outweigh numbers; one identifiable person moves
  people more than statistics), **availability** (vivid, recent things seem common),
  **representativeness** (conjunction fallacy; ignoring regression to the mean), **anchoring**
  (first number sets the frame for price), **overconfidence** (more information raises
  confidence faster than accuracy).
- **Choice overload:** large assortments attract browsers but reduce buying and satisfaction.
  Curate; offer a default; but avoid offering a single option (people want at least a comparison).
- **Reference points:** gains and losses are judged against an expectation (list price, last
  price, competitor). Set the reference before showing the price.
- **Expectations shape experience:** price, label and presentation change how good a product
  actually feels (placebo effects). Preferences are constructed on the spot, so presentation matters.

### 8. Shape the choice (nudges)
- Choice architecture: the way options are presented changes choices without removing any.
- **EAST** checklist for a nudge: make it **Easy** (defaults, fewer steps, less friction),
  **Attractive** (salient, rewarding, personal), **Social** (show what peers do, use commitments
  to others), **Timely** (prompt when people are ready to act; immediate costs and benefits loom largest).
- Guardrails: transparent, easy to opt out, and in the customer's interest. Hidden nudges that
  betray customers trigger backlash.

### 9. Social and wellbeing levers
- Cool = **optimal newness** (most advanced yet acceptable) and **optimal distinctiveness**
  (people want to fit in and stand out: same category, different variant). People diverge
  on identity-signalling goods and converge on functional ones.
- Group variety-seeking: in groups people order different items to avoid seeming the same,
  and enjoy their pick less. Taking orders privately helps.
- Offering someone to delegate a decision to (a stylist, an expert pick) raises purchases.
- Gifts: recipients value what they asked for, practical and flexible items (gift cards)
  over surprise and over-personalised items. Sell gift registries and flexible gifts.
- Happiness: experiences beat things; time-saving purchases raise wellbeing but are under-bought;
  pay first, consume later; buying for others lifts happiness. Use these as positioning.

## Worked pattern
A café sees flat weekday lunch sales. Step 1, observation: customers stand at the menu board
for 40 seconds and many leave. Step 7: the board lists 34 items (choice overload). Hypothesis:
a curated board of 8 items with one highlighted "today's pick" (default, step 8) raises
conversions. Test: alternate boards by day for four weeks, assigned at random. Outcome metric:
transactions per 100 visitors and average ticket. If the curated board wins by 10 percent,
keep it and add social proof ("most ordered this week").

## Common mistakes
- Trusting what customers say over what they do.
- Calling a correlation a result; skipping the control group.
- Generational stereotypes as a segmentation strategy.
- Humour that offends, fear without a doable action.
- More options and more information assumed to be better.
- Nudges that hide the opt-out; personality targeting without consent.
- Audit claims about a business that are not specific and verifiable.
