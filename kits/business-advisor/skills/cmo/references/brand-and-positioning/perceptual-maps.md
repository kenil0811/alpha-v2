# Perceptual maps and competitor sets

Part of `cmo/references/brand-and-positioning.md`.

## Ways to find the competitor set
| Method | What you ask or use | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaided recall | "Which brands come to mind for X?" | Shows salience | Misses brands people would switch to but don't name |
| Aided recall | Show a list, ask which they would consider | Fuller consideration set | Prompts inflate it |
| Overall similarity (multidimensional scaling) | Rate every pair of brands from 0 (same) to 10 (very different) | Most accurate on who competes with whom | Axes unlabelled |
| Attribute ratings (factor analysis) | Rate each brand on 6 to 25 attributes | Labelled, actionable axes | Wrong attribute list gives a wrong map |
| Cross-price elasticity | Sales data: % change in X's sales when Y's price falls 1% | Real behaviour | Needs price variation and data |
| Switching matrix | Panel or loyalty data: last purchase versus this purchase | Real behaviour, shows loyalty on the diagonal | Asymmetric; needs repeat-purchase data |

## Collecting and showing perception data
- Scales: agreement with statements on 1 to 10, or a 5 or 7 point scale between opposite adjectives (cheap to premium).
- Two brands: a profile plot (each brand's average per attribute, lines side by side) shows the gap at a glance.
- Attribute map, how to read it: attributes that do not differ across brands drop out; attributes that move together collapse into one dimension. Vectors pointing the same way measure one idea; at right angles they are unrelated; pointing opposite ways means the market sees a trade-off. A brand's standing on an attribute is where a perpendicular from the brand meets that attribute's vector (extended through the origin if needed). Vector length, where shown, says how well the map captures that attribute.
- Similarity map, inputs: for n brands each respondent ranks all n(n−1)/2 pairs from most to least alike (6 brands, 15 pairs). MDS finds positions whose distances follow those ranks and reports a fit statistic; the analyst names the axes from category knowledge, which is a judgement call. Use it where people cannot name the attributes (taste, scent, style).

## Testing a new concept on the map
Show the concept (a statement, or better a trial), map it with the rivals, and read the zone it lands in: on the intended dimension and distinct (viable); in the middle of the pack (not worth launching); on the parent brand's existing strength (the parent's image swamped it); or better than everyone on both axes (a promise it cannot keep). Remap after launch to see whether communication moved it.

## Reading a switching matrix
Turn counts into row shares: of those who bought A last time, what share bought each brand this time. The diagonal is repeat rate (loyalty). Off-diagonal shares well above the brand's overall share mean the two brands are close substitutes. Pairs that switch heavily with each other form a sub-market; price and promotion fights happen inside it.

## Reading a cross-elasticity table
Rows are the brand whose sales change, columns the brand whose price changes. Diagonal: own-price elasticity (negative). Off-diagonal large positives mark close rivals; near-zero entries mean the brands barely compete even if they sit in the same aisle. Elasticities are asymmetric: a premium brand's price cut can take share from a value brand more than the reverse.

## Attribute-rating map, step by step (standard practice)
1. Pick attributes from customer interviews and reviews, not from the team.
2. Survey 100+ category buyers; each rates 6 to 10 brands on each attribute (1 to 10).
3. Average by brand, run factor analysis (or principal components) on the brand-by-attribute table, keep two or three factors.
4. Plot brands by factor scores; draw attribute vectors from the origin. Name each axis after the longest vectors that point along it.
5. Add an "ideal point" per segment (where that segment's ideal brand would sit) from a rating of "your ideal brand".
6. The opportunity is a segment ideal point far from every brand, in a direction your proof can support.

## Combining the two
Use similarity or switching data to decide which brands belong on the map, then attribute ratings to explain the gaps and choose the position. Published research finds that similarity maps place competitors more accurately and attribute maps are more actionable.
