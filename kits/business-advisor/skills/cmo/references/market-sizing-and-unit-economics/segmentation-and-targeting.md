# Needs-based segmentation, readiness and targeting

Part of `cmo/references/market-sizing-and-unit-economics.md`.

## 1. Segment by scenario of need first
List the circumstances that trigger the need (for a rehydration drink: sick children, adults after drinking, travellers, people in heat, athletes, event-goers). Each scenario carries a job to be done (the task) and a desired outcome (how life is better after). Four trigger families recur: life events, lifestyle, construction or setting changes, and economics.

Then layer the classic factors onto each scenario:
| Layer | Examples | Tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic, geographic | Age, income, region, climate | Who, where, how many |
| Psychographic | Values, identity, attitudes, background, culture, community | Why they would care |
| Behavioural | Current solution, frequency, channel, spend | How hard the switch is |
Market size then measures problem prevalence (people in the scenario × frequency × value), not a headcount of a demographic.

## 2. Who does, who might, who never
Place groups on two dimensions: interest (love it → reject it) and resistance (least → most). Name the segment that is already hard-wired for the product (values, norms, beliefs align), the ones that might come later, and the ones you will never target. Saying who you are not for is a targeting decision too.

## 3. Read resistance
Sources of resistance the product or the marketing must overcome:
- **Status quo bias:** the current way feels safe.
- **Loss aversion:** losses weigh more than equal gains; switching costs loom.
- **Endowment effect:** the longer people have used something, the more they value it.
- **Confirmation bias:** new information is judged against what people already believe.
- **Cognitive fit:** how complex the product is compared with current behaviour.
Product changes that lower complexity, guarantees, trials and familiar formats each attack one of these.

## 4. Perceived value against behaviour change
| | Little behaviour change | Big behaviour change |
|---|---|---|
| **High perceived value** | Smash hit: launch here | Long haul: worth it, budget time and education |
| **Low perceived value** | Easy sell: adopted, but little gain or loyalty | Sure failure: avoid |
Perceived value includes psychological value: the product must match a motivation (health, safety, esteem, independence, idealism, power) and keep a positive emotion going after purchase.

## 5. Score segments on readiness
Rate each candidate segment (1 to 5) on:
- **Motivational fit:** a strong, unmet motivation already aligned with what you provide.
- **Emotional readiness:** open to change and ready to act now (emotions are hope, curiosity, confidence, not anxiety or distrust).
- **Your ability to deliver:** product readiness for this segment's needs, reachable through channels you can afford.
- **Externalities:** cultural, technological, economic or regulatory forces pushing for or against (timing).
Launch where all four are high; sequence the next segments by the same scores. The biggest market is rarely the first one: a technology pushed at a large but unready segment (high resistance, low functional value) burns money.

## 6. Build the segment profile
For the launch segment, write a one-page profile:
- Scenario summary: the trigger, the change in behaviour expected, the needs during the change.
- Needs and desired outcomes (functional and emotional).
- Journey stages (consider → try → continue) with, at each stage, motivations, emotions, perceptions and behaviour.
- Barriers and the resistance types active.
- Where they spend attention (media, communities, events).
- Who influences them.

## 7. B2B segmentation
| Dimension | What it captures | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Industry | Sector, business model, regulation | Priorities and language of value |
| Firmographics | Size, age, revenue, ownership, growth stage | Scale and potential |
| Function | Departments and roles involved | Entry point and framing |
| Buying process | Decision makers, influencers, users | Who to engage, in what order |
| Culture | Mindset, decision style | Motivations and triggers |
| Circumstances | New leader, regulation, relocation, crisis, expansion | Urgency and timing |
| Offering maturity | Readiness to use your solution | How much education, proof and integration support |
Start from needs: list the jobs buyers must get done, group them into a few job categories, and map rivals against each job (who leads where). This shrinks a crowded landscape into a few lanes and shows where the pain is sharpest and least served. Rank lanes by pain and fit; pick industries within the top lane.

**Inside the account:** identify champions (users who gain most), fence-setters and blockers. For champions: equip (data, stories, materials), involve early, make them feel valued. For fence-setters: educate, validate with third-party proof, keep informed. For blockers: listen, acknowledge, reframe around "why now" and the risk of doing nothing. Ask: which roles champion us, where they sit, how the team works, shared pains, who holds the budget, what they have in common.
