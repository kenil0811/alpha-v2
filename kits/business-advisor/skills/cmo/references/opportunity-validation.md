# Opportunity validation

> **Area:** `opportunity-validation` · owned by the CMO skill. **Use when:** Find and test a new business opportunity (a new venture, product line or service) before money is spent on it, by putting customer pain first and turning every assumption into a cheap test. Use when someone asks "is this a good idea?", "who would buy this?", "what should we test first?", or wants a business model sketched on one page.

**Outcome:** a go, pivot or stop decision on a business idea, backed by evidence from real customers rather than the founder's enthusiasm, plus a one-page business model whose riskiest assumptions have been tested cheaply.

## When to use

- A founder or owner has an idea for a new venture, product, service line or market.
- "Would people pay for this?" "Who is the first customer?" "Why hasn't anyone done this?"
- Before a pitch, plan or fundraise (do this first; the pitch skill builds on it).
- A large company wants to explore a new opportunity the way a startup would.

## Method

### 1. Lead with pain, not the solution

People change their behaviour only when something hurts enough. A "nice to have" (pleasure) rarely beats the status quo; a "must have" (pain) does. Pain-driven products sell faster, spread by word of mouth, cost less to market and carry higher margins.

Ask the three questions of every idea:
1. What problem does it solve?
2. What pain does it relieve, and how sharp is that pain?
3. Who cares? (Who has the pain, how many of them, and do they pay?)

Get the order right: **pain, then problem, then solution; sell it, then build it.** The common failure is the reverse: a solution looking for a problem, built first and sold later.

Finding pain-driven ideas:
- Cultivate indignation: note what annoys you or your customers repeatedly ("what a pain").
- Listen for watchwords: "I hate", "always", "every time", "workaround", "we just live with it".
- Look for things everyone privately dislikes but assumes others accept.
- Favour problems you know personally and deeply (founder-market fit). Successful innovators usually know both the problem and the current state of the art.

Test whether it is a good problem:
- Is it real (observed), not hypothetical?
- Is it more than an incremental improvement on what exists?
- What do people use today, including "do nothing"? Doing nothing is a competitor.

### 2. Expect resistance to change (behavioural economics)

Plan for these when judging demand:
- **Status quo bias:** people stick with defaults even when told they are worse. Getting a trial is harder than founders think.
- **Loss aversion:** losses weigh about twice as much as equal gains. Switching costs feel larger than benefits.
- **Present bias:** benefits must be immediate and obvious. "Saves you money over three years" sells poorly.
- **Choice overload:** another option does not automatically add value; complexity delays decisions.
- **Overconfidence:** founders overrate their odds and how much customers like the idea. Treat your own conviction as a hypothesis.
- **Denial and cognitive dissonance:** solving a problem is not enough if customers do not admit they have it.
- **Impatience the other way:** owners quit too early when early profits are low but fundamentals are good. Judge on fundamentals, not the first months.

Implication: "If only 1% of the market tries it..." is a red flag. Show why a specific customer will switch now.

### 3. Write the hypotheses (13 questions)

Before talking to anyone, answer in one line each. Every answer is a guess to test:
1. My target customer is...
2. The pain they want solved is...
3. It can be solved with...
4. They cannot solve it today because...
5. The measurable outcome they want is...
6. My main way to acquire customers is...
7. My earliest adopter is... (narrow: "first-time mothers with children under three", not "parents")
8. I will make money by...
9. My main competition is...
10. I will beat them mainly because...
11. My biggest risk to financial viability is...
12. My biggest technical or delivery risk is...
13. Which assumptions, if wrong, kill the business?

### 4. Sketch the business model on one page

Use the **Business Model Canvas** (nine boxes in four areas):
- Customers: segments, channels, relationships.
- Offer: value proposition (pain relieved, gain created).
- Infrastructure: key activities, key resources, key partners.
- Financial viability: cost structure, revenue streams.

Or the **Lean Canvas** variant for early ideas: problem (top 3), existing alternatives, customer segments and early adopters, unique value proposition (one clear sentence on why you are different and worth buying), solution, channels, revenue, costs, key metrics, unfair advantage (something not easily copied or bought).

Rules: sketch it in one sitting; leave boxes blank rather than guess; write for the present stage; separate the **customer** (who pays) from the **user**; sketch a separate canvas per segment.

Check alignment: the competitive strategy (what you sell, to whom) and the organisation (people, incentives, resources) must fit each other.

A third lens, from lean-startup practice: a business model is four linked sets of choices. **Customer value proposition** (need, segment, differentiation or low cost, MVP and roadmap, complements, pricing, switching costs), **technology and operations** (what to do in-house versus outsource, cost drivers, IP, scalability), **go-to-market** (channel mix and partner margins, acquisition cost against lifetime value, free versus paid demand, the chasm from early adopters to the mainstream, whether to race for scale) and **cash flow formula** (contribution margin, fixed costs, break-even volume as a share of the market, capital per dollar of revenue, depth and timing of the cash trough). Make one quick back-of-envelope pass through all four early, to catch elements that contradict each other (for example, a product that needs explaining face to face but whose margin cannot pay for direct sales). The full question set and a test of whether a model can transform its industry are in `cmo/references/opportunity-validation/business-model-design.md`.

### 5. Create value and capture it

Value created (X) and share captured (Y) are different and can move in opposite directions. Easy-entry markets (low fixed costs, many rivals, no proprietary edge) are easy to create value in and hard to profit from. Capture comes from differentiation that lasts: proprietary technology, scale economies, network effects, brand. Most small ventures that claim to be unique are in fact in competitive markets; be honest about which you are.

### 6. Map the value proposition

**Value Proposition Canvas:** on the customer side list jobs to be done, pains and gains; on the offer side list products, pain relievers and gain creators. Fit is when relievers and creators match the most important pains and gains found in interviews.

One-line test: "Our [product] helps [segment] who want to [job] by [reducing a pain] and [enabling a gain], unlike [alternative]."

**Buyer utility map** (Kim and Mauborgne, *Blue Ocean Strategy*): rows are levers of value (productivity, simplicity, convenience, risk reduction, fun or image, environmental friendliness); columns are buyer experience stages (purchase, delivery, use, supplements, maintenance, disposal). Plot where existing offers deliver value; a strong idea removes the biggest block to utility in a cell nobody serves. Then check price against what the mass of target buyers already pays for substitutes, and whether you can hit that price with a profitable cost. The price corridor, cost target, partner and pricing-model questions are in `cmo/references/opportunity-validation/price-corridor-and-business-model.md`.

### 7. Customer discovery (get out of the building)

1. Pick the segment and the early adopter.
2. Interview to understand the problem, not to sell the solution. Ask about the last time the pain happened, what they did, what it cost, what they tried.
3. Do not pitch, do not ask "would you buy?" (people are polite). Ask for commitments: time, a pilot, a deposit, a letter of intent.
4. Look for patterns after each batch of interviews; update the canvas.
5. Iterate: hypothesise, test, learn, then persevere, adjust or pivot.

Rough targets (standard practice): 20 to 30 problem interviews before building; 100 or more before claiming a segment is validated.

Run the cheapest test of the riskiest assumption first: landing page with a sign-up, concierge (manual) service, pre-sales, a pilot with one customer. Build-measure-learn, as fast as possible.

### 7a. Turn assumptions into falsifiable tests

1. **Write each hypothesis so it can fail**, with a number and a date: not "it will spread by word of mouth" but "each new user brings at least 0.5 more within twelve months".
2. **Pick the minimum viable product (MVP):** the smallest set of features or activities that could disprove that hypothesis. Constrain features (ship only the must-have), constrain operations (people doing by hand what software will do later, hidden from the customer), or run a smoke test for a product that does not exist yet (landing page, demo video, letter of intent, a paid pre-order). Charge in smoke tests whenever you can.
3. **Set the pass mark before the test** (for example, a 5% conversion rate needed for the model to work).
4. **Rank tests by risk removed per dollar.** Deal-killers first (assumptions that, if wrong, end the venture: often whether anyone will pay, or whether a needed input exists at a workable cost); then path-dependent forks (choices that are costly to reverse, such as which market to enter first; keep options open by outsourcing until resolved); then cheap, quick wins. Test in sequence when one result decides whether another matters; in parallel when both are independent and speed matters.
5. **Guard the reading of results:** false positives from enthusiast samples; false negatives from a badly built MVP; stated versus revealed preference (act on what customers do); surprises (unexpected uses can be the better business). Use cohorts and A/B tests for numeric metrics.
6. **Decide:** persevere, pivot (change one or more of the four model elements while keeping the vision), or stop. Do not scale before product-market fit (demonstrated demand from early adopters and solid profit potential), except platforms with strong network effects, which may need users before they can test how to earn.

Step-by-step detail, the pivot types, limits of the method and the biases to guard against are in `cmo/references/opportunity-validation/hypothesis-testing-and-mvps.md`; generalised lessons from teaching cases are in `cmo/references/opportunity-validation/case-lessons.md`.

### 8. Keep options open (entrepreneurial mindset)

Entrepreneurs act like holders of real options: commit small amounts to learn, then expand or abandon.
- Convert fixed costs into variable ones: rent, outsource, license, partner, pay on success. Pursue the opportunity before owning the resources.
- Stage commitments around milestones; at each, decide to stop, redirect or continue.
- A small, lean firm can often test an opportunity that a large firm would reject because its fixed-cost machine makes the test too expensive.

### 9. Decide

Go when: a specific segment has sharp, admitted pain; early adopters commit something; you can reach them affordably; unit economics work on paper (see market-sizing-and-unit-economics); the kill-assumptions have survived tests.
Pivot when the pain is real but the segment, solution or channel is wrong. Stop when nobody will commit after honest tests.

Once the venture is running, recheck it periodically against goals, strategy and execution: `cmo/references/opportunity-validation/venture-health-check.md`.

## Worked pattern

Idea: scheduling software for small HVAC contractors.
- Pain: owners lose two to four hours a day rebuilding the schedule by phone when jobs overrun; missed appointments cost repeat business.
- Who cares: owner-operators with 3 to 15 technicians; early adopter is the owner who still dispatches personally.
- Status quo: whiteboard and phone. Barrier: switching mid-season feels risky (loss aversion), so offer setup off-season and a 30-day parallel run.
- Riskiest assumption: owners will pay $300 a month. Test: 25 interviews, then a concierge pilot where the founder dispatches by hand for 5 firms at $150 a month.
- Result: 4 of 5 pay and renew, 2 refer peers. Go, with the off-season launch.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with the solution and searching for a problem (confirmation bias).
- Defining the customer as "everyone"; no early adopter named.
- Treating surveys, likes and compliments as validation instead of commitments.
- Ignoring "do nothing" and spreadsheets as competitors.
- Incremental ideas (a slightly better version) that do not justify switching.
- Benefits that are deferred or hard to see.
- Building a full product before testing demand; spending before learning.
- Overconfidence about how fast customers will change.
- Vague hypotheses that cannot fail ("people will like it"), or no pass mark set before the test.
- Testing only on enthusiasts, then extrapolating to the mainstream.
- Spending months on interviews of intermediaries when a two-week seminar or a few phone calls could test the deal-killer.
- Escalating commitment: continuing because of what has already been spent (sunk cost).
