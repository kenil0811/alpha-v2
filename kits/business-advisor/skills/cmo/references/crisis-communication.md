# Crisis communication: prepare, respond, repair

> **Area:** `crisis-communication` · owned by the CMO skill. **Use when:** Prepare for and respond to a business crisis: a pre-crisis plan with risk brainstorm and pre-drafted statements, first responses using the CAP model, social and employee messages in the right order, a press conference statement with Q&A, and an apology that repairs trust. Use before a crisis to get ready, or the moment one breaks.

**Outcome:** the business has a plan and drafts ready before anything goes wrong, and when it
does, it speaks fast, accurately and humanly, in the right order, protecting people first and
reputation second.

## When to use
- Building a crisis plan for an organisation (pre-crisis).
- An incident has happened or is about to go public: injury, data breach, product defect,
  environmental harm, executive misconduct, regulator action, viral complaint.
- Drafting holding statements, social posts, an employee email, a press conference opener,
  reporter Q&A or a public apology.

## Method

### 1. Know the crisis lifecycle (CERC)
Use the CDC Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication (CERC) model. Its phases: **pre-crisis** (prepare), **initial** (first hours: acknowledge,
express concern, give what is known, say what to do), **maintenance** (updates, correct
rumours, explain), **resolution** (recovery, lessons, rebuild), **evaluation** (review the
response). CERC principles: be first, be right, be credible, express empathy, promote action,
show respect.

### 2. Pre-crisis plan (do this now, client-ready, 7-8 pages)
1. **Organisation summary** (≤1 page): what it does, size, locations, stakeholders, past incidents.
2. **Crisis brainstorm** (≤1 page): list plausible crises for this type of organisation,
   grouped, for example: operational (outage, recall, accident), people (misconduct,
   layoffs, death), data and cyber, legal and regulatory, financial, environmental,
   reputational (viral post, partner scandal), natural disaster. Rate each by likelihood and impact.
3. **Pre-drafted statements and posts** (≤3 pages, at least two): holding statements with
   blanks for facts, a social post set, an employee note, a customer notice.
4. **Recommendations** (2-4 pages): crisis team and roles (lead, spokesperson, legal, HR,
   operations, social media monitor), approval chain that works in an hour, contact lists,
   media training, monitoring tools, dark website or page, drills twice a year, AI use and
   disclosure policy for statements, after-action review.
Format as a branded business report: headings, bullets, single-spaced.

### 3. First response: the CAP model
Every initial statement has three parts:
- **Concern:** express genuine care for those affected, first, before facts about the company.
- **Action:** what you have done and are doing now (stopped the product, called authorities,
  helping families, investigating).
- **Perspective:** context that helps people judge the risk, and what they should do; when
  the next update comes.

### 4. Choose a response stance
Situational crisis communication theory (Coombs; standard practice):
- Victim crises (disaster, rumour, product tampering): low blame; instruct and express concern.
- Accident crises (technical error): moderate blame; explain, correct, compensate.
- Preventable crises (misconduct, known risk ignored): high blame; full apology and corrective action.
Rule: the more responsibility the public assigns, the more accommodating the response must be.
The classic model is a 1980s product-tampering recall: the maker put customers first, pulled the
product nationwide and redesigned the package. A cautionary case is a company attacking the regulator's safety warning
after a child's death: defending the product before showing concern deepens the crisis.

### 5. Apology rhetoric (apologia)
Strategies to recognise and use deliberately (Ware and Linkugel; extended by image repair
theory, standard practice):
- **Denial** (only when true and provable), **bolstering** (recall good record and values),
  **differentiation** (separate this act from what it looks like), **transcendence** (place it
  in a larger frame or purpose).
- Image repair adds: evading responsibility (accident, good intentions), reducing offensiveness,
  **corrective action** (fix it and prevent recurrence) and **mortification** (sincere admission
  and apology).
- A credible apology: names the wrong specifically, takes responsibility without "if", shows
  understanding of harm, states corrective action and how progress will be reported.

### 6. Urgent response package and timing
When several crises run at once, write for each:
- A **4-post social thread**: (1) acknowledge and concern, (2) what we know and did,
  (3) what affected people should do and where to get help, (4) when the next update is.
- An **employee email** from the executive team: the facts, what to say and not say to
  customers and media, who handles enquiries, support available.
- **Sequence** by harm and spread: life and safety first; then issues where people must act
  (change passwords after a breach); then reputational issues. Employees hear before or with
  the public, never after. Log the time and the reason for each release.

### 7. Press conference
1. **Message map** (see `strategic-communication-plan`): one core message, three key
   messages, proof points.
2. **Opening statement** (≤5 minutes) built on CAP.
3. **Talking points** the whole executive team agrees on; one spokesperson leads.
4. **Q&A prep:** 5-7 likely reporter questions (who is to blame, when did you know, will
   anyone be fired, is it safe now, what about compensation, has this happened before) with
   answers that bridge back to key messages. Never speculate, never say "no comment";
   say what you will find out and when.

### 8. After the crisis
Hold an after-action review: timeline, what worked, delays, message accuracy, stakeholder
reaction; update the plan and statements.

## Worked pattern
A retailer faces a customer data breach at 9:00. By 10:00, a holding post: "We learned this
morning that some customer payment data may have been accessed. We are sorry and we are taking
this seriously (concern). We have closed the affected system and called in investigators and
law enforcement (action). If you shopped online since 1 May, watch your card statements; our
help line is open now. Next update at 3 pm (perspective)." Employee email goes at 9:45 with
the same facts and a script. Press conference at 4 pm with a map built on: protect customers,
find the cause, make it right.

## Common mistakes
- Silence or "no comment" while others fill the gap.
- Defending the brand or blaming victims, regulators or customers before showing concern.
- Speculating on facts not yet confirmed; contradicting earlier statements.
- Employees learning from the news.
- A plan with no approval chain fast enough for the first hour.
- Apologies with "if anyone was offended" or no corrective action.
