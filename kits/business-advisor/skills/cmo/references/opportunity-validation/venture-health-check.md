# Venture health check: goals, strategy, execution

A periodic check for a venture already running, from a classic article on the questions founders must
answer. Ask the questions in order: big issues before details. Run it at least
yearly and whenever growth stalls or races (the cadence is standard practice).

## 1. Are my goals clear?
- **Personal aims:** quick profits, steady income for a way of life, a capital gain from selling, or a lasting institution? "Independence" is too vague.
- **Size and durability needed:** a lifestyle business can stay small and depend on the founder; one built to sell must run without the founder; an institution must renew itself across generations of products and staff.
- **Risk accepted:** durable businesses need long, risky bets (brand building, reinvesting profit, outside equity, personal guarantees, trusting new staff). Small personal-service firms trade that risk for endless hours and a business that is hard to sell.
- If the business, even if successful, won't meet the goals, or needs more risk than you'll take, reset the goals.

## 2. Is the strategy right? Four tests
1. **Defined:** does it say what needs the firm serves, where, with what capabilities, and rule out attractive-looking distractions? It should describe where the firm is going, not where it is.
2. **Profitable and able to grow:** what is the edge? If differentiated, does the premium cover the extra cost, at enough volume to cover fixed costs? If a commodity, are costs lower? Is the market big enough; do diseconomies of scale block growth? Hard work cannot fix weak economics. If it can't make money and can't grow, change industry or find new economies of scale or scope, or walk away; waiting for the big order or a buyer rarely works.
3. **Sustainable:** a firm riding a wave (new technology, deregulation, a shortage) prospers while supply lags demand. When the wave crests, have a durable model ready, or sell. Firms built on one or two strengths get copied; build several complementary capabilities (a product line, integrated operations, distributor ties, a responsive culture, a pipeline of new products).
4. **Right growth rate.** Grow faster when: scale, scope or network economies are strong; customers lock in or switch rarely; good locations or resources are scarce; rivals are racing; customers tolerate early flaws. Grow slower when: talent or capital is short; the firm funds itself (a self-funded firm can't grow sales faster than its return on equity); customers won't tolerate failures; the founder dislikes constant crisis.

## 3. Can I execute it?
- **Resources and relationships:** upgrade talent as soon as a track record lets you hire better (you can't coach height). Early customers and early money may not be the ones you need later. Some outsourced capabilities must come in-house; a firm with no assets and no staff signals low commitment and is hard to sell.
- **Organisation:** hard infrastructure (structure, systems, controls) should match the strategy. Sluggish growth and late products suggest too many rules; fast growth with slipping control suggests too few. Fast growers can give front-line staff wide autonomy while keeping tight central financial control. Build what growth needs: delegation with standard procedures, specialised roles with ways to coordinate them, cash forecasting, and clean records if you plan to sell.
- **Culture:** set the norms on purpose; otherwise the first hires' personalities set them, and culture is hard to change later.
- **The founder's role:** it should move from doing the work, to teaching it, to setting results, to shaping the context. Ask: have I learned a new skill lately? Do I want to keep changing? If not, plan a different role or exit.
