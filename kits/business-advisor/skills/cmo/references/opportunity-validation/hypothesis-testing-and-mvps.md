# Hypothesis testing and minimum viable products

Depth for `cmo/references/opportunity-validation.md` section 7a. From published work on hypothesis-driven
entrepreneurship and on beating the odds; our words.

## Why test, and what "lean" means

The biggest risk a new venture faces is building something nobody wants. Testing a falsifiable version of
each business-model assumption gets the most learning per dollar and per week. "Lean" means avoiding
waste, not spending little: a validated venture may later spend heavily on growth. Risk and value move in
opposite directions: each major risk removed raises what the venture is worth (one magazine founder's
reply-card test, at about double the usual response rate, raised the venture's value roughly tenfold).

## Three other ways founders launch, and why they fail

| Approach | Strength | Flaw |
|---|---|---|
| Build it and they will come | Strong vision | No customer feedback until launch |
| Stage-gate ("waterfall") plan | Coordination, predictability | Feedback only at the end; errors found late; assumptions go stale |
| Just do it (improvise) | Uses whatever resources appear | No rule for when or where to change course; early fixes clash with later needs |

The hypothesis-driven way keeps the founder's direction and adds a disciplined way to change it. Published
research: ventures that pivot once or twice are about half as likely to scale too early as those
that pivot never or many times.

## The seven steps

1. **Vision:** the problem and a possible solution. Ideas come from deep immersion in a problem (in
   business-to-business markets this usually needs years in the industry, or close observation of
   customers), obsession with the problem but not the first solution, time to incubate, combining unrelated
   ideas, writing ideas down to sharpen them, and working with collaborators.
2. **Hypotheses:** for each of the four model elements, write falsifiable statements with numbers. Make a
   quick pass through all four early; go deeper only as earlier answers firm up (you cannot estimate
   acquisition cost before choosing the segment).
3. **Specify MVPs:**
   - *Constrained features:* when early adopters will buy the must-have without the costly nice-to-haves, or
     when one segment needs fewer features than another (launch for the simple segment first). Keep the
     product simple enough for the mainstream; power-user features can label it "not for me".
   - *Constrained operations:* people do by hand what technology will do later; use it while the core
     features are still being defined and when the makeshift delivery does not distort the customer's
     experience.
   - *Smoke tests:* landing page with sign-up, demo video, letter of intent (for enterprise sales), paid
     pre-order or pledge. Charge when you can; a sign-up shows interest, a payment shows demand. A demo
     video is the responsible test for anything mission-critical.
   - *A series of MVPs:* move from friendly, high-touch tests to conditions like the real channel; early
     tests tune the offer so later tests do not fail for the wrong reason.
   - *Small customer sets:* just enough for a reliable result. A test brand can shield the main brand.
   - Fear of idea theft is usually overstated: execution, not secrecy, wins, and early feedback is worth more.
4. **Prioritise:** risk removed per dollar ("experimental return"). Deal-killers first, then
   path-dependent forks, then quick cheap fixes. Some checks are cheap and decisive (a patent search before
   building, when infringement could shut you down). Sequence what depends on earlier answers; run
   independent tests in parallel when a rival could get there first. Pick a committed first ("stake in the
   ground") customer early to fix a rough price while technical work continues.
5. **Run and learn:** test one thing at a time; limit the duration; distinguish targeted experiments (one
   risk: battery life, toxicity, bandwidth) from integrated ones (the business in miniature: a pilot, a
   test site), and give integrated pilots enough time. Watch for false positives (enthusiast samples) and
   false negatives (a slow, buggy MVP). Trust what customers do over what they say. Look for surprises in
   the data and outside it (a regulator, a rival, a fraud warning).
6. **Decide:** persevere, pivot or stop. Be willing to switch experiments off; often the hard part is
   stopping them, not starting them.
7. **Scale and keep optimising:** after product-market fit, tests shift from validating to improving.

## Pivot types

Change one element, keep the rest and the vision:
- Feature set: narrow it, widen it, or replace it while keeping the customer.
- Customer set: add a segment or switch segments.
- Technology and operations: change what is done in-house versus by partners.
- Go-to-market: change the main way of winning customers (paid search to viral, partners to direct).
- Cash flow formula: change how money is made (licensing to advertising, sale to subscription).

## Premature scaling

Scaling before fit loses agility, alienates many customers if a pivot follows, and burns runway (think of
runway as the number of pivots you can still afford). Exception: platforms with strong network effects may
need a large user base before partners appear and ways to earn can be tested; some defer charging on purpose.
Ventures born with large funding, and ventures inside big companies, struggle most with this: big budgets,
annual cycles and stage-gates push spending ahead of learning and keep failing projects alive.

## When the method fits less well

- Mistakes cannot be tolerated: no fix after launch, mission-critical use, or regulated testing (drugs).
  Use smoke tests and contingency planning instead of live trials on customers.
- Demand is not in doubt (a cheap, safe cure): test engineering options, not demand.
- Development cycles are long and demand is uncertain: test needs any way you can, and watch hardest for
  escalation of commitment.

## Biases and their counters

| Bias | What it does | Counter |
|---|---|---|
| Optimism | Overrates good outcomes | Falsifiable targets; a pre-mortem (each team member writes why the venture failed) |
| Planning fallacy | Underrates time and cost (validation often takes 2 to 3 times longer than planned) | Compare with similar past projects; let MVP results correct the estimate |
| Confirmation | Designs and reads tests to agree | Ask how a test could mislead; keep talking to sceptics, including investors who passed; a devil's advocate |
| Sunk cost | Persists because of past spending | Small early spend; treat pivots as normal from the start, with staff and investors |
