# Market entry and go-to-market

> **Area:** `market-entry-and-go-to-market` · owned by the CMO skill. **Use when:** Plan the go-to-market for a new product, service or market, at home or abroad: the full launch plan (target, brand, position, price, place, properties, media, trust, metrics), the beachhead segment and the order in which to win later segments, timing, and how fast to spend. For a new country, also choose how to enter: measure the distance to the market, score the entry modes from agent to own plant, decide what to own versus contract, pick a beachhead and stage the commitment with gates and kill criteria. Use when a business is about to launch something and asks "who first, in what order, and what is the plan?", or asks whether and how to enter a foreign market, whether to switch from a distributor to its own subsidiary, or whether to build or buy abroad.

**Outcome:** a go-to-market plan whose decisions reinforce each other, a launch segment and a sequence for the next ones, a chosen entry mode per segment, a beachhead, a staged plan with money released gate by gate, and the kill criteria agreed before the first dollar is spent.

## When to use
- A new product or service is months from launch (or launched with no paying customers) and needs a go-to-market plan.
- "Which customers do we go after first, and when do we move to the mainstream?"
- Deciding whether being first to market matters, or when in the season to launch.
- Choosing between selling to users first (bottom-up) or to buyers and executives (top-down).
- "Should we sell in country X, and how: agent, distributor, our own office, a partner, a plant?"
- A distributor abroad is underperforming or growing fast, and the owner asks whether to take the market in-house.
- A tariff, local-content rule or customer demand makes local assembly or a local plant look attractive.
- A partner offers a licence or a joint venture, or a local competitor is for sale.

Related skills: `market-sizing-and-unit-economics` (is the market worth it, segment readiness), `brand-and-positioning`, `digital-marketing-and-channels` (the growth engine), `new-product-forecasting` (adoption curves for each path), `industry-analysis` (the local industry's structure), `risk-and-compliance` (what it costs to get product in, and whether it qualifies), `pricing-strategy` (local price and price gaps between countries), `supply-contracts-and-coordination` (contract terms that align a partner), `real-options` (valuing staging and the right to expand or quit).

## Method

### 0. Go-to-market plan (any launch, home or abroad)
Most launch failures are not bad products but misalignment between market understanding, targeting and execution. Ignoring marketing execution makes forecasts too high; ignoring the full opportunity makes them too low. Treat the launch as one system of decisions, answered in this order, each checked against the others:
1. **Who to target, why, and when:** the segment that feels the tension most, adopts fastest and talks loudest; named segments you will not target (`market-sizing-and-unit-economics`, section 1b).
2. **Brand:** values, personality, proof (`brand-and-positioning`).
3. **Position:** a distinct functional and emotional space you can own and defend.
4. **Price and placement:** how to approach price (`pricing-strategy`) and where the product is discovered and bought (step 0b).
5. **Properties:** website, listings and social accounts ready to convert before launch.
6. **Media and growth engine:** content and channels for each stage of the journey, within the budget the business actually has (`digital-marketing-and-channels`).
7. **Trust gap:** proof, validators and trial before the brand has earned credibility.
8. **Metrics:** a few that show awareness, trial and repeat.
Separate what you control (product, business model, marketing) from what you can only steward or anticipate (brand image, people's psychology, externalities: environmental, economic, social, technological, political). Scan for cultural shifts by tracking the emotional energy of public conversation, not just its sentiment: rising negative energy is an emerging risk, rising positive energy an emerging opportunity. Finish by checking the plan against a list of common launch failures (template: `cmo/references/market-entry-and-go-to-market/go-to-market-plan.md`).

### 0a. Timing and first-mover claims
Being first brings learning curves, scale, scarce resources, customer relationships, standards and brand association, but followers learn from the pioneer's mistakes, uncertainty is highest early, and early-market buyers differ from the mainstream. Do not treat speed as the strategy. Do treat timing seriously: launch into the buying season (search-trend data shows it) and when the segment is emotionally ready; a product the market talks about more than it adopts is high risk, high potential.

### 0b. Sequence targets along a diffusion path
- **Start where interest is highest and resistance lowest,** with segments whose motivation and readiness fit the product's current state, then expand once traction and references exist. Many fast-growing brands started in narrow, passionate niches (a hunting-and-fishing core for a cooler brand, coffee shops' baristas for an oat drink, gyms and campuses for a fitness drink) before the mainstream.
- **Pick meaning-makers early:** the first segment and venue set what the brand means to everyone after. Choose those whose adoption others watch.
- **Users before buyers (B2B, developer-first):** solve one critical problem for users extremely well, make trial easy and cheap from their own budget, then expand within the organisation until the enterprise formalises the relationship and the product becomes a platform. Selling top-down to IT or procurement first often means long cycles and low close rates.
- **Product or platform:** enter an existing ecosystem as a product, or enable one as a platform; the choice changes who the first customer is.
- **Mass-market first versus credibility-first:** going broad builds scale and data fast; going to professionals first builds trust and controls exposure. Choose by what the next segment needs to see.
- **Placement sequence:** independent and specialty outlets or regional chains first, then mass retail once sell-through proves demand; each step should buy discovery as well as sales.
- **Plan spending by path:** model each path (big splash with partners versus niche-first) with analogous products' adoption (a conservative and an aggressive curve, see `new-product-forecasting`) and lay out year-by-year budget for product, research, content and media against it. Choose the path whose worst case the business can survive.

### 1. Frame the decision
Write down: the product lines, the target country, the decision date, the money and management time available, and what "success in 3 years" means (revenue, margin, share of a named segment). Entry is a sequence of decisions, not one; frame the first commitment, not the final footprint.

### 2. Measure distance to the market (CAGE) (Ghemawat; standard practice)
Distance, not market size alone, decides how hard entry is. Score each dimension low, medium or high and write what it implies.

| Dimension | What to check | What high distance implies |
|---|---|---|
| Cultural | Language, buying habits, taste, trust norms, how business relationships form | Local partner or local hires; adapt product and message; slower ramp |
| Administrative | Tariffs, product approvals and certification, government-buying rules, legal system, currency controls, political ties between the countries | Trade costs and approvals before any sale; consider local assembly or a local entity; read `risk-and-compliance` |
| Geographic | Physical distance, time zones, transport routes and lead times, climate, product weight and fragility | Local stock (3PL or bonded warehouse); longer cash cycle; service footprint |
| Economic | Income, price levels, labour and input costs, channel structure, the size of the target segment | Different price point or product tier; different channel |

Products differ in sensitivity: heavy, low-value goods suffer most from geographic distance; regulated goods (food, medical, defence, children's products) from administrative distance; taste-driven goods from cultural distance. Approvals can be per plant, not per company: a plant certified for one market may not be allowed to ship to another. Products built for one market's specification may have no buyers elsewhere.

### 3. Lay out the entry-mode ladder
From least to most committed:

| Mode | What it is | Typical when |
|---|---|---|
| Export via agent | Agent finds buyers for commission; you sell, ship and invoice | Testing demand; few large customers |
| Export via distributor | Distributor buys, stocks, sells and services; takes margin | Fragmented customers; local service and stock needed |
| Own subsidiary + 3PL | Local entity sells; a third-party logistics firm stores and ships | Proven demand; brand, price and data control matter |
| Licence | Local firm makes and sells under your IP for a royalty | Trade barriers or freight make export uneconomic; IP well protected |
| Joint venture | Shared-ownership local company | Partner holds something you can't buy: access, approvals, relationships |
| Acquisition | Buy a local firm | Speed, an installed base, or local status worth paying for |
| Greenfield plant | Build local production | Volume proven, landed cost or local-content rules demand it |

### 4. Score the modes
Score each feasible mode 1 to 5 (5 = best) on five criteria, weight them, and sum.
- **Control:** over price, brand, customer data, service quality.
- **Cost:** cash and fixed cost to enter (5 = cheapest).
- **Speed:** time to first meaningful revenue.
- **Risk:** money at risk plus IP, partner and legal exposure (5 = least risky).
- **Reversibility:** cost and time to exit or switch modes (5 = easiest).

Weights come from the business's situation: a premium brand weights control; a cash-poor firm weights cost and reversibility; a closing window weights speed. Agree the weights before scoring, so nobody tunes them to a favourite.

**Weight-sensitivity check:** move each weight up and down 25% in turn (renormalise so they sum to 1), and re-rank. If the winner holds, the choice is robust. If it flips, the decision rests on that weight: name it, and settle it explicitly with the owner. Also check which single score, if wrong, would change the winner, and verify that score first.

### 5. Own versus contract (OLI and transaction costs)
Integrating an activity pays only in a limited set of conditions; otherwise buy it on the market. Licensing is a contract; acquiring is ownership. Apply three tests (Dunning's OLI framing; standard practice):
- **Ownership advantage:** does the firm have something worth exploiting abroad (brand, technology, process)? If not, don't enter.
- **Location advantage:** is there a reason to produce there (tariffs, local content, freight, lead time, customer proximity)? If not, export.
- **Internalisation advantage:** is it cheaper to own than to contract? Own when the partner would need to make investments specific to you, when quality or know-how is hard to specify in a contract, when the partner could copy the IP or hold you up, or when the customer relationship and data are the asset. Contract when the activity is standard, competitively supplied and easy to measure.

Long-term contracts can stand in for ownership: committing up front changes how both sides behave, because each values the future relationship.

### 6. Stage the commitment (standard practice)
Commit incrementally: start where the cost of being wrong is small, learn, then step up. Each step should buy information (real demand, price acceptance, service cost, channel behaviour) before the next, larger step. Value the right to step up or quit with `real-options`; staging is worth most when uncertainty is high and early steps are informative.

Pick a **beachhead** (the launch segment of step 0b, placed on the map): one segment in one region where the product's edge is clearest, customers are reachable, references carry to the next segment, and competitors are weakest. Prefer a region with dense target customers so one stock point and one sales team cover it.

### 7. Choose mode segment by segment
A firm can use different modes for different segments at the same time: a distributor for small retailers, direct sales for large accounts, a licence for a low-price line, an agent for government buyers. Score each segment separately; the weights and scores differ. Watch for channel conflict (two of your channels chasing one customer) and settle it in the contracts.

### 8. Select and contract a distributor or agent (standard practice)
Selection: check their customers (do they reach your segment?), competing lines they carry, service ability, financial strength, stock-holding capacity and reputation with buyers. Call three of their current principals.

Contract terms that matter:
- **Exclusivity and territory:** grant exclusivity only for a defined territory and segment, and only tied to performance. Competition law in some places (for example the EU) limits how far you can stop sales outside the territory.
- **Minimums:** annual purchase or sales minimums; missing them ends exclusivity or the contract.
- **Term and termination:** short initial term, renewal on performance, termination for convenience with set notice. Some countries protect agents and distributors on termination (compensation or indemnity): get local legal advice before signing.
- **Data ownership:** you own end-customer data, warranty registrations and sales-out reports, delivered monthly. Without it, switching to your own subsidiary later starts from zero.
- **Brand and price:** trademark stays yours and is registered in your name locally; set marketing standards; resale-price rules depend on local competition law.
- **Stock, warranty and service:** who holds what stock, return terms, service levels, spare parts.
- **Buy-back and transition:** on termination, you may buy back stock and transfer customer accounts at agreed terms.

### 9. Set up a subsidiary (standard practice)
- **Entity:** a local company limits liability and is usually expected by large customers; a branch is simpler but exposes the parent. A dependent agent who signs contracts for you can create a taxable presence anyway.
- **Transfer pricing:** sales between parent and subsidiary must be at arm's length, documented under local and OECD-style rules. A common pattern is a limited-risk distributor earning a modest, stable margin, with the parent carrying market risk.
- **Who carries what:** decide who is importer of record (who pays duty and carries customs liability), who owns the inventory, and who carries currency risk (invoice in which currency, hedge or not). Write it into the intercompany agreement.
- **Basics:** bank account, tax registrations, payroll and employment law, insurance, product liability, local certifications.

### 10. Gates and kill criteria
Set them before entry. For each stage: the evidence needed to move on (revenue, gross margin after landed cost, reorder rate, number of reference customers, service cost per unit), the date it is judged, and the result that stops or reverses the step (exit, revert to distributor, pause). A kill criterion written after the money is spent is never used.

### 11. Analogue check
Find three to five firms that entered the same country in a similar product or from a similar home market. For each: mode chosen, sequence, how long to break-even, what went wrong, whether they stayed. Use trade press, the partner's own history, competitors' filings and customs import data. If most analogues switched modes after a few years, plan for that switch now (data rights, buy-back terms).

## Worked pattern
A mid-size European maker of premium espresso machines (€60M revenue) plans to enter North America. Two segments: home buyers through specialty retail and online, and specialty cafés (commercial machines needing fast service).

- **Distance:** cultural low-medium; administrative medium (electrical certification, tariffs to check); geographic high (ocean freight, service distance); economic low (similar income, large premium segment).
- **Home segment scores** (weights: control 0.30, cost 0.20, speed 0.15, risk 0.20, reversibility 0.15):

| Mode | Control | Cost | Speed | Risk | Reversibility | Weighted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agent | 2 | 5 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 3.60 |
| Distributor | 3 | 5 | 5 | 4 | 3 | **3.90** |
| Subsidiary + 3PL | 5 | 2 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3.40 |
| Licence | 1 | 5 | 3 | 2 | 2 | 2.45 |
| Joint venture | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 2 | 2.85 |
| Acquisition | 5 | 1 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 2.85 |
| Greenfield plant | 5 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2.20 |

- **Sensitivity:** each weight moved ±25% in turn: the distributor stays first every time; second place flips from agent to subsidiary when control rises. Robust.
- **Café segment:** service response is the purchase driver, and distributors in the region can't service commercial machines. Re-weighted (control 0.40, the other four 0.15 each) and re-scored (distributor control 2): the subsidiary with its own technicians scores 3.65 against the distributor's 3.35.
- **Plan:** beachhead = one dense metro region. Stage 1 (year 1): exclusive distributor for home, limited to that region, with a minimum of 2,500 units, customer data owned by the maker, 90-day termination and stock buy-back. Stage 2 (year 2, if cafés reorder): small subsidiary with 3 technicians, 3PL stock, limited-risk distributor margin. Kill: home sell-out below 1,500 units or gross margin after landed cost below 35% at month 12 → keep agent-only and stop spending.
- **Own vs contract:** no plant: no location advantage until volume passes a level where landed cost beats local assembly; recheck at each gate.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the 4 Ps as separate decisions; a launch plan whose price, place and promotion contradict the position.
- Targeting everyone, or the biggest market first, instead of the readiest segment.
- Assuming first to market wins; launching outside the buying season.
- Selling top-down to buyers when users would have pulled the product in.
- Choosing the mode before measuring distance; or treating a large market as an easy one.
- Signing nationwide exclusivity with no minimums and no data rights, then being unable to leave.
- One mode for every segment.
- Scoring with weights set after the scores, to fit a preferred answer; skipping the sensitivity check.
- Committing a subsidiary or plant before demand is proven, or staying too long with a partner who caps growth.
- Leaving importer of record, inventory ownership and currency risk unassigned.
- No kill criteria, or kill criteria set after entry.
- Assuming trade rules, tariffs or approvals from memory instead of checking them as of the decision date.
