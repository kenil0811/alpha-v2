# Business storytelling and persuasive presentations

> **Area:** `business-storytelling` · owned by the CMO skill. **Use when:** Turn data and findings into a business story that wins a decision: situation-complication-resolution structure, a win theme, insight-to-action slides, short pitches, and the questioning and listening that find the story. Use when writing a recommendation deck, a pitch, an outreach note, or a short "win the room" talk.

**Outcome:** findings become a clear, believable story that ends in a decision, not applause.

## When to use
- Presenting a recommendation to an owner, board or client.
- Turning a data analysis into "so what, now what".
- A 3-minute pitch, an elevator answer to "tell me about yourself", an outreach email.
- Interviewing customers or experts to find the story before writing it.

## Method

### 1. Find the story first: ask and listen
- Ask four kinds of questions:
  - **Clarifying:** what exactly do you mean? (removes ambiguity)
  - **Adjoining:** what about the related area we have not discussed? (widens the view)
  - **Funnelling:** why, how, what happened next? (digs into one point)
  - **Elevating:** what is the bigger issue here? (steps back to the pattern)
  Follow each answer with a question of another type.
- Probing questions that avoid yes/no: How long has this been an issue? What is the
  financial impact? What have you tried, and what worked? What would the ideal outcome be?
- Listen actively. Check yourself: how do I usually listen? Why do I need to listen now?
  Who is the focus? What am I missing? Am I in my own way, or in an information bubble?

### 2. Know the audience
- Who decides, what they care about (money, risk, mission, reputation), what they already
  believe, and what would make them say no. This sets the order, depth and tone.

### 3. Structure: situation, complication, resolution (SCR)
- **Situation:** the context everyone agrees with.
- **Complication:** what changed or what is at risk; the tension.
- **Resolution:** the recommendation and why it works.
Lead with the answer for executives (top-down), then the support.
Same shape for a 3-minute pitch: the challenge you were given, the approach you took and why
it was different, the solution and why it can be trusted. The listener should leave knowing
what success looks like, how it will be reached, and why it will work.

### 4. A win theme
One core idea that threads through every slide ("make the safe choice the easy one"). Every
section should visibly serve it. Where are we in the story? Each slide moves it forward.

### 5. From data to insight to action
- Data is a fact; an **insight** is what the fact means for the decision; an **action** is what
  to do because of it. Write slide titles as insights (full sentence), not topics.
- Show the **methodology** briefly: what you did and why it is credible (sources, interviews,
  sample sizes).
- Use a **value-adding framework** that fits the decision: organisational (who does what),
  process (steps), action (roadmap), decision (which option, which market first).
- **Validate:** show evidence for each claim and how the recommendation holds up against
  alternatives and risks.

### 6. Clarity and cohesiveness
- What is seen (slides) and what is experienced (the talk) must match.
- Rules that work: no more than about 20 slides, font 20 points or larger, data tables in the
  appendix, present only the essential slides when time is short (8 minutes means 6-8 slides).
- End with the decision you need, the owner, and what happens first.

### 7. Personal narrative and outreach
- "Tell me about yourself": past (what shaped you), present (what you do and are good at),
  future (why this conversation). Under 90 seconds; one concrete story.
- Outreach letter for an informational interview: who you are, why this person, a specific
  ask (20 minutes), flexibility, thanks. Prepare open-ended questions; send a thank-you and a
  reflection on what you learned.
- Executive presence: preparation, calm voice and posture, dress for the audience, concise answers.

### 8. Reflect to build narrative intelligence
Keep a weekly work journal (observations about culture, decisions, people). Turn fragments
into stories with **DIEP** reflection: **Describe** what happened, **Interpret** what it means,
**Evaluate** what went well or badly, **Plan** what you will do next.

## Worked pattern
A food bank's board must decide on a capital campaign. Situation: 20,000 meals a month, at
capacity. Complication: 56 percent of revenue is government funding now at risk, while senior
demand is rising. Resolution: a phased campaign to automate production (2x to 10x capacity)
and add catering revenue. Win theme: "grow the mission by owning our funding". Slides: insight
titles, one capacity chart, a funding mix chart, a three-phase roadmap, risks and mitigations,
the ask: approve a feasibility study by December.

## Common mistakes
- Presenting everything you did instead of what the client must decide.
- Topic titles ("Survey results") instead of insight titles.
- Data without "so what"; recommendations without evidence.
- Ending with "questions?" instead of the decision and next step.
- Asking closed questions; talking more than listening.
