# Case lessons

Generalised lessons from three teaching cases, in our own words. No case solutions
are given. Linked from `ratio-analysis`,
`cash-flow-analysis` and `earnings-quality`.

## A big-box retailer, before and after a turnaround

(A retailer case.) Data across an eight-year gap, a much larger rival's
statements, sales mix, segment results from the earlier year and lease disclosures under the old
and new lease rules.

- **Restate the "then" to match the "now".** Between the two dates the retailer sold its
  credit-card receivables (keeping only a profit-sharing fee) and shut a failed foreign
  expansion. Comparing raw totals mixes a retailer-plus-lender with a pure retailer. Strip the
  unlike segment (its revenue, bad-debt expense, receivables and funding) and the closed
  operation's losses before judging whether retail performance improved.
- **Lease rules changed the balance sheet, not the business.** When operating leases came onto
  the balance sheet, assets and liabilities jumped. Capitalise the earlier years' operating leases
  (present value of the disclosed minimum payments) or compare on a basis that excludes them both
  times.
- **Buybacks flatter ROE.** Retained earnings roughly halved while assets barely moved; ROE rises
  with no change in operations. Read the improvement through RNOA, not ROE.
- **Compare a smaller firm with a giant through ratios and mix.** Different category mix
  (groceries versus apparel and home) explains much of a gross-margin gap; check the sales-mix
  table before calling either firm better run.
- **Watch fiscal calendars.** Retailers' 52/53-week years and reclassified categories break
  year-on-year comparisons; scale flows by 52/53 and use the restated prior-year mix.
- **Working capital tells the strategy.** A fall in days payable or a rise in days inventory
  moves cash by days × cost of sales per day; size it before discussing it.

## Two investment banks in a pandemic year

(A bank case.) Income statements, balance sheet totals, segment results
and league tables for two banks.

- **For a financial firm, financial assets are the operations.** Interest income and trading
  gains stay in operating profit; deposits and borrowings fund the business. Use ROE on common
  equity (after preferred dividends and NCI), by segment, rather than RNOA.
- **Revenue can hold while profit falls.** Expected-loss accounting books credit-loss provisions
  when defaults become likely, not when they happen; in a downturn the provision jumps and
  earnings drop before any loan fails. Separate provisions from revenue when judging the year.
- **Segment ROE finds the engine.** The two banks earn similar group ROEs from different mixes
  (trading and advisory versus wealth and asset management). Fee-based segments are steadier;
  trading segments swing with markets. Weight the steadier earnings higher.
- **Capital actions carry information.** Suspending buybacks to protect liquidity was read by the
  market as bad news. Compare the share price at key dates with the index and the closest peer to
  separate firm news from market-wide fear.
- **A consumer-lending start-up inside a bank** can dilute group returns for years (low segment
  ROE, rising provisions). Ask whether it is investment or a drag.

## A streaming service when growth stalls

(A streaming-video subscription case.) Twelve years of balance sheets, income and
cash flow, content assets and content liabilities, subscriber and market data.

- **Profit and cash can diverge for a decade.** Content spending is capitalised and amortised over
  a few years, so NI rose every year while operating cash flow stayed negative for years. Additions
  to content sit inside operating cash flow; compare NI, CFO and CFO − capex side by side.
- **The key estimate is the amortisation pattern.** Accelerated amortisation based on expected
  viewing is management judgement; a slower pattern raises profit now. Content is tested for
  impairment as a group, so write-downs of individual flops come late or never.
- **The asset should not outgrow revenue for long.** Content assets growing faster than sales
  means each year's spending earns less; watch content assets / revenue and amortisation /
  revenue.
- **Commitments sit off the balance sheet.** Obligations for future titles not yet available (and
  some with unknown fees) are disclosed, not recorded; add them when judging leverage.
- **The price carries growth expectations.** When subscriber growth turned negative, the market
  value fell by about two-thirds in five months. Operating metrics that feed growth (subscribers,
  churn, price per member, password sharing) matter more to value than the year's EPS.
- **Saturation and competition cap growth.** High home penetration and well-funded rivals with
  deep libraries shift the question from growth to monetisation (price tiers, advertising,
  charging for shared accounts).
