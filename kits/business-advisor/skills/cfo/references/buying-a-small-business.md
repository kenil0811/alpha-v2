# Buying a small business (entrepreneurship through acquisition)

> **Area:** `buying-a-small-business` · owned by the CFO skill. **Use when:** Become an owner-operator by buying a profitable small business with your own money and a bank (SBA) loan, roughly $0.5M to $3M EBITDA: decide whether to start or buy, set criteria, run the search funnel, prove add-backs, test debt coverage and plan the handover. Use for self-funded buyers and for owners who want to see how small-business buyers think; for investor-backed search funds, independent sponsors or family offices use small-business-acquisition-eta.

**Outcome:** a well-chosen, fairly priced acquisition of a profitable small company that the buyer can finance, run and grow, instead of building a startup from zero.

## When to use

- "Should I start a company or buy one?"
- A buyer needs acquisition criteria, a sourcing plan or a first view on a target.
- Pricing a small business and structuring the financing (SBA loan, seller note, investor equity).
- An owner nearing retirement wants to see the deal from the buyer's side.

For deeper work use the related skills: `pe-deal-sourcing-screening` (target lists), `private-company-valuation` (adjusted EBITDA and multiples), `lbo-model-returns` (returns model), `buyout-due-diligence` (diligence).

## Method

### 1. Choose the path: start-up or acquisition

| | Start-up | Acquisition |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Must find product-market fit and reach revenue | Buys a working model with customers and cash flow |
| Capital | Little at launch | Much more up front, mostly debt |
| Odds | Most fail; a tiny share reach $10M sales, but upside is unlimited | Far higher survival, lower ceiling |
| Team | Must recruit | Comes with the business |
| Mindset | Innovator | Operator |
| Ownership | Diluted by rounds | Can own a majority or all |

Supply is rising: many owners of small businesses are baby boomers reaching retirement, and most lack a succession plan. Higher interest rates make cash and debt management critical.

Self-check: tolerance for risk and debt, whether you are a natural CEO, people-management skill, need for prestige (are you willing to own a pest-control company?), and willingness to go it alone.

### 2. Choose the financing model

**Self-funded search:** the buyer pays for the search, puts in roughly 10% equity, borrows most of the price through an SBA 7(a) loan with a personal guarantee (loan size capped around $5M), and often adds a seller note. The buyer owns most or all of the company and reports to no investors.

**Traditional search fund:** investors (typically 10 to 15) fund a salary and search costs for up to about two years, then fund the acquisition equity. The searcher earns about 20% to 30% of the company, vesting in tranches (closing, time, return hurdles). Larger targets are possible; investors provide guidance and expect an exit in about 3 to 7 years. Investor capital is commonly preferred stock, often split between a redeemable piece and a participating piece.

Track record: the 2024 Stanford study reports aggregate search-fund IRR around 35% and ROI around 4.5x, with wide dispersion; about a third of searches never acquire, and about 3 in 10 acquisitions lose money. Median search to close is about 19 to 23 months. Self-funded searchers typically end up owning 50% to 70% of a smaller company (2024 study). Full figures: `../cfo/references/small-business-acquisition-eta/search-fund-benchmarks.md`.

### 3. Set acquisition criteria ("a good company in a great industry")

Industry, desirable: fragmented, growing, large with many companies in the target size, simple operations, early or mid life cycle, healthy margins.
Industry, avoid: consolidated, declining, intense competition with low barriers, customers with strong pricing power, exposure to unpredictable outside shocks.

Company, desirable: history of profits and cash flow, recurring or repeat revenue, durable competitive edge, a seller motivated by non-business reasons (retirement), several routes to growth, solid middle management, financeable, reasonable price, realistic exit in 3 to 6 years.
Company, avoid: turnarounds, customer concentration, high churn, no management bench, competitive auctions.

Typical self-funded ETA targets: $1M to $7M revenue, $0.5M to $3M EBITDA, about 15% EBITDA margin or better (to service debt), long profit history, price $1M to $10M. Traditional search funds aim higher. Treat criteria as a framework, not a cage: many of the best deals miss one criterion; know which risk rises and what reward offsets it.

### 4. Run the search funnel

It is a numbers game: contact roughly 1,000 owners, visit about 50, send about 10 letters of intent, diligence 1 to 3, close one. Sources: direct outreach to owners, brokers, industry insiders ("river guides"), investors' networks, professionals (accountants, lawyers). Focus on one or a few industries to build credibility and speed. Proprietary (non-auction) deals usually mean better prices.

### 5. Evaluate in stages

1. **First pass:** is the seller genuine? rough value range; key issues; credible positioning as buyer. Sign an NDA. Rank against criteria.
2. **Valuation and LOI:** get three years of statements and projections; study customers (contracts, concentration, churn, growth). Value with more than one method: buyout model (returns after debt service), public comparables and precedent transactions (discount for small size), DCF rarely, asset value only for asset-heavy firms. Sign a letter of intent with price, structure, key terms and a diligence plan.
3. **Due diligence:** verify financials (quality of earnings), customers, operations, legal, people.

### 6. Normalise earnings (add-backs)

Buyers pay a multiple of adjusted EBITDA, and banks lend on it, so every add-back must be proven.
- Usually fair: owner's personal expenses run through the business, above-market owner pay (replace with a market salary for the new CEO), truly one-time costs.
- Challenge: "one-time" costs that recur or were needed for growth (systems, a new facility), failed projects in a business that must keep launching products.
- Deduct: below-market rent from related parties, thin insurance, deferred maintenance or capex, unfunded benefits.
- Avoid paying for run-rate (annualised recent months); lenders use trailing results.
- Make sure the lender accepts the same add-backs, or equity must fill the gap.

### 7. Structure and stress-test the debt

- Debt service coverage = cash available for debt service ÷ annual principal and interest. Lenders commonly want 1.25x or more (standard practice).
- Model a downside year (revenue down 15% to 20%) and check the business still covers its payments.
- Seller notes and earn-outs keep the seller invested in a smooth transition and bridge price gaps.

### 8. Plan the transition

- Decide how long the seller stays (often 1 to 12 months), in a role the buyer can end.
- First 100 days: learn before changing; communicate on day one to employees, then customers, suppliers and investors; keep key people and customers.
- Set up a board of experienced advisers or investors; meet regularly.
- Then grow: pricing, sales process, add-on acquisitions, margin gains. Small companies bought at lower multiples and grown can sell at higher multiples (multiple expansion).

## Worked pattern

A plumbing services company: revenue $6M, reported EBITDA $950K.
- Add-backs: owner's car and family phone plans $40K; owner salary $350K versus a $150K market CEO salary → +$200K; one-time lawsuit $30K. Deduct below-market rent from the owner's building, $20K. Adjusted EBITDA = $1.2M.
- Price at 4.5x = $5.4M. Self-funded: 10% equity ($540K), 10% seller note ($540K), SBA loan $4.32M at 10% over 10 years → about $703K a year.
- Cash for debt service after capex and taxes ≈ $1.0M → coverage 1.42x. Downside (EBITDA −20%) brings coverage near 1.1x, so negotiate a seller note that is interest-only for two years.
- Criteria check: fragmented industry, recurring service contracts are 35% of revenue, no customer above 5%, retiring seller. Proceed to LOI.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for a perfect target and never closing; or the opposite, overpaying in an auction (winner's curse).
- Accepting the seller's add-backs and run-rate figures without proof.
- Buying a turnaround or a business that depends on the departing owner's relationships.
- Too much debt for the cash flow; no downside case.
- Changing too much in the first months; poor communication with staff and customers.
- Ignoring personal fit: running a small, unglamorous company day to day.
