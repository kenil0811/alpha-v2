# VC deal screening and the investment memo

> **Area:** `vc-deal-screening` · owned by the CFO skill. **Use when:** Screen, diligence and decide on an investment in an early-stage company (as an angel, venture fund or corporate investor) and write the investment memo with a recommended deal. Use when someone is offered a chance to invest in a startup, must compare several pitches, interview founders, or defend a yes or no to an investment committee.

**Outcome:** a fast, disciplined yes, no or "yes on these terms" for each startup reviewed, backed by diligence on the few questions that matter and written up in a one-page memo with a term sheet summary.

## When to use

- An owner, angel or fund is pitched and must decide whether to invest.
- Several startups compete for one budget (rank them, allocate the cheque).
- Preparing questions for a founder meeting.
- Writing or reviewing an investment memo or a recommendation to partners.
- A founder wants to see their company the way investors will (pair with business-plan-and-pitch).

## Method

### 1. First screen (look for a reason to say no)

Investors see far more deals than they fund (one VC's analysis of 200+ pitches: about 2% got a term sheet). Pass quickly if:
- The sector or stage does not fit the fund's thesis or expertise (fit matters a lot).
- The deal is far too small or too large for the fund.
- Management is clearly unsuited to the task.
- The plan assumes away competitors or difficulties.
- The market cannot be verified, or the idea is "too secret" to explain.
- Valuation or terms are unrealistic (an order of magnitude off).
- The math cannot return the fund even if things go well (see startup-valuation-and-deal-math).
- The capital need is too large for the fund to keep diversified, or the venture is a proven, low-risk business that a lender should fund instead.
- The deck leans on stock lines: "conservative" projections, an analyst's giant market figure, a partner "about to sign", hires who join "once funded", "no competition", a request to sign a non-disclosure agreement, patents as the only moat, "just 1% of the market". Each is a prompt to probe, and several together suggest a founder who does not know the industry (list in `ceo/references/business-plan-and-pitch.md`).

### 2. Score the core dimensions

Use the five Ts, each scored 1 to 5:
- **Team:** right people to build fast? relevant experience, founder-market fit, gaps and plans to fill them, advisors and board, coachability, staying power to an exit, any legal issues. Would you work with them for ten years?
- **TAM (market):** real demand? size and growth, built bottom-up; competition; barriers to entry; TAM, SAM, SOM credibility.
- **Tech (product and moat):** why customers choose this over substitutes; defensibility (IP, data, network effects, switching costs); scalability; capital intensity; product-market fit.
- **Traction:** paying customers, growth rate, conversion, revenue (ARR or MRR), retention, milestones, approvals.
- **Timing:** why now? regulatory or behavioural shifts, needed infrastructure in place, too early versus too late.

What predicts a yes: one VC's regression of his own decisions weighted team and market about equally and highest, traction next, product least. Strong teams in big markets get funded even before product; weak traction undermines even a good demo.

Reading the team the way an experienced plan reader does: what do they know (the product, the process, the market), whom do they know (suppliers, customers, hires), and how well are they known (reputation lowers the risk others see in dealing with a start-up). Have they worked together, not just studied together? A strong test: can the founder recruit good people before the money arrives?

Two quick pictures to ask for or sketch: the cash hole (how deep, how long until cash turns positive, how steep the payoff) and the spread of outcomes (chance of total loss, modest return, big hit). Discount an optimist's shallow hole; a plan showing almost no chance of loss is itself a warning.

Cross-check against the common list of startup risks: market size, business model, adoption, timing, execution, technology, capital structure, platform dependence, management, financial, legal, and fit with the founder.

### 3. Plan the diligence

Diligence confirms facts and finds what the deck does not say. Sources: customers and references, industry experts, consultants, market data (private-company databases for comparables, competitors, funding history and growth signals), company visits (look for clear objectives, urgency, morale). Present adverse findings to the founders for rebuttal.

Assign each team member a theme (team, market, traction, product, competition, terms) and write the questions in advance. Prioritise the questions that would change your decision. A 5 M's checklist, diligence habits, fund mechanics and exit paths are in `cfo/references/vc-deal-screening/diligence-and-exits.md`; dated survey benchmarks on how VCs decide and how funds earn are in `cfo/references/vc-deal-screening/vc-decision-benchmarks.md`.

### 4. Interview the founders

Lead the meeting, build rapport, and dig into the weak spots. Question bank by theme (common patterns):
- **Market:** how was SOM computed? what is the wedge segment? why the recent change of target customer? is demand big enough for the claimed SOM?
- **Traction:** customers to date, CAC, LTV, churn, this year's revenue and next year's plan; is a named partner a customer, an investor, or both (conflict)?
- **Unit economics:** why will customers pay your price when market prices are lower? how does your fee compare with industry norms?
- **Deal terms:** is the valuation pre- or post-money? how many months of runway does this buy? what is the cap table today?
- **Use of funds:** is the budget realistic for what must be built and sold?
- **Product:** how does it actually work and integrate? how accurate is it? how much manual work hides behind the software?
- **Team:** who fills technical gaps? advisors? commitment and location?
- **Regulation and exit:** policy risks; who buys the company, or is an IPO plausible?

### 5. Decide with numbers and probabilities

1. Value the company and size the cheque with the VC method and reverse round analysis.
2. Model ownership through future rounds, follow-on reserves, MOIC, IRR and fund return in base, downside and upside cases.
3. Put probabilities on key risks per stage; multiply to see the real odds; weight outcomes.
4. Guard against bias: confirmation (falling in love), anchoring on past failures, pattern-matching to a famous success. Assign a devil's advocate. Also check the opposite error: passing because the founder is unknown can miss a team that will change (one well-known fund passed on a payments start-up's series A because it could not judge the first CEO; the better-known co-founder took over months later).
5. Remember the asymmetry: a portfolio investor can rationally prefer riskier swings than the founder should. Choose terms that keep both sides wanting the same outcome, or expect conflict when the plan slips.
6. Decide: invest, invest on changed terms, or pass with reasons.

### 6. Write the memo (one page plus attachments)

**Deal snapshot:** pre-money, your investment, syndicate investment, total round, post-money, your ownership, option pool; headline MOIC, IRR, fund-return share.

Then:
- Top 5 reasons to invest and top 5 reservations (each one sentence with evidence).
- Overall decision in two or three sentences.
- Valuation rationale (method, comparables, multiple, exit).
- Expected return (cases, dilution, follow-on).
- Diligence done and what remains before closing.
- For deals passed on: top reasons not to invest, strengths noted, one-line explanation.

**Term sheet summary (one page):** pre-money, investment, option pool, board structure, closing conditions, liquidation preference, dividends, anti-dilution, key dates, other key terms (see raising-startup-capital). Choose terms that fit the risks found, not a boilerplate list.

Angel version (for smaller cheques): rate pain, solution, market potential, competitive position, forecast believability and founders 1 to 5; list three likes and three dislikes; state IRR used and why, terminal value, three comparables and the multiple, post-money, cheque and stake.

### 7. Defend it to the committee

Partners judge: diligence quality (key issues covered, understanding shown, meeting controlled), written deliverables (decision, pros and cons, appropriate terms, realistic exit analysis, path to exit), and the partner meeting (VC knowledge, explaining the deal, handling pushback, conviction, everyone contributing).

### 8. After investing

Monitor through the board and committees, regular financials, involvement in key hires, further financing and M&A. Management usually fails by not seeing or admitting a problem, being unable to fix it, failing to raise capital, or losing energy. Plan liquidity: IPO, sale to a strategic buyer, or asset sale.

## Worked pattern

A $40M seed fund sees an AI scheduling tool for clinics: $3M raise on a $15M post-money.
- Screen: fits thesis (vertical software); deal size fits ($1.5M cheque = 3.75% of fund).
- Scores: team 4 (ex-clinic operator plus engineer), market 4, tech 3, traction 3 ($400K ARR, 8% monthly growth), timing 4.
- Math: $1.5M for 10%; expect 40% later dilution → 6% at exit. To return the fund needs a $667M exit; base case $300M exit in 7 years → $18M, 12x MOIC, IRR about 43%. Probabilities: 55% early success × 40% scale × 50% strong exit ≈ 11% for the base case, which alone contributes about 1.3x (0.11 × 12x), so the case rests on the upside tail. Decision: invest $1.5M if the round adds a 1x non-participating preference, a board observer seat and pro-rata rights; key diligence before closing is customer churn by clinic size.

## Common mistakes

- Investing outside the fund's thesis or in a deal that cannot return the fund.
- Accepting the founder's TAM and projections without bottom-up checks.
- Counting surveys and social-media numbers as traction.
- Interviews that let the founder pitch rather than probing weak points.
- Ignoring dilution and follow-on reserves in return math.
- Memo reasons with no evidence; boilerplate terms unrelated to the risks found.
- Confirmation bias; skipping the devil's advocate.
