# Deal process, structure and terms

> **Area:** `deal-process-and-terms` · owned by the CFO skill. **Use when:** Run an acquisition from teaser to closing and structure its terms (price adjustments, seller notes, earn-outs, escrow, rollover, reps, indemnities, asset vs stock). Use when drafting or reading an NDA, IOI, LOI or purchase agreement, or when bridging a price gap with a seller.

**Outcome:** a deal that moves through each stage on time, with documents that fix the price, allocate risk and keep the seller motivated to close.

## When to use

- A teaser or CIM arrives and the next steps must be planned.
- Writing an IOI or LOI.
- Buyer and seller disagree on price or on risk.
- Reviewing a purchase agreement, disclosure schedules or closing checklist.

## Method

### 1. The sequence
The steps are the same in most deals; the timing varies widely (a search-fund deal often takes 4 to 12 months from first contact to close).
1. **Seller preparation** (weeks): buyer list, teaser, CIM, model, valuation view, data room.
2. **Teaser:** 1 to 2 anonymous pages; business, highlights, summary financials; region not city; little on management.
3. **NDA:** standard and short for widely shopped deals (negotiate the term); for fundless sponsors, a non-circumvention clause is added and fought over; for proprietary deals either side may draft it.
4. **CIM:** executive summary and desired transaction; history, markets, products, customers, suppliers, operations, facilities; industry and competition; management and organisation; historical and projected financials with proposed EBITDA adjustments.
5. **IOI** (non-binding, 1 to 2 pages): price range in dollars or as a multiple of EBITDA; sources of financing; key diligence items and timeline; proposed structure (asset vs stock, seller note, rollover, earn-out); management plans; time to close. Based on the CIM and maybe a call. Lenders give an early leverage read before IOIs.
6. **Data room and management meetings** for the short-listed: request missing items, send questions, tour the site, and (often) a dinner to test personal fit. Sellers sell; buyers confirm findings and position themselves as the best owner.
7. **LOI** (3 to 15 pages): firm price; detailed structure and capital stack; exclusivity (no-shop) period, which is binding; closing date and conditions; management pay and retention; remaining diligence; approvals. Lenders give term sheets.
8. **Confirmatory diligence and QofE** during exclusivity.
9. **Definitive documents** and signing, with financing commitments signed at the same time.
10. **Closing:** debt and equity fund the new company together.

After the LOI the seller's leverage falls; the LOI is a roadmap, not the deal. Hundreds of pages of definitive documents follow. The deal lead usually project-manages the closing checklist (every document and task, who owns it, status); lawyers draft.

Recent process variations: sellers commissioning their own QofE; packed management meetings with less social time; sellers keeping a second bidder in documentation as a back-up; rep and warranty insurance replacing escrow.

### 2. Definitive documents
- Purchase agreement (stock or asset) with disclosure schedules and exhibits.
- Secretary's certificates (organising documents, authorising resolutions); board, shareholder and third-party consents.
- Ancillary agreements: non-compete, non-solicit, employment, licences, transition services, stockholder agreement.
- Legal opinions.
- New entity documents: operating agreement or charter and bylaws (ownership, board, voting, cash waterfall and preferences, investor protections).
Read the defined terms first; capitalised words mean exactly what the definitions say.

### 3. Price and consideration
- **Cash** is most common and keeps sellers motivated.
- **Stock** is rare in PE except in roll-ups of small owners; management equity is set separately.
- **Seller note:** deeply subordinated debt from the seller bridges the gap between agreed value and what lenders will fund.
- **Earn-out / holdback:** deferred, performance-contingent payment bridges disagreement about the future. Earn-outs breed disputes; define the metric (and its EBITDA definition) tightly.
- **Rollover equity:** management or the owner reinvests part of proceeds, aligning incentives. Managers without a stake get options or phantom equity.
- **Escrow:** part of the price held (often 9 to 18 months; standard practice) to pay claims under the reps.
- **Working-capital peg:** price assumes normal working capital, usually the LTM average. Sellers could otherwise cut inventory, collect receivables and stretch payables before closing. After closing the price moves up or down by the surplus or deficit; true-up usually within about 90 days. Define working capital precisely (exclude bad receivables, obsolete stock).
- **Cash-free, debt-free:** seller keeps cash and repays debt; EV is the headline price.

### 4. Asset vs stock purchase
- **Stock:** seller gets capital-gains treatment; employment, contracts, licences and permits carry over more easily; buyer inherits all liabilities.
- **Asset:** price is allocated to assets; buyer steps up tax basis and re-depreciates; goodwill amortised for tax over 15 years; unlisted (contingent) liabilities stay behind; minority holders are easier to drag along.
- **338(h)(10) election:** legally a stock sale, taxed as an asset sale (where eligible).
- Entity choice: C-corp pays tax; S-corp, partnership and LLC pass income through. Funds often use a C-corp holding company to shield tax-exempt investors from unrelated business taxable income.

### 5. Risk allocation
- **Covenants:** promises to act or not act. Interim operating covenants keep the business "ordinary course" between signing and closing (needed when approvals, consents or financing delay closing).
- **Representations and warranties:** seller statements of fact; if false, seller is liable. Narrowed by materiality, knowledge ("to seller's knowledge", define whose knowledge after what inquiry), range (e.g. only data-room documents) and time (as of a date).
- **Disclosure schedules:** list the exceptions to each rep; reading them against the reps is a top source of diligence.
- **Indemnification:** seller pays losses from breaches. Claims must clear a threshold (basket) and are capped; fraud, tax, title, authority and some litigation are often carved out of the cap. Usually the buyer's only remedy; buyer must mitigate.
- **Rep and warranty insurance:** often a cost-effective substitute for a large escrow.

### 6. Ownership after close
One fund usually takes a supermajority (around 80%+); sometimes 2 to 3 firms co-invest; growth equity takes minorities. Continuing managers roll some proceeds.

## Worked pattern

Seller wants $24M for a business with $4M EBITDA; buyer's model supports about $21M in cash and lenders will fund $11M.
- LOI at $24M headline: $20.5M cash at close, $1.5M seller note (4 years, 7%, subordinated, interest only for 2 years), $2.0M rollover into the new company alongside the sponsor.
- $2.0M of the cash held in escrow for 12 months for rep claims; title, authority and tax claims outside the cap.
- Working-capital peg $3.2M (LTM average); true-up within 90 days.
- Sources: revolver $2.0M, term loan $9.0M, sponsor equity $10.3M, seller note $1.5M, rollover $2.0M = $24.8M. Uses: purchase $24.0M, fees $0.8M.
- Seller gets the headline; buyer pays $20.5M in cash up front and keeps the founder invested.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the IOI or LOI as the deal; key terms left vague come back as fights.
- A loose working-capital definition.
- Earn-outs tied to a metric the buyer controls and the seller can dispute.
- Accepting a huge disclosure schedule the night before closing without re-reading it against the reps.
- Letting exclusivity run out without a plan.
- Overlooking change-of-control consents in key contracts.
