# Thesis and sourcing playbook

Depth for `SKILL.md` steps 1, 4 and 5. Figures are from practitioner decks; their year is given.

## 1. Know your own box first
Size tiers used by practitioners (2026 deck; fund-size tiers from a 2026 lower-middle-market firm):

| Tier | Fund size | Target EV | Target EBITDA | Typical seller |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Micro | under $30M | under $15M | under $2M | founder |
| Lower middle market | $50M to $350M | $15M to $100M | $2M to $15M | founder, first institutional capital |
| Middle market | $350M to $1B | $100M to $500M | $15M to $50M | founder or sponsor (secondary) |
| Upper / mega | over $1B | over $500M | over $50M | sponsors, public companies |

Equity cheque math: equity is usually 30% to 50% of deal value, depending on lenders. Many fund
agreements cap one platform at 20% to 25% of the fund, with reserves kept for add-ons. Example: $17M of
EBITDA at 8x is a $136M deal; at 40% equity that is about $54M, which needs a fund of at least about $220M.

Write the criteria down: size (revenue, EBITDA, margin), sectors and geography, control vs minority, seller
profile (founder-owned, owner wants a partner, will roll equity, cares about legacy and culture), and
business traits (critical product or service, strong customer relationships, positive free cash flow).

## 2. Thesis lifecycle and budget
Stages: Developing, Active, Sponsored, Paused, Retired. Budget each stage so ideas die cheaply:

| Stage | Entry test | Time | Cash |
|---|---|---|---|
| Developing | 2-page proposal with 3 to 5 thesis points, a test plan for each, and what must be true | under 100 hours | under $5k |
| Active | approved by the investment team after testing and a fuller (about 20-page) thesis deck | 100 to 250 hours | under $10k |
| Sponsored | stricter bar (see SKILL.md) and a formal budget | 250+ hours | $10k to $50k |
| Paused | lead stops, or not approved but not abandoned | none | none |
| Retired | deal done, no deal, or rejected | none | none |

Build the budget bottom up: hours to draft the two-page external summary, banker calls (number of
bankers x calls a year x half an hour), operator e-mails (count x minutes each, plus follow-ups),
city-trip days (travel cost per day), conferences, expert calls for any deal that reaches deep analysis.
One sample came to about 145 hours (3 hours a week) and about $11k for a year.

Typical two-year arc of a thesis: establish the theme and buy-in (quarter 1); validate the market with
bankers, 10+ industry calls and M&A and valuation data (quarters 1 to 2); build a market map and target
list, attend conferences, benchmark industry KPIs (quarters 2 to 4); tier targets and recruit industry
advisers; then run outreach and a recurring meeting cadence until a deal closes (year 2).

## 3. Is the niche worth a buy-and-build? Three indices
From a market map of the niche (2026 deck):

| Index | Measures | Low | Medium | High |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fragmentation | potential targets in the market map | under 30 | 30 to 70 | over 70 |
| M&A activity | relevant acquisitions in the last 3 years | under 10 | 10 to 15 | over 15 |
| Consolidators | active platform-level acquirers | under 3 | 3 to 6 | over 6 |

High fragmentation with medium M&A and medium consolidators is the sweet spot: plenty of targets, proven
deal relevance, and a consolidation cycle that is not yet crowded.

## 4. First-pass grid for any market or company
| Factor | Lower value | Higher value |
|---|---|---|
| Addressable market | under $2B | very large |
| Fragmentation | under 1,000 companies | over 10,000 |
| Cyclicality | high | low |
| Urgency of the product | deferrable | essential or compliance-driven |
| Key assets | fixed, balance-sheet heavy | people and technology |
| PE activity in the niche | mature | early |
| EBITDA margin | under 10% | over 20% |
| Revenue growth | low | high |
| Technology disruption risk | high | low |
| Barriers to entry | low | high |
| Industry tailwinds | weak | strong |
| Exit options | few natural buyers | several credible paths |

Then write a brief: three pros and three cons (for example recurring compliance revenue, regional share
leader, 25%+ margins vs broad auction, weather-driven seasonality, hiring risk in expansion).

## 5. Intermediary coverage
Types (2019 practitioner view):
- Large banks: professional, broad auctions, frequent and well-fitting deals, full price.
- Regional or boutique banks: sector or regional focus, limited processes, medium frequency and fit.
- Business brokers: little information, sometimes just a referral for a success fee; low fit, little process.
The intermediary's job is the highest price; yours is to be known early and to have an angle (sector
expertise, executive advisers, a clear thesis, a distinctive way of working). Price is not the only thing
that wins.

Tiering (2026 deck): spend about 80% of time on tiers 1 to 2 and 20% on the rest.
1. Boutique banks best aligned with your criteria and running processes you can win (100 to 200 firms);
   recurring calendar meetings.
2. Qualified brokers and boutiques that bring the occasional platform-size deal, mostly add-ons.
3. Centres of influence: law, accounting and wealth firms serving owners; slow but can introduce owners.
4. Larger banks working above your size: market intelligence, executives, valuation views, later exit help.
5. The long tail: e-mail updates until they prove themselves.
Score each source on deals sent (deal score) and touchpoints (connectivity) and promote or demote.

## 6. Direct outreach campaign (nine steps)
1. Kick-off: agree criteria and strategy (with portfolio management if it is an add-on search).
2. Research and tier targets with a data tool (company finder, contact enrichment); rank A (platform-size,
   best fit), B (platform-size or small but strong fit), C (add-on size or tangential).
3. Outreach plan across e-mail, phone, LinkedIn, letters; personalised with company-specific insight.
4. Launch; sequence touches; watch intent signals (opens, replies).
5. Convert interest into a qualification call (headcount, financials).
6. Hand aligned targets to the deal team for a management call.
7. Weekly or bi-weekly pipeline review.
8. Report conversion by stage.
9. Keep re-engaging those who said "not now".
In-person contact stands out as automated outreach floods inboxes. One conference campaign (2026) on
about 70 A and B targets saw about 39% open rates and meetings with roughly 8% to 10% of core targets.

## 7. Funnel and KPIs
Inputs: deal-source meetings, conferences, city trips, campaigns. Funnel: opportunities received, detailed
reviews, IOIs, LOIs, add-ons closed, platforms closed. Track per channel: opportunities per unique source,
proprietary deals and discussions, share of opportunities in limited processes. A firm may see 600 to 700
deals a year to close a few platforms and add-ons; that is a good year. Pass fast with a stated reason
(industry, size, margin, cyclicality, concentration) and tell the source promptly: quick feedback keeps
sources sending deals.

## 8. Why proactive and niche sourcing pays (evidence, with dates)
- Market context (Bain, 2026 report): about $1.3T of buyout dry powder, about 32,000 unsold PE-backed
  companies, average holds near 7 years. Over 80% of available buyout capital sits in funds above $1B,
  while companies with $10M to $250M of revenue are over 90% of targets.
- Niche specialists (Mantra index cited 2026): about 2.4x MOIC and 34% net IRR vs 1.7x and 16% for a broad
  benchmark, with faster exits (4.0 vs 4.9 years).
- Specialists beat generalists inside their sector (Cambridge Associates, 2014): about 2.2x vs 1.9x MOIC.
- Smaller deals buy cheaper and leave room to grow into a higher multiple, but a $25M deal takes as much
  work to close as a $250M one and caps fund size.
- A growth firm reported about 10,000 companies called, 750 visited, 12 deals closed (2004), with nearly 90%
  of investments starting from a cold call.
