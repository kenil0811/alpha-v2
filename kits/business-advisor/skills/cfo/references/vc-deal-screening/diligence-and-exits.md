# Diligence checklist, fund mechanics and exit paths

From VC practice (2019-2020). Complements SKILL.md sections 3
and 8.

## Five things to diligence (the "5 M's")
| M | Ask |
|---|---|
| Market | Size, growth, timing; who pays and why now |
| Management | Track record, completeness, coachability, integrity |
| Method | Product, technology, business model, defensibility |
| Money | How much is needed to reach breakeven or the next value step; who else invests |
| Metrics | Traction and unit economics that prove the model |

A shorthand from one investor: quality of team + idea + strategy + market, minus problems squared. One big
problem outweighs several strengths.

## Habits that prevent expensive mistakes
- Always run background checks on founders, however good the references.
- Check that every employee and founder has signed an NDA, an invention-assignment agreement and (where
  enforceable) a non-compete, with proper consideration; without them the company may not own its IP.
- Fund the company all the way to breakeven or a clear value milestone, and syndicate with other investors
  so the money is there. Milestones both raise value and lower risk; tie rounds to them.
- Use a written diligence checklist so nothing is missed under deal pressure.
- Keep an "anti-portfolio": record deals you passed on that later succeeded, and what you got wrong.

## Fund mechanics a screen must respect
- **Waterfall:** limited partners get their capital back, then a preferred return, then the general partner
  catches up, then profits split 80/20.
- **Where deals come from:** referrals from lawyers, accountants, other investors and portfolio founders.
  Funds compete for deals on brand, network, sector specialisation and platform services (recruiting,
  marketing, events offered across the portfolio).
- **Fund strategy must hang together:** business model, investment thesis, team and fundraising have to fit
  (a fund for a region should be based there; a fund for under-served founders should reflect them on its
  team). Models seen: very large funds that can force their way into deals at high prices; micro funds
  (under about $50M, seed, focused, but short of follow-on money); data-driven selection (unproven);
  platform funds; peer-selected funds where founders choose each other.

## Exit paths
Investors need liquidity in about 4 to 7 years.
| Path | Suits |
|---|---|
| IPO | Large, fast-growing, predictable revenue; strong management; a story public investors can follow |
| Sale to a strategic buyer | Product fills a gap for a bigger company; synergies; smaller scale |
| Sale to a buyout fund | Cash-generating, slower-growth companies; often an add-on to a fund's existing company |
| SPAC merger or direct listing | Faster or cheaper listing; more price risk |
| Secondary sale of shares | Early investors or founders take partial liquidity before a full exit |
- Sales to buyout funds have grown from a few percent of VC-backed exits (2000) to about a fifth (2019);
  more than half of those are add-ons.
- A very large exit needs a huge market, a company that leads it, and capital to keep scaling; plan the
  exit thesis at entry.
- Earlier exits can give higher IRRs, and sometimes higher multiples, than holding for the biggest outcome.
