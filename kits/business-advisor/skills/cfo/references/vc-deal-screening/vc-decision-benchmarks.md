# How VCs decide, and how their funds earn: benchmarks

Two dated published sources (plus older base rates below): a 2016 survey of about 900 US-heavy institutional VCs, and a 2012
review by a large foundation of its 20 years in about 100 VC funds. Use them to calibrate a screen, to brief
a founder on what investors look for, or to judge a fund as a limited partner. Numbers are from those dates;
check current data before quoting them.

## The deal funnel (2016 survey, per closed deal)
| Stage | Roughly |
|---|---|
| Opportunities considered | 100 |
| Meet management | 25 |
| Reviewed at partners' meeting | 8 |
| Due diligence | 4 to 5 |
| Term sheet offered | 1.5 to 2 (about 60% of offers close) |
| Closed | 1 |
- Median firm closes about 4 deals a year. Average deal: 83 days to close, about 120 hours of diligence, about 10 reference calls (early-stage fewer, late-stage more).
- Sources of deals: about 30% professional networks, 20% other investors, 8% portfolio companies, 30% self-generated, only about 10% cold inbound. Implication for founders: get a warm introduction.

## What drives the decision
- **Team** is the most-cited factor (95% call it important; 47% the most important). Business model, product and market follow. Valuation is ranked fifth overall but matters more in later stages. Healthcare investors weight the business more than the team.
- Team is also the top cited reason for both successes and failures; timing, luck, technology, model and industry each matter about equally after that.
- Deal selection is seen as the largest source of returns, then post-investment help, then deal flow.

## How they value and structure
- Few use DCF; most use cash-on-cash multiple, then IRR. About 1 in 5 early-stage VCs forecast no cash flows at all.
- Required returns: average IRR about 31% (early-stage and small funds higher); average required multiple about 5.5x (early stage about 7.5x, late stage about 3x). Most adjust targets for risk; about half for time to exit.
- Least negotiable terms for VCs: pro-rata rights, liquidation preference, anti-dilution, vesting, valuation, board control. Most negotiable: option pool, participation, amount, redemption, dividends. Founders should spend negotiating capital accordingly.
- Post-investment help most offered: strategic guidance, introductions to investors and customers, operational advice, hiring.
- Outcomes: about three-quarters of successful exits are sales, not IPOs; roughly a quarter of investments lose money and under 10% return 10x or more.

## Fund economics seen from the LP side (2012 review)
- Standard terms: 2% annual management fee on committed capital plus 20% of profits ("2 and 20"); general partners typically commit only about 1% of the fund. Fees reward raising bigger funds regardless of results.
- In that portfolio, about 6 in 10 funds failed to beat a public small-cap index after fees and carry; only 1 in 5 beat it by more than 3% a year; only 4 of 30 funds above $400M beat small-cap stocks.
- The expected early dip in returns (the "J-curve") was often absent: many funds showed peak interim returns early, around when the managers raised their next fund.
- Funds meant to last ten years often ran 12 to 15.
- Their remedies: judge funds by a public market equivalent (PME: invest the fund's cash flows in a comparable index and compare), not by IRR, quartile or vintage claims; pay fees from a budget, and carry only after capital plus a preferred return; ask for the manager's economics; prefer smaller funds with consistent PME and at least 5% manager commitment.

## Older base rates

Dated figures, useful as orders of magnitude:
- **Who gets venture money:** in one strong year (1987) VCs financed about 1,700 companies against about
  631,000 new incorporations. Around 2010, VC-type start-ups were under 1% of new US firms; angel and VC
  markets were each roughly $20B a year. Of about 26M US firms, about 20M had no employees.
- **How new firms are funded:** a 2004 survey put the average new venture's money at about half from the
  owner and internal sources, under a tenth from outside equity, and about two-fifths from outside debt.
  Of 100 fast-growing private companies studied around 1989, over 80% started on founders' savings,
  credit cards or mortgages; median start-up capital was about $10,000; under a fifth later raised equity.
- **Cheque sizes:** VC rounds typically $1M to $10M; individual angels often $25K to $50K; angel-group
  rounds often gather 15 to 20 angels. Roughly 1% of plans seen get funded.
- **Portfolio skew:** one 1980s study found about 7% of investments made over 60% of profits and a third
  lost money in part or whole; an analysis of 468 deals (1990 to 2006) found about 60% of dollars returned
  under cost (under 4% of returns) and about 8% of dollars made over 70% of returns. VCs expect at least
  half of deals to fail.
- **Survival:** one study found fewer than 15% of venture-backed firms still operating three years after
  first funding. Successful fast-growers redirected strategy about five times before finding their path.
- **What a VC needs to see:** potential for about $50M of revenue within five to seven years; home runs
  that return 10x to 25x.

## Using these in a screen
- Check the deal can return the fund (see `startup-valuation-and-deal-math`), then test team first.
- Expect founders to concede little on control and downside terms; trade on pool size, participation and dividends.
- When evaluating a fund to invest in, ask for net PME, the manager's commitment, fund life to date, and fee terms.
