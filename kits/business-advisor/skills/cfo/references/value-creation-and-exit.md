# Value creation, the 100-day plan and exit

> **Area:** `value-creation-and-exit` · owned by the CFO skill. **Use when:** Plan and run value creation after an acquisition (100-day plan, growth, cost, add-ons, deleveraging) and prepare and choose the exit. Use right before and after closing a deal, when reviewing a held company's progress, or 18 months before a planned sale or recapitalisation.

**Outcome:** a value-creation plan tied to the return model, a 100-day plan ready at closing, and an exit route chosen and prepared well before the sale.

## When to use

- "We just bought it. What do we do first?"
- "Our returns are behind plan. Which levers are left?"
- "Should we sell, recap, or hold longer?"
- "How do we get ready for a buyer's diligence?"

## Method

### 1. Map every initiative to a return lever

Depth: `cfo/references/value-creation-and-exit/add-on-roll-up-math.md` (buy-and-build value decomposition, acquisition engine, roll-up operating structure, integration checklist) and `cfo/references/value-creation-and-exit/exit-route-evidence.md` (strategic vs financial buyer, writing the exit backwards, dated research on routes and timing).
Equity value grows in three ways (one operating partner's frame):
- **EBITDA growth:** revenue (pricing, cross-selling, new channels, new products and markets, add-ons) and margin (sourcing, cost control, operating leverage).
- **Multiple expansion:** brand, proprietary IP or share, scale, professional management, buying small and selling as a bigger platform (multiple arbitrage).
- **Deleveraging:** cash flow repays debt.
Add finance levers: capital structure, lower capital intensity (working capital, capex). With prices high, most firms now prefer growth over pure cost-cutting; the value you create during the hold is the part you control.

### 2. Write the 100-day plan before closing
The first 100 days are the high-impact window. A strong plan (BCG's frame):
1. Sets a clear strategic direction.
2. Builds the organisation to deliver it (key hires, roles, incentives).
3. Lists initiatives for short-term cash generation.
4. Ranks operational improvements by value and ease.
5. Lays out an implementation roadmap with owners and dates.
Feed it with diligence findings (deferred capex, pricing gaps, weak systems).

### 3. Choose how involved to be: TOP SCAN
The investor's possible contributions:
- **T**eam building: design and recruit the human capital.
- **O**perations: admin, accounting, legal, technology.
- **P**erspective: strategy, positioning, target market, product scope.
- **S**kill building, especially for senior management.
- **C**ustomer development: finding and opening doors to customers.
- **A**nalysis: how performance is measured and reported.
- **N**etwork: introductions to investors, acquirers, hires, partners (cheapest and often most valuable).
Investor resources: cash, brand, industry network, funding network, in-house experts. Styles: financier (structure and monitoring, board and reporting templates), mentor (coaching on request, introductions), operating partner (proactive staff acting as an extension of the company: recruiting, marketing, technology, business development).

### 4. Proven value-creation plays
- **Pricing:** act soon after closing and regularly after (one vet roll-up raised prices about 30 days after each acquisition).
- **Leveraged sourcing:** pool spend across portfolio companies in freight, packaging, insurance, printing, IT, professional services, commodities. Example: 7.5% saved on $600M of spend = $45M of EBITDA, worth about 8x that in value.
- **Buy-and-build:** buy a platform, then add-ons at lower multiples; integrate; exit as a larger, higher-multiple business. Over 70% of PE deals are add-ons. Build a proprietary acquisition engine (referral agreements, direct outreach); proprietary sources convert to LOIs best.
- **Organic growth engines:** central demand generation, digital marketing and call centres shared across companies; data and analytics to target customers and price.
- **Brand building:** buy under-valued brands and monetise them.
- **People:** retention (pay, benefits, training) is a lever when the value walks out the door each night.
- **Dividend recap:** as debt falls and EBITDA grows, re-borrow to return capital early (lifts IRR; adds risk).

### 5. Prepare the exit 18 months out
Readiness scan: early enough to fix weaknesses, late enough to read the market. Ask:
- Is the timing right? Is the route optimal?
- Which milestones must be hit first?
- What value-creation options remain for the next owner, and who will run them?
Set up an exit committee (fund, company, or both).
Then:
- **Keep creating value** through the sale; leave some well-evidenced opportunities on the table for the buyer, and be ready to explain why they were not done.
- **Make bold long-term moves:** new products, segments, channels, pricing, cross-selling, vendor renegotiation, add-ons still in reach.
- **Think like the buyer:** expect their diligence questions; disclose problems early (they will find them; hidden problems poison deals); have deep answers ready. Consider a seller QofE.
- **Tell one clear equity story:** rock-solid value proposition and results, tangible future options, unique assets. Back it with pilots, trials, surveys, A/B tests and past wins ("we did X in market A; Y is the same play in market B"). Watch for events outside your control that could undercut the story mid-process.

### 6. Pick the exit route
| Route | Fits when |
|---|---|
| IPO, direct listing, SPAC | durable competitive edge vs larger rivals; established, fast, predictable growth; a product platform, not one hit; market leadership |
| Strategic sale | critical mass reached and model de-risked; defensible IP or first-mover edge; a large player can scale revenue through its distribution or broader portfolio |
| Financial sale (secondary buyout) | established, growing EBITDA with low execution risk; room for more operating and financial leverage; platform for further add-ons; growth limited mainly by capital. Now often over half of exits by count |
| Dividend recap | keep holding but return capital |
| Continuation vehicle, long-hold fund | best value comes from holding longer |
| ESOP | owner wants employees to own it; tax benefits; often wrongly seen as too complex |
| Minority recap, dual track, sell in parts | partial liquidity; price tension between IPO and sale; units worth more apart |

Exit choice depends on company traits and on debt and equity market windows; sellers under fund pressure exit sooner and at lower multiples. Over-long holds raise pressure: recently about 40% of PE companies were held more than 5 years.

## Worked pattern

Platform: $5M EBITDA, bought at 7x ($35M) with 4x debt ($20M).
- 100-day plan: price increase of 3% (adds $0.6M EBITDA on $20M revenue at full flow-through), group purchasing on $8M of spend at 6% ($0.48M), CFO hire, monthly KPI pack.
- Years 1 to 3: three add-ons with combined $3M EBITDA bought at 5x ($15M: $10M delayed-draw debt, $5M new sponsor equity).
- Year 5: EBITDA $11M with organic growth; debt $22M (peak $30M, less $8M repaid from cash flow). Readiness scan at month 42 picks a sale to a larger sponsor, who values scale at 8.5x: EV $93.5M, equity $71.5M.
- Equity invested $15M plus $5M for add-ons = $20M; MOIC 3.6x.

## Common mistakes

- A 100-day plan written after closing, or a list of ideas with no owners.
- Counting on multiple expansion instead of earning it with growth and scale.
- Ignoring exit readiness until the banker is hired.
- Pulling every lever before sale and leaving the buyer no story.
- Hiding problems from buyers.
- Over-relying on research-contested claims: evidence that PE managers drive operating gains is mixed; some PE outperformance may be small-company and value premiums plus leverage. Prove your own value-add with data.
