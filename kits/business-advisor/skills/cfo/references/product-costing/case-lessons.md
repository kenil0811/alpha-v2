# Product costing: lessons from teaching cases

Part of `cfo/references/product-costing.md`. Generalised lessons from cost-system cases.
No case answers are given.

## One overhead rate in a business that automated (an imaging-centre case)

- **Symptom:** the overhead rate per direct-labour dollar climbs year after year (from under
  100% to well over 100%) while managers say they are cutting costs. That is the signal that
  labour no longer drives overhead: equipment does.
- **Effect on prices:** with full-cost pricing, labour-heavy, low-tech services are charged
  overhead they do not use. Their prices look uncompetitive, customers move them elsewhere,
  volume falls, and the rate climbs again (a death spiral). Equipment-heavy services are
  under-costed at the same time.
- **Fix in steps, and see how far each step moves the costs:**
  1. Split overhead into labour-related and equipment-related pools; charge equipment overhead
     by machine time.
  2. If machines differ a lot in cost and upkeep, split equipment overhead again (high-tech and
     low-tech) and charge each by time on that kind of machine.
  3. Stop when the next split no longer changes decisions.
- **Idle capacity is a separate cost.** A new machine run at a small share of capacity should
  not load its whole cost onto the few jobs using it. Charge users at practical capacity and
  report the cost of the unused part as a management cost of the decision to buy it.
- **There is more than one "right" cost.** Different systems give different costs for the same
  service; the right one is the one that reflects how resources are consumed for the decision at
  hand. Explain this to the managers, or they will reject the new numbers.
- **Controllability and buy-in.** Operations managers will not own costs they cannot influence
  (outsourced maintenance contracts negotiated by others). Show controllable and allocated costs
  separately in their reports.

## Order and customer profitability (an industrial-heating-alloys case)

- The old system spread selling and admin as a percentage of sales, so every order with the same
  margin looked equally profitable.
- Most support costs fell into two kinds: those driven by **volume** and those driven by
  **orders** (taking, negotiating, following up an order; setting up production for an item not
  normally stocked). A few more drivers were tested and carried under 5% of cost, so they were
  dropped. Start with two or three drivers and add one only if it moves results.
- Products held in stock are replenished in big batches, so their production-order cost behaves
  like volume cost; only special (non-stocked) items carry a set-up per customer order.
- Results come as a whale curve: some customers carry "hidden profit" (large orders of standard
  items), others "hidden loss" (small orders, custom items, heavy technical support, discounts).
- Sales pay tied to gross volume works against the new view; change it along with the cost
  system.
- The point is not to drop customers but to redeploy resources: price for order size and
  custom work, set minimums, move sales effort toward profitable accounts, cut the cost of
  handling orders. Drop a customer only on relevant costs (`cfo/references/relevant-cost-decisions.md`).
- A management slogan from such cases worth keeping: over the long run, all costs are variable;
  the question is which activity they follow.
