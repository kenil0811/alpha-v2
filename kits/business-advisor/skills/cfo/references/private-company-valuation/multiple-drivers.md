# Multiples in practice: metrics, benchmarks, tax adjustments and method choice

## 1. Pick the metric and the period before the multiple
- Metric: EBITDA (default), revenue (fast-growing or not yet profitable), EBITDA minus capex (capital-heavy),
  free cash flow, EBIT. EBIT tracks free cash flow better than EBITDA because it charges
  for capital use; the market still quotes EBITDA.
- Period: last twelve months (LTM), last quarter annualised (LQA), annual or monthly recurring revenue
  (ARR, MRR) for subscription models, next fiscal year (NFY).
- Basis: reported, adjusted, or pro forma (including acquisitions or run-rate savings). State which, and
  never mix bases between the target and the comps.
- Multiple sources: private (bankers, lenders, industry talk) and public (Capital IQ, Bloomberg,
  PitchBook, fairness opinions). Trading multiples (public shares) differ from transaction multiples
  (control deals, which include a premium).

## 2. Benchmark ranges, with their dates (refresh before use)
| Segment | Multiple of EBITDA | Source and year |
|---|---|---|
| US public market | about 18x | market data, year-end 2020 |
| Median US PE buyout | about 14x | same |
| Deals of $250M and over | 10x or more | PitchBook, cited 2021 |
| Deals of $25M and under | 6x or less | same |
| Lower-middle market, $10M to $25M EV | about 6.2x average (7.1x for above-average companies); 5.8x to 7.3x over 2015 to 2019 | GF Data, Q3 2019 |
| US small buyouts vs broader PE buyouts | small about 6x to 7.7x; broad about 8x to 13x; gap widening, 2010 to 2023 | Capital IQ, cited in a 2026 practitioner deck |
| By industry | wide spread | Damodaran's EV/EBITDA dataset (updated each January) |

The size ladder is the strongest single pattern: smaller deals trade lower because earnings are more
volatile, they cannot go public, fewer buyers compete, and a small-company premium exists in public
markets too. Practitioners (2019) noted lower-middle-market prices had risen 20% to 40% in five years
because more PE buyers, record unspent capital, cash-rich corporates and easy lending chased a flat
supply of good companies.

## 3. Adjust the price for tax assets (standard practice detail, method from a real deal)
In an asset purchase (or a stock deal treated as one for tax), the buyer amortises purchased goodwill for
tax, in the US over 15 years. The tax saved is part of what the buyer gets, so compare deals on the price
net of its present value.
1. Tax-deductible goodwill = price paid - fair value of identifiable net assets.
2. Annual tax saving = goodwill / 15 x tax rate.
3. PV of savings at a high (equity-like) discount rate, 15% in one practitioner example.
4. Effective price = price - PV; effective multiple = effective price / EBITDA.
Own numbers: price 100, EBITDA 12.5 (8.0x), goodwill 80, tax 25%. Saving 80 / 15 x 25% = 1.33 a year;
PV over 15 years at 15% (annuity factor 5.85) = 7.8. Effective price 92.2, effective multiple 7.4x.
One real deal moved from about 8.1x to 7.5x. A firm reporting its entry multiple may quote it net of
this benefit and excluding contingent payments: ask which basis a quoted multiple uses.

## 4. Match the method to the situation (generalised from practitioner deal studies)
| Situation | Lead method | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Growing roll-up candidate, active strategic buyers, public comps far larger | private comps plus LBO return; platform premium | public multiples (30x+) do not transfer to a $5M EBITDA firm |
| Two different business lines, customer concentration | sum of the parts; discount for concentration | each line has its own comp set |
| Declining product portfolio, no debt capacity | DCF over a finite life | almost no terminal value; leverage of zero |
| Carve-out with synergies for one owned company | stand-alone value plus a share of synergies | seller cares about speed, secrecy, certainty and liabilities as much as price |
DCF rarely sets a buyout price (terminal value dominates, and the discount rate could be 8% or 20%), but
it is the right tool where cash flows are contractual, finite or certain.

## 5. Valuation is more than the headline number
- Composition matters: cash at close vs rolled equity vs seller note vs earn-out. Contingent pay closes
  gaps when buyer and seller disagree on the forecast.
- Bids in a process are always spread out; one bidder usually stands apart. Fund factors move a bid too:
  portfolio fit, where the fund is in its life, partners' comfort with the sector, must-own assets.
- A practitioner view: deals rarely fail because the price was too high; they fail on operations and
  leverage. Treat that as a reason to stress the case, not to overpay.

## 6. Qualitative check before pricing: OUTSX (a practitioner framework, 2019)
- **O**pportunity: is it positive NPV? Check competition (can it earn excess profit?), undervaluation (can
  we buy well?), our edge (why do we earn a return others would not?), improvement (cost, focus,
  add-ons) and distress capacity (can it carry the debt?).
- **U**ncertainties: the main ones and how to mitigate each.
- **T**eam: can management deliver the plan?
- **S**tructure: financing mix, governance (board, control), management incentives.
- **X** exit: IPO, sale to a strategic or financial buyer, or recapitalisation.

## 7. Fair-value marks after closing: what research shows
- Funds tend to understate and smooth marks, then mark up during fundraising; interim IRRs shown to
  investors during fundraising predict final results poorly (Jenkinson et al., 2013).
- Inflated marks concentrate in weaker managers, who then struggle to raise the next fund (Brown et al., 2019).
- Funds under fundraising pressure manage portfolio-company earnings more aggressively (2020 study).
For an owner or lender reading a sponsor's marks: ask for the comp set and the EBITDA basis each quarter.
