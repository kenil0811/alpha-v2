# Buyout due diligence

> **Area:** `buyout-due-diligence` · owned by the CFO skill. **Use when:** Run due diligence on a company being acquired so that bad deals are caught before closing and price, terms and the plan reflect what was found. Use after a target is chosen and before an LOI is finalised or a purchase agreement is signed.

**Outcome:** a prioritised findings list (deal-killers, price issues, terms issues, items for the 100-day plan), a validated EBITDA figure, and a clear go / re-price / walk decision.

## When to use

- A CIM or data room has arrived and the buyer must decide how far to go.
- An IOI or LOI is being drafted and the diligence list must be set out.
- A quality of earnings (QofE) report is being scoped or read.
- Someone asks "what could we be missing?" before signing.

## Method

### 1. Treat diligence as a filter, not an audit
Think of every deal as great, marginal or lousy. In a fund, nearly all profit comes from the great deals; marginal deals return capital and little more; lousy deals lose the equity (the company may live on, but the equity is gone). So:
- The costly error is calling a lousy deal good (a false positive). Missing a great deal hurts much less: find another.
- Errors trade off: loosening the filter to miss fewer great deals lets in more lousy ones.
- The rule: do not do lousy deals.

Diligence also narrows the information gap. Sellers know their company and intentions; buyers know deal structures and the market. Sellers will play down anything that marks the deal as lousy. When a red or yellow flag appears, dig deeper.

### 2. Guard against your own biases
- **Framing:** the seller's story sets the frame; restate the deal in your own terms.
- **Representativeness:** "this looks like our last winner" is not evidence.
- **Overconfidence:** your projections are not facts; use ranges.
- **Confirmation:** you have spent weeks on it and want it to work. Assign someone to argue against.

Checklists help because they force red alerts and blunt these biases. But a finished checklist is necessary, not sufficient: the aim is to find what you do not know you do not know.

### 3. Work the 20-item checklist
**Core (most time):**
1. Financial: 3 years of annual, quarterly and monthly statements; audited or not; all liabilities including contingent; margin trends; projections versus the board budget; normalised working capital and how the purchase agreement will define it; capex needs and commitments; asset condition and liens; debt and guarantees; revenue recognition; receivables ageing; whether to commission a QofE; cash to fund operations and deal costs to closing.
2. Technology and IP: patents, trademarks, copyrights, trade secrets, invention-assignment and confidentiality agreements, infringement either way, in-licences and exclusive out-licences, licensed critical software.
3. Customers and sales: top 20 customers and revenue from each; concentration; risk of losing customers after the change of owner; satisfaction (call customers); warranties; backlog; terms, returns, refunds; sales compensation and how the deal changes it; seasonality.
4. Strategic fit (for add-ons): proven or hoped-for fit; capabilities gained; key people and their retention; integration time and cost; synergies; consent costs; whether the combination exits better.
5. Material contracts: loans and guarantees, customer and supplier contracts, JVs, leases, licences, indemnities, employment, exclusivity, franchise, non-competes, union agreements, and any contract with a change-of-control or assignment consent.
6. Employees and management: org chart and bios, disputes and stoppages, employment and restrictive-covenant agreements, related-party loans, 3 years of executive pay, benefit and pension plans, incentive plans, contractor versus employee classification, policies, legal history of key people.
16. Production, 17. Marketing, 18. Competition.

**Check-box items (often specialists):** 7 litigation; 8 tax (including carry-forwards); 9 antitrust and regulatory; 10 insurance; 11 corporate records and capitalisation; 12 environmental; 13 related-party transactions; 14 government regulation and compliance; 15 property.

**Logistics:** 19 data room (well organised rooms speed deals; watch for problems buried in odd corners); 20 disclosure schedules (they list every exception to the seller's reps; read them against each rep; beware large schedules delivered just before closing).

### 4. Validate EBITDA (quality of earnings)
Most buyers hire an accounting firm for a QofE after the LOI. It typically:
- tests each EBITDA adjustment in the CIM and looks for others;
- explains material year-on-year movements in all three statements;
- reviews customers, suppliers and working capital;
- checks asset quality, a cash proof and tax.

Increasingly sellers commission their own QofE before marketing; useful, but not independent.

Typical add-backs to test: owner and family pay to market rate; personal expenses run through the business; normalised benefits and rent; one-time legal costs; items that could be capitalised. Accept an add-back only if it truly stops after closing and the lender will also accept it.

### 5. Look for known ways numbers are dressed up
- Averages that hide a failing site or division (or the reverse).
- Channel stuffing: sales to distributors booked as end-market demand.
- Optimistic payback on new technology or capex.
- Capex, maintenance or SG&A deferred in the years before sale, flattering free cash flow.
- Sales pushed with generous service terms whose cost shows up later.
- Overhead hidden by scattering a function across field offices.
- Recurring costs labelled one-time (for example bad debts written off as restructuring).
- Inflated claims for a website or app as a cheap sales channel.

### 6. Sort findings and act
- **Deal-killers ("elephants"):** walk, or restructure fundamentally.
- **Price issues:** a working-capital shortfall comes off the price dollar for dollar; an earnings shortfall comes off at the multiple. Bridge gaps with a seller note, earn-out or rollover (see `deal-process-and-terms`).
- **Terms issues:** specific indemnities, bigger escrow, more reps, closing conditions.
- **Plan items:** feed the 100-day plan (see `value-creation-and-exit`).

### 7. Then diligence the upside
Once confident the deal is not lousy, ask: why is this worth more to us than to others? What hidden value did we find? Which of our theses does it serve? In competitive auctions, winners must stretch, and only a buyer with a real upside case can stretch safely.

Research finds that more time in diligence goes with better outcomes, and diligence done by the investor's own people correlates better with results than outsourced work.

## Worked pattern

A $6M-EBITDA manufacturer. CIM adjustments: +$400K owner salary above market, +$250K one-time lawsuit, +$300K "start-up losses" on a new line.
- QofE accepts the salary (new CEO costs $200K, so net +$200K) and the lawsuit; rejects the new-line losses (the line still loses money). Adjusted EBITDA falls from $6.95M to $6.45M.
- At 6.5x that is $3.25M of price.
- Customer calls show the second-largest customer (18% of sales) is re-bidding next year: a terms issue, handled with a $1.5M escrow and an earn-out on that account.
- Capex was $300K a year versus $900K depreciation: likely deferred. Budget $1.2M of catch-up capex in the model.

## Common mistakes

- Using diligence to confirm a decision already made.
- Taking CIM adjustments at face value in the model (fine for an IOI, not for a final price).
- Not defining working capital precisely; definitions swing the number a lot.
- Ignoring change-of-control clauses in key contracts.
- Skipping customer calls.
- Assuming a "recession-proof" history will repeat: in the Caesars buyout, revenue fell about 20% in 2008 and leverage of 7 to 8 times EBITDA became 13 to 14 times.
