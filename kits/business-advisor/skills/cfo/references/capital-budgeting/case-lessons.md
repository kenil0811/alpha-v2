# Capital budgeting: lessons from teaching cases

Part of `cfo/references/capital-budgeting.md`. Generalised lessons from two project cases.
No case answers are given.

## A long-lived asset bought for a short contract (a shipping case)

- **The contract covers only the first years.** After it ends the asset earns the market rate.
  Value the contract years at contract terms and the rest at a forecast market price; most of
  the NPV usually sits in the uncontracted years, so the contract alone does not justify the buy.
- **Forecast the market price from supply and demand.** Capacity on order and scrapping drive
  supply; the end-market drives demand. Order books more than two or three years out are
  unreliable; treat them as a scenario, not a fact.
- **Age changes the economics.** Older assets earn a lower rate, spend more days out of service
  and need larger periodic overhauls. Build these by year of the asset's life, not as a flat
  average.
- **The holding period is a decision.** Compare selling or scrapping before a costly overhaul with
  keeping the asset to the end of its physical life. Value each and take the higher NPV; the
  overhaul capex and the salvage value decide it.
- **Staged purchase payments** (deposits during construction, balance on delivery) go on the
  timeline when paid, not on delivery.
- **Working capital** at start grows with inflation and comes back at the end.
- **Inflation must be consistent.** If costs grow at inflation plus a margin and the price
  forecast is nominal, discount at a nominal rate.
- **Tax regime matters.** The same asset can be worth much more where shipping income is lightly
  taxed; value it under the regime that actually applies, and say so.
- **Counterparty risk** on the contract is small only if the customer is reputable; otherwise
  haircut the contract cash flows.

## Entering a related business (a seafood processing-plant case)

- **Use the new business's cost of capital,** taken from listed companies in that business, not
  the parent's. A safe parent entering a riskier step of the chain must use the riskier rate.
- **Real or nominal, never mixed.** A forecast without inflation is discounted at a real rate; an
  inflated forecast at a nominal rate. With high inflation, the mix-up swings the answer.
- **Depreciation tax shields do not inflate.** In a real-terms model they lose value each year;
  model them in nominal terms or deflate them.
- **Spontaneous liabilities** (payables, taxes due) fund part of working capital; net them off.
- **Ramp-up:** a plant sold at a third of capacity in year 1 and near full by year 5 needs that
  path in the forecast, including the extra launch marketing.
- **Investment tax credits** are a cash inflow when the parent can use them; consolidated tax
  losses in early years also save tax now if the parent is profitable.
- **Subsidised financing** (tax-exempt or development bonds tied to the project) is valued
  separately: APV = NPV of the project all-equity + PV of the financing benefit (the interest
  saving after tax, discounted at the debt rate). If such financing may not be offered again,
  do not build it into the hurdle rate for future projects.
- **"It lowers our return on equity" is not a reason to reject.** A business earning unusually
  high returns on its core will see its average fall with any fairly priced new project. Accept
  if NPV is positive at the project's own rate.
- **A failed acquisition sets a reference.** If the owner turned down an offer for a similar
  business, the build-versus-buy comparison should use that price as the buy option.
