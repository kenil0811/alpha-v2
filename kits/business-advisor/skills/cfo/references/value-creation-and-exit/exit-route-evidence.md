# Exit routes: buyer types, timing and the evidence

## 1. Strategic or financial buyer: what the seller gives up (2026 practitioner view, seller's side)
| Issue | Strategic (industry) buyer | Financial (PE) buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Management | often redundant | needed and kept |
| Employees | layoffs more likely | more security and opportunity |
| Benefit plans | moved to the buyer's | rarely changed |
| Owner reinvestment | rarely possible | often required, an opportunity for a second gain |
| Buyer's thesis | cost savings | invest for growth |
| Speed | slower, more process | quicker |
| How the seller is seen | selling out | partnering for growth |
| Price | either may pay more | either may pay more |

## 2. Write the exit backwards (2020 practitioner view)
Start from the exit: what must the company have in place, by when, to earn the target return? Shape the
story around three questions every next buyer asks:
1. Is the foundation solid? Sound accounting, known business drivers, a clear customer value proposition.
2. How will I create value? For example sales-team expansion into new regions, pricing, data, management.
3. Can I sell it on again? Strategic attributes that last, and the macro conditions the plan needs.

Reassess timing every period, not just at the end:
- Inside the company: customer churn, key wins and losses, product launches, management gaps and
  succession, capex needs.
- Outside: regulation, technology shifts, supplier and customer consolidation, the economic cycle (exit
  multiples have swung by two to five times across cycles in some sectors).
- The original thesis: what has changed since entry.

What kills or saves a sale (practitioner examples, generalised): inaccurate inventory accounting ended one
deal outright; a technology shift (engine electrification) made a buyer walk from a growing company;
planning 18 months out surfaced a key customer renewal, a facility lease and succession gaps early enough
to fix, and the company sold well to a strategic buyer seeking a foothold in a growing market.

## 3. Route-by-route evidence
- **Secondary buyouts** are now a main route: 43% of European PE exits in 2000 to 2014 (Jenkinson and Sousa,
  2015); over 40% of US exits by count in the five years to about 2020 (industry data).
- **What tips the route:** IPOs rise after strong stock markets; secondary sales rise when debt is cheap and
  buyers hold unspent capital, more so for cash-generative, low-capex companies; trade sales are likelier
  for smaller, faster-growing companies.
- **Pressure distorts:** buyers near the end of their investment period pay higher multiples with less
  debt; sellers under pressure exit sooner at lower multiples; deals done under pressure underperform
  (Arcot et al., 2015). Secondary buyouts are priced higher than first buyouts, mainly because of easier
  credit, with little sign of further operating gains (Wang, 2012).
- **IPO is a staged exit:** sponsors sold down over about three years after listing on average and kept
  board seats longer; they sold faster if dividends had already returned their capital (US deals 1999 to
  2008, published 2015). Plan the lock-up and follow-on offerings into the return.
- **Partial sale or carve-out:** if buyers want one part, split the business by channel or line, build
  enough stand-alone cash flow in the rest, and sell the wanted part at a price buyers can justify.
- **Long hold:** core funds (lower risk and fees, about 12% to 14% target IRR, up to 15-year life) and
  long-hold buyout funds. A Bain model (2018) found a long hold beat four successive 5-year deals by nearly
  2x after tax over 24 years, from lower transaction costs and deferred tax. Re-diligence a long-held
  company every 3 to 4 years and sell if its duration risk has grown. Permanent holding companies are a
  further variant.
- **Selling to yourself:** a sponsor can move assets it wants to keep into a new vehicle (continuation
  fund); old investors choose to roll or sell. Conflicts, information gaps and valuation doubts make this
  hard; it needs an independent price check.
- **Investor liquidity without selling the company:** fund investors can sell fund stakes on the
  secondary market; this is not a secondary buyout.

## 4. Market context, with dates
- Exits fell about 70% globally in May 2020 vs May 2019; deal professionals had spent only 3% to 5% of their
  time on exit preparation (2019 survey). Firms used the delay to invest in growth areas and pull forward
  value programs meant for the next owner (one expected a 15%+ EBITDA lift).
- Bain 2026: average holding periods near 7 years; about 40% of companies held over 5 years (29% in 2019);
  distributions under 15% of NAV for four straight years. Expect slower exits and plan for longer holds,
  dividend recaps or continuation vehicles.
