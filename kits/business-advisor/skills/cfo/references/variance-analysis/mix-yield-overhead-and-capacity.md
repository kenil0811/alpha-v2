# Mix and yield, overhead variance splits, standards, capacity and throughput costing

From standard cost-accounting textbook procedures, restated.

## Direct materials (or labour) mix and yield
Use when inputs can substitute for one another (ingredients in a recipe, grades of labour).
They split the efficiency variance; price variances are unchanged.
- Budgeted mix % for input i = standard quantity of i / total standard quantity per unit.
- **Yield variance** = (actual total input − budgeted total input for actual output) ×
  budgeted average standard price per unit of input (at budgeted mix).
- **Mix variance** = Σ_i (actual mix %_i − budgeted mix %_i) × actual total input × standard
  price_i.
- Mix + yield = efficiency variance. A favourable mix variance (more of the cheap input) can
  hurt quality or the product's appeal, and often comes with an unfavourable yield variance.

## Variable overhead
- Spending variance = (actual rate per driver unit − standard rate) × actual driver quantity.
- Efficiency variance = (actual driver quantity − standard driver quantity for actual output) ×
  standard rate. It reflects use of the driver (for example machine hours), not overhead
  spending itself.

## Overhead summary formats
- Four-variance: variable spending, variable efficiency, fixed spending, fixed production volume.
- Three-variance: combined spending (variable + fixed), variable efficiency, production volume.
- Two-variance: flexible-budget variance (all spending and efficiency), production volume.
- The production-volume variance never appears for variable overhead.

## Setting standards
- Ideal standards assume no waste or downtime: useful as a target, demotivating as a yardstick.
- Attainable (currently achievable) standards allow normal spoilage and downtime; most firms use these.
- External benchmarks (a competitor or trade association) make standards harder to dispute and
  show the size of the gap, but data may not be comparable (accounting, product, scale).

## Capacity denominator and income
- Theoretical and practical capacity are supply measures; normal and budgeted capacity use are
  demand measures. The lower the denominator, the higher the fixed overhead rate per unit.
- Under absorption costing the choice changes inventory values and operating income in any
  period where production and sales differ, unless the volume variance is prorated.
- Cost-based prices set on budgeted use invite the downward demand spiral; practical capacity
  avoids it.

## Throughput (super-variable) costing
- Only direct materials are inventoriable; all other costs, even variable conversion, are
  expensed in the period.
- Income: throughput ≤ variable ≤ absorption when production exceeds sales (and the reverse when
  inventory falls). Throughput costing removes almost all reward for building inventory.
