# Term sheet terms, in plain words

Each term: what it does, what is normal at an early stage (standard practice; check current market data), and what to watch. Payout formulas are in `../../cfo/references/startup-valuation-and-deal-math/deal-math.md`.

## Economics

**Price (pre- and post-money).** Post = pre + new money. "Fully diluted" counts all options, warrants and convertibles. Watch: whether the pre-money includes an enlarged option pool.

**Option pool.** Shares reserved for future hires. Investors usually require it to be created or topped up before the round, so it dilutes only existing holders and lowers the effective pre-money. Do not fight the mechanism; negotiate the pool size (based on a hiring plan for the next 18 to 24 months) or a higher pre-money.

**Warrants.** Rights to buy more shares later; a back-door price cut. Avoid in priced equity rounds; normal in bridge notes.

**Liquidation preference.** Who is paid first in a sale, merger or wind-down (a sale counts as a "liquidation"). Normal: 1x, non-participating (investor takes the greater of its money back or its converted share). Watch: multiples above 1x; full participation (money back and a share of the rest); how series stack (later series first versus equal rank, "pari passu").

**Dividends.** Normal: non-cumulative, only if declared. Cumulative dividends add to the preference each year and act like interest.

**Anti-dilution.** Protects investors if a later round is priced lower (a down round) by adjusting their conversion price. Normal: broad-based weighted average. Founder-hostile: full ratchet (resets to the new low price regardless of how few shares were sold).

**Pay-to-play.** Investors who do not take their share of later rounds lose some rights (often convert to common). Good for founders: keeps investors committed.

**Vesting.** Founder and employee shares are earned over time. Normal: 4 years, 1-year cliff, monthly after. Watch: acceleration on a sale (single trigger: on sale; double trigger: on sale and termination; double trigger is more common).

**Redemption rights.** Investors can force the company to buy back their shares after some years. Rarely used, but hangs over the company; resist or push far out.

## Control

**Board of directors.** The most important control term. Early-stage normal: small board (3 or 5) such as two founders and one investor, or two founders, two investors and one independent. Watch: investors gaining a majority early.

**Protective provisions.** Investor vetoes over specific actions: selling the company, issuing senior stock, changing the charter, taking on debt, changing board size. Normal: a short list, voted by preferred holders as one class. Watch: long lists that reach into operating decisions; separate votes per series.

**Drag-along.** Forces minority holders to join a sale approved by the majority (or the board and a defined group). Fine if the trigger group includes founders or the board.

**Conversion.** Preferred converts to common at the holder's choice, and automatically at an IPO above a threshold or by majority vote.

**Voting rights.** Preferred votes with common on an as-converted basis, except for protective provisions.

**Information rights.** Major investors receive annual audited and quarterly financials and the budget. Normal; limit to holders above a minimum.

**Registration rights.** Rights to have shares registered in an IPO. Standard; rarely worth negotiating early.

**Right of first refusal and co-sale.** Company and investors may buy founders' shares before outsiders; investors may sell alongside founders. Normal.

**Pro-rata (participation) rights.** Investors may buy their share of future rounds to avoid dilution. Normal for major investors.

**Founders' activities.** Founders devote full time and do not compete. Normal; watch the scope.

**Conditions precedent.** Due diligence, legal documents, sometimes management rights or hires. Watch: vague conditions that let the investor walk late.

**No-shop.** The company stops talking to other investors for a period (30 to 60 days is common). Sign only once the terms are agreed.

**Indemnification and fees.** Company indemnifies directors; company often pays the lead investor's legal fees, capped.

## Convertible note specifics

Amount; closings; maturity (6 to 24 months); interest (7% to 12%, simple); automatic conversion in a qualified financing above a set size; discount (10% to 30%); cap; voluntary conversion at maturity at a pre-agreed valuation; repayment at a multiple (1.5x to 3x) if the company is sold before conversion; unsecured; amendments by majority of note holders.

## Deeper mechanics (VC practice, 2020)

**Security types, from most to least investor-friendly on a sale:** participating preferred (preference
plus a share of the rest), capped participating (stops participating once a set multiple is reached, then
converts if better), convertible preferred (preference or convert, whichever is larger), redeemable
(debt-like, repaid at a set date). A convertible holder converts only when its share of the sale price is
worth more than its preference plus accrued dividends: find that exit value for each series.

**Stacked preferences:** with several series, preferences are paid in a set order, usually last money in
first (or all series equal, "pari passu"). Work out conversion series by series starting with the one whose
conversion threshold is highest; each decision changes the shares outstanding for the next.

**Three kinds of dilution:**
| Kind | What falls | Protection |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage | Your share of the company | Pro-rata (pre-emptive) rights |
| Dollar value | Value per share, in a down round | Anti-dilution adjustment |
| Liquidity | Your place in the payout order | Negotiating new preferences in later rounds |

**Anti-dilution in practice:** it is commonly waived in a down round, because the new investor demands it,
so its real effect is to give existing investors a say (a near-veto) over the down round.

**Other control terms:**
- Certain decisions (budget, senior hires, new debt) need the approval of the investor's board member, not
  just a board majority.
- Sale rights and unlocking rights: after a set period, investors may force a sale or exit process.
- Redemption at about year 5 to 7: rarely exercised, used as a forcing device to get to an exit.
- Management rights letter: lets a pension-backed fund show it has management rights (an ERISA requirement).
- Tranching: money paid in parts on reaching milestones; it shifts risk to founders and invites argument
  about whether a milestone was met.
- Pay-to-play: investors who don't take their pro-rata in a later round lose preferences or convert to common.

**How common (US law-firm deal data, 2020; recheck):** multiple preferences under about 10% of deals;
over 80% non-participating, and caps on participation are rare; broad-based weighted average is the norm;
drag-along is common; redemption rights, cumulative dividends and tranching are uncommon (tranching more
common in life sciences). See `cfo/references/vc-deal-screening/vc-decision-benchmarks.md` for which terms
investors will and won't bend on.

**Seed instruments:** notes and SAFEs both defer the price; a note is debt (interest, maturity) and usually
pays a multiple (often 2x) if the company is sold before it converts.
