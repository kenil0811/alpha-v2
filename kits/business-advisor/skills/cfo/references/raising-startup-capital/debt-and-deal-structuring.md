# Debt for young ventures, and structuring the deal

Depth for `cfo/references/raising-startup-capital.md` section 4. From material on financing new ventures,
deal structure and deal terms, and bootstrap finance; our words and our own numbers. Percentages are the readings' dated rules of thumb; check current lender terms.

## Debt forms a young business can get

- **Cash-flow loans:** lent against expected ability to repay; need an earnings history; protected by
  covenants (minimum cash, maximum debt to equity). Hard for a start-up.
- **Asset-based loans:** a lien on assets that can be sold. Typical advance rates in the reading:

| Asset | Share lent | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Receivables from creditworthy customers | up to about 85% to 95% | Or sold to a factor at a discount; the factor collects |
| Finished, saleable inventory | about 50% | |
| Equipment | about 50% to 80%, over 3 to 10 years | Depends on resale market; leasing is the rent alternative |
| Real estate | about 75% to 85% | |
| Patents | rarely | Their value collapses exactly when the loan fails |

- **Personal guarantees:** a founder or backer pledges assets.
- **Letters of credit:** a bank guarantee that lets the business buy, often abroad.
- **Government-guaranteed loans** (in the US, SBA and farm programmes).
- **Trade credit:** ask suppliers for long terms on a first order; it is often the cheapest early money.

The rule: lenders lend against what they can sell if the loan goes bad, and the thicker the market for the
asset and the less its value depends on the borrower, the more they lend.

## Structure the deal by carving up the cash flows

The founder's task is to split the venture's future cash into pieces and sell each to whoever demands the
lowest return for it, because the lowest-return buyer is the one who sees the least risk in that piece.
1. Project the venture's cash flows, including the exit value.
2. Break them into sources of return: operating cash, any tax benefits of early losses, and terminal
   value. Discount each at the venture's overall return to see what share of value each provides.
3. Sell the safest slice first: a dependable floor of operating cash can carry a loan; tax benefits (where
   law allows them to pass through, as in partnerships) suit a high-tax investor; the risky rest and the
   exit go to equity at a high rate.
4. What is left belongs to the founder. Adjust for what the founder wants beyond cost: a cash cushion,
   fewer covenants, a backer with useful contacts.
5. Pick the legal form that allows the split (partnerships and LLCs can divide cash flows flexibly;
   corporations use classes of stock, debt and warrants). Use a lawyer and a tax adviser.

**Worked pattern (own numbers, in $000).** A plant needs 1,500. Projected cash: 300, 300, 500, 600, then
10,000 in year 5 including the sale.
- All equity at a 50% target: present value of all flows ≈ 1,917, so the investor needs 1,500 ÷ 1,917 ≈ 78%.
- With a loan: a bank is sure of at least 100 a year for interest; at 10% that supports a 1,000 loan,
  principal repaid from the sale. Remaining flows: 200, 200, 400, 500, 8,900, worth ≈ 1,612 at 50%. Equity
  now needs only 500, so the investor takes 500 ÷ 1,612 ≈ 31% of the remainder.
- The founder keeps about 69% of the equity instead of 22%, at the cost of a debt obligation that must
  be met even in a bad year. Test that floor hard: if the 100 is not truly safe, the structure can sink the
  venture.

## Term-sheet sections

Representations and diligence (the company vouches for its share count and records; investors may inspect
minutes, contracts, budgets, leases before closing); the security (common, preferred, convertible, with
voting, dividends, participation, conversion, redemption and anti-dilution); control (board seats,
approvals needed for new stock, dividends or a sale; information rights); and conditions to close or
complete within 30 to 90 days (sign a key employee, extend a lease, start a search for a new CEO or a CFO).
Definitions and market norms are in `cfo/references/raising-startup-capital/term-sheet-terms.md`.

## Fund and angel-group structures (why investors behave as they do)

- **VC fund:** limited partners (pensions, endowments, wealthy families) commit capital, drawn in stages;
  general partners run it for about a 2% yearly fee and 20% of profits; a fixed life of about ten years,
  investing in the first three to five, so they need exits within roughly three to seven years. A
  partner's track record decides the next fund, so they seek home runs.
- **Angel group:** individuals who screen together but each decide and write their own cheque (often 15
  to 20 angels make a round); no fixed fund life, so they can wait longer and accept smaller exits, but
  have less to put in later.
- Founders should match the venture to the structure: big funds need big exits; angel groups suit smaller,
  earlier, slower ventures.
