# Deal sourcing and screening

> **Area:** `pe-deal-sourcing-screening` · owned by the CFO skill. **Use when:** Find and rank companies worth buying, using an investment thesis, quantitative screens and a qualitative short-list. Use when a buyer (PE fund, family office, searcher or a company planning add-ons) needs a target list or must decide which inbound deals deserve time.

**Outcome:** a written thesis, a screened and scored list of targets, and one or a few companies chosen for deeper work, with the reasons each was kept or dropped.

## When to use

- "We want to buy a business: where do we look?"
- "Which industry should we focus on?"
- "We have 200 names from a database. Which 3 should we call?"
- "An intermediary sent us a teaser. Is it worth an NDA?"
- Planning add-on acquisitions for an existing company.

## Method

Depth: `cfo/references/pe-deal-sourcing-screening/thesis-and-sourcing-playbook.md` (size tiers and equity-cheque math, thesis lifecycle and budget, buy-and-build indices, first-pass grid, intermediary types and tiers, nine-step outreach campaign, funnel KPIs, dated evidence for niche and proactive sourcing).

### 1. Start from a thesis, not a list
Proprietary or lightly shopped deals are the prize: less competition means better entry prices. They come from a focused thesis plus relationships built before a sale process starts.

Write a short thesis proposal (about 2 pages) with:
- A well-defined sub-sector inside the buyer's chosen verticals.
- 3 to 5 thesis points (why this niche is attractive), each with a test plan and a statement of what must be true.
- Market size and fragmentation estimate.
- The buyer's angle: why this buyer wins deals or adds value here.
- A time and money budget for testing.

Test it. If two or more critical points fail, pause the thesis. If it holds, write a fuller document: market definition and size, where you will play, what a good company looks like (metrics), channel dynamics, risks, value-creation levers, comps, industry resources (associations, trade shows, sector bankers), and a business-development plan.

Approval bar used by one lower-middle-market firm (adapt to your size):
- Market over about $200M, fragmented, with clear M&A potential; not declining; predictable demand (4%+ growth for a funded thesis).
- No more than two major industry risks, each with a plausible mitigant.
- At least 30 to 60 named targets, several sector bankers, 3+ conferences.
- Profit opportunity modelled and above the buyer's minimum.
- A named owner willing to commit 100 to 250+ hours.

Review each active thesis twice a year: deals seen, outreach made, bankers met, conferences attended, time and money spent, and whether every thesis point is still true.

### 2. Score industries
A weighted scorecard used by a family-office investor (a good default):
- Non-negotiables, 40%: fragmented; not a commodity; low cyclicality; positive macro trends; diversified customers and suppliers.
- Market potential, 15%: margin, size, growth.
- Strategic fit, 15%: fit with your expertise; synergy with what you own; product maturity.
- Competition, 15%: the five forces (rivalry, supplier power, buyer power, entrants, substitutes).
- Risk, 15%: regulatory, market, operational, technology.

Then answer for the top industries: who the major players and consolidators are, how it did in past recessions, capex intensity, how predictable cash flow is, which bankers cover it, who would buy at exit, and financing options. Build a generic cash-flow model for a typical company.

### 3. Translate LBO criteria into screens
Traits that make a company a good buyout target. The first rows are quantitative and go into the database screen; the rest are judged later by hand.

| Quantitative (screen) | Judgement (short-list) |
|---|---|
| Lower entry multiple | Industry tailwinds (or a fixable impairment) |
| More borrowing capacity (stable EBITDA, collateral) | Fragmentation with platform potential |
| Higher growth rate | Strong management, or clear room to improve it |
| Lower capex need | Clear operational improvement opportunity |
| Higher return on invested capital | Non-core assets that can be sold |
| Consistent free cash flow | Good exit options; expected future performance above current |
| | Overall risk profile acceptable |

Screen steps (S&P Capital IQ, PitchBook or similar):
1. Define the industry with codes (NAICS or the database's own tree) and geography.
2. Set company type and status (operating; private or small-cap public).
3. Add size bands (revenue, EBITDA) that fit your equity cheque and lenders.
4. Add financial filters: EBITDA margin floor, growth, capex/revenue ceiling, EV/EBITDA ceiling for public names, positive FCF in recent years.
5. Save the screen; export; remove obvious misfits by hand.
6. Score survivors on the judgement column; keep 3 to 4.
7. Research each (filings, investor decks, earnings calls, industry reports, analyst reports on public peers) and pick one, writing a paragraph on why each other name was dropped.

Small and micro-cap companies often screen well: less analyst coverage, fewer institutional buyers, lower multiples.

### 4. Run the sourcing engine
Two channels, run together:
- **Intermediaries:** tier bankers, brokers and advisors by fit and deal flow. Top tiers get regular calls and visits; the long tail gets periodic e-mail updates. Tell the market what you want, specifically.
- **Direct to owner:** a ranked list of 50 to 100 high-priority companies per thesis; outreach by warm introduction (best), e-mail, phone, LinkedIn, then visits. Expect a long funnel: one growth firm reported roughly 10,000 companies contacted, 750 visited, 12 deals closed.

Data-driven sourcing helps: combine public data (patents, SEC Form D, government grants and loans, Inc. 5000, ISO certifications, UCC filings) and score companies with a simple model, then let a person review the top of the list. Most targets say "not now"; keep in touch, because timing changes (one cited company called back after a later acquisition).

Track everything in a CRM: contacts, touches, NDAs, IOIs, LOIs by source. Measure conversion by channel and drop what does not convert.

Semi-annual activity targets one firm set per funded thesis: about 50 direct outreaches, 10 banker conversations, 3 operator discussions, 10 new watch-list targets, 1 conference, 1 IOI.

### 5. Triage inbound deals fast
For a teaser or CIM, answer in under an hour:
1. Does the size fit our cheque and lender appetite?
2. Is it in a thesis or adjacent to one?
3. Do the strengths outweigh the risks we can see?
4. What are the 2 or 3 "elephants" that would kill it?
If all pass, sign the NDA and go to `buyout-due-diligence` and `private-company-valuation`.

## Worked pattern

A buyer with $10M to $20M of equity wants specialty distribution.
- Thesis: industrial MRO distribution in the Midwest; 400+ firms with $10M to $60M revenue; top 10 hold 25% share; growth tracks industrial output plus share gains from e-commerce laggards.
- Screen: NAICS 4238; revenue $15M to $75M; EBITDA margin above 8%; capex under 2% of revenue; 3-year revenue growth above 3%. Result: 140 names, 35 after hand review.
- Score: 6 rank above 70 of 100. Two dropped for customer concentration over 30%, one for a recent failed ERP roll-out.
- Outreach: 3 owner meetings; one 64-year-old founder with no succession plan agrees to share numbers. That becomes the target.

## Common mistakes

- Reacting to whatever bankers send instead of building a thesis; you then compete in full auctions at full prices.
- Screening only on numbers. The judgement factors (management, platform potential, exit buyers) decide most deals.
- Picking an industry because the screen is easy, not because the team can add value or is passionate about it.
- Double-counting: small size, higher risk and fewer exit buyers are linked; do not penalise the same weakness three times.
- Letting leads die: no follow-up cadence, no CRM.
- Spending big on a thesis before testing its critical points cheaply.
