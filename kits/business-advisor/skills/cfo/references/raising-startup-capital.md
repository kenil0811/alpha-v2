# Raising startup capital

> **Area:** `raising-startup-capital` · owned by the CFO skill. **Use when:** Help a founder choose where to raise money (own funds, friends and family, angels, accelerators, venture capital, debt, grants), pick the instrument (SAFE, convertible note, priced equity) and negotiate the term sheet. Use when a venture needs outside money, when a term sheet or note arrives, or when an owner asks "should we raise, from whom, and on what terms?".

**Outcome:** the right amount of money from the right kind of investor, on terms the founder understands and can live with, so that the business keeps both its economics and its control where they matter.

## When to use

- A venture's plan needs more cash than its revenue provides.
- Choosing between bootstrapping, angels, venture capital, loans or grants.
- A SAFE, convertible note or term sheet needs explaining or negotiating.
- Planning the sequence of rounds and how much each should raise.

## Method

### 1. Decide whether this is a venture-capital business

Venture funds need a few huge exits (returns follow a power law), so they back companies that can plausibly reach exits large enough to return the fund, within about 3 to 7 years. Ask honestly:
- Can this become a very large company quickly? Is the market big and the advantage defensible?
- Does the founder want a liquidity event, a board and dilution?

If not, it is a cash-flow business: fund it with own money, customer pre-payments, revenue, bank or SBA debt, grants and competitions, and possibly angels who accept slower, smaller outcomes. This is a good business, just not a VC one. Note that many investors do not want "dividend" businesses because they cannot exit.

**Map the venture on two axes** (capital needed to reach positive cash flow; novelty of the technology or model):

| | Low novelty | High novelty |
|---|---|---|
| **High capital** | Proven big projects: commercial banks, project finance, strategic investors | The "valley of death": too costly for VC, too risky for project lenders; government programmes or corporate partners |
| **Low capital** | Most small businesses: own savings, personal credit, bank loans, retained earnings | Angels and VC (a small share of all new firms) |

Why: a lender's upside is capped at interest, so it avoids uncertainty and pushes for safe strategies; an equity investor shares the upside, so it accepts high failure odds but charges for it (hurdle rates of 50% to 75% against loan rates of 10% to 15% in older data) and wants a say. A venture can move left as uncertainty falls, unlocking cheaper money. Design to move: cut the capital need or the uncertainty (a lean build that fits a VC budget; keeping a proven contractor so project lenders stay in). A change that raises perceived risk can lose financing already agreed.

### 1a. Bootstrap first when you can

Most fast-growing private companies started on the founders' savings, credit cards and modest sums, then grew on debt and retained earnings, not outside equity. Venture investors are not wrong to pass: they need scale, a proprietary edge, a credible plan and proven founders, which most start-ups lack. Early outside money also has hidden costs: it removes the discipline of scarcity, invites spending money not needed, and makes radical changes of course harder (investors ask whether the old plan was wrong or badly run). Bootstrap rules:
1. Get operating quickly, even with a copycat idea in a small niche; opportunities show up once in the flow of business.
2. Seek quick break-even, cash-generating work (consulting, reselling) to fund the bigger product.
3. Sell high-value products where the founder's personal selling can replace a marketing budget; avoid cheap impulse goods that need mass advertising.
4. Hire eager, unproven people who want to grow, rather than a costly "crack team".
5. Keep growth to what cash can fund; add people and capacity only when there is no alternative.
6. Focus on cash, not profit or share: healthy margins from day one, supplier terms, fast collection, refusal of slow payers; be frugal everywhere except the one or two things that matter.
7. Court a bank before you need it: clean books, a relationship with the branch, then borrow once creditworthy (often after about three years) and repay on time to earn a credit line.

Later the founder must often reverse these habits: compete beyond the niche, standardise, bring key services in-house, think strategically rather than penny by penny, and hire higher-priced talent.

### 2. Know the funding ladder

| Stage | Typical sources | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| Idea, prototype | Own savings, credit, friends and family, grants, competitions | Testing the idea |
| Seed | Angels, accelerators, seed funds, SAFEs or notes | Product, first customers |
| Early (Series A) | Venture capital, priced preferred stock | Proven product to repeatable sales |
| Expansion | Later venture, growth equity, venture debt, corporate investors | Scaling |
| Liquidity | Sale (M&A), IPO | Exit |

Each step usually raises several times more money at a several-times-higher valuation, if milestones were hit. Bubbles show the reverse: huge rounds without a working model end in failure.

### 3. Match strategy and financing

- Raise to reach the next value-creating milestone (proof of demand, revenue level, regulatory approval) plus buffer; 12 to 24 months of runway is common practice.
- Taking a large round early sets expectations that shape strategy (growth over profit); a small raise keeps options open.
- Angels versus VCs: angels invest their own money, decide fast, write smaller cheques, often bring less follow-on capacity and support. VCs invest others' money, take board seats, provide recruiting, partnering and follow-on rounds, and push for large exits. Angel returns improve with more diligence time and industry expertise.
- Pick investors as partners: early investors shape every later round (existing investors may resist a down round or argue for a high price to limit their dilution).
- The best way to improve price and terms is several interested investors at once.
- Who you raise from matters more than the terms. Sophisticated investors help when things go wrong; inexperienced ones panic and stop funding. Top-tier investors charge more (take more equity) and are usually worth it.
- Mixing investor types (a fund wanting a home run, a corporate wanting a product for its own use, nervous angels) creates conflict when the plan slips. Past investors also constrain later choices: a fund backing a blockbuster may veto a safe side business.
- Do not maximise the first valuation. Each price sets expectations; missing them forces a down round, which is painful. Leave room for a run of rising rounds (roughly doubling each time is a common aim).
- Raise in stages tied to milestones: enough to reach the next one with a cushion (it always takes longer and costs more), not so much that dilution is wasted. Running out of cash is the worst negotiating position there is.
- Build relationships with investors months before the raise; short update meetings let them watch progress, so diligence is half done when you ask.
- Create urgency with real alternatives or momentum, but do not list every firm you are talking to: they know each other.
- Show you can recruit strong people before the money arrives; investors read it as a test of leadership.
- Valuation is a negotiated result, not a fact: it moves with the market's heat and with bargaining power. Terms are often easier to negotiate than price, and they matter most in the outcomes founders ignore (a small exit, a down round). Beware a lawyer paid only on closing.

### 4. Choose the instrument

- **SAFE:** a right to future shares; no interest or maturity; converts at the next priced round using a valuation cap and/or discount. Simple and cheap.
- **Convertible note:** debt that converts; has interest (often 7% to 12%), maturity (often 6 to 24 months), a qualified-financing threshold for automatic conversion, a discount (10% to 30%) and often a cap; may require repayment at a multiple (1.5x to 3x) if the company is sold first. Bridge notes often carry warrants.
- **Priced equity round (usually preferred stock):** sets a price per share and a full term sheet; costlier but clear.
- **Debt:** keeps ownership but needs cash flow to repay; suits businesses with steady revenue. Cash-flow loans need a history and carry covenants; asset-based loans lend a share of saleable assets (receivables, inventory, equipment, property), and banks will not lend against patents. Advance rates, government guarantees and how to split a venture's cash flows among lenders and investors who each want a different risk are in `cfo/references/raising-startup-capital/debt-and-deal-structuring.md`.

Model every instrument's conversion before signing (see startup-valuation-and-deal-math).

### 5. Read the term sheet: economics and control

Investors care about two things: **economics** (what they get at exit) and **control** (what they can decide or block). If a term affects neither, do not fight hard over it. Key terms, explained in `cfo/references/raising-startup-capital/term-sheet-terms.md`:

Economics: price (pre- and post-money), option pool, liquidation preference and participation, dividends, anti-dilution, pay-to-play, vesting, warrants.
Control: board composition, protective provisions (vetoes), drag-along, voting, redemption, information rights, right of first refusal and co-sale, no-shop.

### 6. Negotiate

1. Agree the headline: amount, pre-money, option pool size.
2. Ask for a pro-forma cap table showing every holder after the round, on a fully diluted basis.
3. Prioritise: price, liquidation preference, board control and vetoes, then the rest.
4. Market-standard early terms today (standard practice; check current data): 1x non-participating preference, broad-based weighted-average anti-dilution, non-cumulative dividends, 4-year founder vesting with a 1-year cliff, board balanced between founders, investors and independents.
5. A large pre-money with harsh terms (participation, high multiples, full ratchet) can be worth less than a lower price with clean terms; compute payouts at several exit values.
6. Decide "rich versus king" up front: a founder who wants to keep control may accept a smaller raise or lower valuation; one who wants the largest outcome may give up control.
7. Use a lawyer who does venture deals; the term sheet is non-binding except for confidentiality and no-shop.

### 7. Run the process

Prepare deck, executive summary, financial model and data room; build a target list by fund fit (stage, sector, cheque size, fund size); approach through warm introductions; run meetings close together; expect most investors to pass; close the lead investor, then fill the round. Pitch-process tips, failure lessons and how to structure a small friends-and-family raise (units, staged calls) are in `cfo/references/raising-startup-capital/pitching-and-small-raises.md`.

## Worked pattern

A software venture with $30K monthly revenue needs $1.2M to reach $100K monthly.
- VC fit: bottom-up market of $400M and strong gross margins; founders want a large exit, so VC is appropriate.
- Instrument: a $1.2M post-money SAFE round with an $8M cap (15% dilution) rather than a priced round, to save time and legal cost.
- An alternative term sheet arrives: $1.2M at a $10M pre-money, but with a 2x participating preference and a 15% pool created before the round. At a $20M exit the investor would take 2.4 + 10.7% × 17.6 ≈ $4.3M (21%) versus $2.1M (10.7%) under a 1x non-participating preference. The pool shuffle also cuts the effective pre-money to $8.3M. The SAFE is the better deal unless the price can be raised with clean terms.

## Common mistakes

- Raising venture money for a business that cannot produce a venture-size exit.
- Raising too little to reach a milestone, forcing a weak next round.
- Judging an offer by the headline valuation; ignoring the option pool, preferences and participation.
- Not modelling SAFE and note conversions; stacking many SAFEs.
- Giving away board control or broad vetoes at seed stage.
- Chasing investors instead of customers.
- Talking to one investor at a time; signing a no-shop before terms are settled.
- Pitching a lender with upside and a VC with safety; each wants the other story.
- Spending founders' time chasing investors the venture cannot qualify for, instead of bootstrapping.
- Forgetting the asymmetry: an investor with a portfolio can rationally push for riskier swings than a founder with one company should take, and can use the founder's confidence to justify terms that pay the investor first ("if you are sure of the plan, you lose nothing").
