# Search playbook: raise, pick industries, source, diligence

Process detail behind the main skill, drawn from the Stanford search fund primer (2021 edition; the 2020 edition is the same in substance). Own words; examples are generic.

## 1. Before launching

- Self-test: attention to detail, persistence through rejection, ability to win trust from investors and owners, belief in one's own leadership, appetite for advice, flexibility on criteria, willingness to relocate and live on a modest salary.
- Partner or solo: decide early. If partnered, settle in writing: post-close roles; "yours, mine, ours" decisions and a tie-break for "ours"; non-starter industries or places; who reports to the board; what happens to equity if one leaves; hours, money needs, and what a great exit looks like for each.
- Failure modes ranked: buying a bad company is far worse than buying nothing. One investor's ordering: good business, then no business, then bad business.

## 2. Raising search capital

- Investor pool, in rough order of usefulness: professional search investors, high-net-worth people who have backed searches, former colleagues, business owners and executives, friends and family.
- Size each investor by the follow-on, not the unit: can they put $100K to $1M into the deal, not just $35K to $50K into the search? Alternatives are typically 5% to 15% of a wealthy individual's portfolio.
- Before accepting money, ask: how have they helped other searchers; do they want economic or voting control; will they sit on the board; where are they most useful; when do they need liquidity; quick flip or long hold?
- Offering document (private placement memorandum) contents: summary; how search funds work; search method and resources; industries and geographies; screening criteria; timeline; search budget; proposed acquisition security and coupon; illustrative returns model; exit routes; principals' backgrounds and roles.
- Meeting rules: research the investor first (other searchers who pitched them); listen more than pitch; answer unknowns quickly afterwards; ask for the commitment with a term sheet in hand; keep "not now" investors updated, they often join the deal round.
- Set up the CRM before fundraising and use it for investors and targets alike.

## 3. Choosing industries (funnel)

Idea generation → initial screen → information gathering → thesis → selection. Allow 1 to 2 months to cut a long list to 5 to 10, then pick the top 2 or 3.

- **Idea sources:** top-down lists (industry codes, growth-company lists; one searcher started from about 75 industries), megatrends (ageing population, outsourcing), own prior industry, deals that brokers show.
- **Scorecard:** score each industry 1 to 3 on about ten criteria (fragmented, growing, recurring revenue, sizeable, healthy margins, simple operations, entry barriers, low customer concentration, no consolidation wave, low exogenous risk). Sum to 30; cut the bottom. A simpler alternative: two or three must-haves (for example recurring revenue, scalable, at least 20 targets in the size band).
- **Thesis document** headings: industry description; size and growth; players and shares; business model and unit economics (ticket size, contract length, margin by line, capital intensity); five forces; exogenous risks and roll-up potential; megatrend link; go/no-go. Cap the time: three days each on ten industries is already a month.
- Validate the shortlist with investors, then become an insider: trade shows, owner meetings, customer and supplier interviews, a River Guide.

## 4. Sourcing

- **Mix:** most searchers spend about 75% of time on target industries and 25% on brokered deals.
- **Lists:** trade association members, trade-show exhibitors, private-company databases by industry code, outsourced list builders. Screen on revenue, owner age, headcount; treat self-reported figures with caution.
- **Outreach:** warm introduction beats a letter, a letter beats a cold call. Make the first sentence relevant (another CEO, a supplier, the association). Expect 10 to 20 voicemails per callback. Ask every owner who is not selling who might be.
- **Qualify the seller early:** state your size range and a multiple range up front. Signs a seller is serious: shares sensitive data, lets you meet staff, names a price, signs an exclusive LOI, spends own money on advisers.
- **River Guide** (retired CEO or association head): success fee about 0.5% to 1.0% of deal value, often capped. Agree term, fee trigger and timing, carve-outs for companies already known, and how to describe the fund.
- **Brokers:** build a broker list (hundreds to a thousand-plus), email criteria, repeat periodically. Brokered sellers want liquidity now, but expect auctions and adverse selection (funded PE may have passed). Publish a standard finder's fee so anyone can bring a deal.
- **Discipline:** weekly call and visit quotas; report activity to investors; keep sourcing until the closing documents are signed; show deals to lenders before making an offer.

## 5. From first look to close

| Stage | Goals | Typical commitment |
|---|---|---|
| First pass | Worth more time? Real seller? Rough value; key issues; position as a credible buyer | NDA; maybe an IOI in auctions |
| Valuation / LOI | Owner truly selling; deeper industry and customer analysis; value; major legal terms; prioritised diligence list; lender feedback; tell investors | LOI with price or formula, terms, exclusivity |
| Confirmatory diligence | Test offer assumptions; verify data; set final price and terms; secure financing; shape the 100-day plan | 30 to 120 days; outside advisers |

Whole process: commonly 4 to 12 months from introduction to close.

**A typical 120-day diligence calendar**
- Days 0 to 45: searcher does core business diligence; hires an industry expert for benchmarks; sends a preliminary investment memo to investors and gauges appetite; prepares the company for accounting, legal and environmental work.
- Days 46 to 90: accountants (quality of earnings), lawyers, environmental and other specialists start; lenders see the deal; purchase documents begin.
- Days 91 to 120: negotiate purchase agreement; firm up debt and equity; more time with managers; talk to key customers and suppliers; write the hour-by-hour first week and daily first month; sign, close, wire.

**Who helps with what:** industry consultants and River Guides (market); background-check firms and references (management); accountants and IT consultants (financial, systems); law firms (legal); insurance brokers (insurance, often free on the hope of the account); HR providers (people); environmental consultants (Phase I for owned sites). Do the core work yourself; outsource QofE, legal, environmental and IP. Many lawyers defer fees to closing; accountants and consultants usually do not.

**Working with the seller:** explain each stage and who will ask for what (lenders and investors run parallel diligence); say what you need to learn and let the seller suggest existing reports rather than firing a long request list; push the seller towards an experienced M&A lawyer, since an inexperienced one slows things down.

**Sort the findings into four bins:** deal killers (results far from reported, major hidden liability, ethics, unbridgeable add-back gap, business cannot run without the seller, big unplanned investment, broken controls); price issues (working-capital shortfall dollar for dollar, earnings shortfall at the multiple; bridge with earn-out, seller note or seller equity); terms issues (specific indemnity, bigger escrow, offset against seller note, lower basket, higher cap, longer survival, more reps, closing conditions; exclude assets or liabilities in an asset deal); operating items for the 100-day plan. Put a minimum and maximum impact on each issue.
