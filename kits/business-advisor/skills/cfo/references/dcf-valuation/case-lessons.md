# DCF valuation: lessons from teaching cases

Part of `cfo/references/dcf-valuation.md`. Generalised lessons from valuation cases. No case answers are given; each lesson is written so it applies to any business.

## Growth paths funded by an investor (an expansion-financing case)

- Value each path (grow on your own, or raise money and enter market A, B or C) as a whole-firm
  forecast, then compare it with the do-nothing-new path. The decision rides on the difference.
- Expect two forecasts: the investor's and management's. They can differ by a factor of two or
  more. Value both; the investor's sets the price of the stake, the owner's sets what the owner
  thinks they keep (see `cfo/references/valuation-multiples-and-deals.md`, section 7).
- Watch for depreciation buried in cost of sales. Add it back once, then subtract capex.
- When capex, depreciation and working capital are given as a percentage of sales, the FCF
  margin is fixed; value is then driven almost entirely by the growth path and the rate.
- Read the customer list: one customer at a third or more of revenue is a risk to price into the
  forecast or the rate, and a reason an investor will push for a bigger stake.
- Bigger is not always better: the market that needs the most capital can produce the most
  revenue and still leave the owner with less once dilution is counted.

## A bid for a package of assets (a broadcasting roll-up case)

- Set a price range with two ends:
  - **Floor (pre-emptive):** the stand-alone DCF value of the targets, so the seller has no
    reason to wait for another bidder.
  - **Ceiling (no dilution):** the most that leaves the buyer's value per share unchanged, which
    is roughly the buyer's own trading multiple. If the buyer's price already reflects the
    market's hope of this deal, that ceiling is inflated; paying it hands the gain to the seller.
- In an asset purchase the target's working capital usually stays with the seller. The buyer
  funds the opening working capital, and it belongs in year 0 of the cash flows.
- Add maintenance capex per unit acquired (per site, per station, per store) even when the
  industry's headline profit measure leaves it out.
- Industry profit measures (broadcast cash flow, store-level EBITDA) often exclude corporate
  overhead. Multiples quoted on them are not comparable with EV/EBITDA until overhead is charged.
- A turnaround record (bought at a high multiple of current profit, now a low multiple of
  improved profit) is the buyer's real edge; value the improvement separately from the base so
  you know how much of the price depends on it.
- Asset betas from peers: unlever with a debt beta (a small positive number), not zero, when
  peers carry heavy debt.

## A newly listed growth company (an IPO aftermath case)

- Build revenue from drivers: users × revenue per user, or users × ads per user × price per ad.
  Check each driver against the most similar mature peers; a plan that needs the newcomer to
  match the leader's revenue per user is a bet, not a base case.
- Line up the analysts' DCFs: their discount rates, terminal growth and year-5 cash flows can
  differ widely (in such cases the free cash flow forecasts disagree far more than revenue).
  The spread tells you how little the market price pins down.
- When most of the value sits beyond year 10, run a reverse DCF: what growth and margin does
  today's price need? Then judge whether that is plausible.
- Subtract stock-based pay as a cost; it is how such firms pay staff.
- Convert the price target into an implied multiple of next year's revenue and compare it with
  peers. A target several times the peer multiple needs a stated reason.
- Read who wrote the report. Analysts at the underwriting banks may face pressures; compare their
  views with those of independent analysts.
- Many non-voting or low-voting shares: outside holders cannot change management, so there is no
  control value to add.

## A project with its own debt (a home-delivery launch case)

- Get the project's asset beta from companies in the new line of business, not from the parent.
- Decide how project debt will behave before choosing the method: a fixed amount (kept or paid
  down on a schedule) calls for APV; debt reset to a fixed share of value calls for WACC.
  Both should give the same answer if done consistently.
- If start-up costs are partly expensed and partly capitalised, the tax timing differs; check
  which treatment the forecast assumes.

## An acquisition with buyout-style leverage (a wireless-carrier case)

- Size the opening debt by what lenders allow (coverage of EBITDA over interest, a cap on debt to
  value), then pay it down to the industry's normal leverage. Value the paydown years with APV
  and the years after with a WACC terminal value at the long-run ratio.
- Value non-operating items apart from operations: cash used to fund the deal comes out of the
  forecast; stakes in companies you do not control are valued by a multiple of their earnings
  when you cannot forecast their cash flows.
- Rank synergies by how sure they are: cost savings first (they are in your control), revenue
  gains second (they need customers to act). Show the stand-alone value, then the value with
  synergies.
- Terminal value two ways: a growing perpetuity and an exit multiple. Long-run growth should
  equal return on new capital × share of profit reinvested; growth with no reinvestment is not
  free.
- Do not apply a private-company discount to a large, profitable firm that could list; the
  discount is about illiquidity and fits small owners who cannot sell (see
  `cfo/references/private-company-valuation.md`).
- Paying too much and taking on too much debt are the two ways such deals destroy value.
