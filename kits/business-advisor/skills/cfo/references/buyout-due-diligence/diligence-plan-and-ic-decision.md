# Planning diligence and making the call

How practitioners (venture investors in healthcare and technology, and buyout partners) sequence diligence and turn it into a yes or no; the frames below apply to any acquisition.

## Sequence diligence in phases
1. **Get the basic facts.**
2. **Look for non-starters** first and stop early if one appears.
3. **Write the diligence plan:** the few high-priority questions, who answers each (own team, industry experts, third parties), and a tracker of request, owner, status and finding.
4. **Phase one: test the thesis.** Market and adoption, fit with your strategy, the company's capabilities, conversations with existing and prospective customers and partners, a first financial review, the economic model and its levers, pipeline and growth plan.
5. **Phase two: detailed review with third parties.** Final structure; operations by site and market; back office and people; ability to scale; full technology review; legal, regulatory and compliance; full financials (QofE); any sector-specific review the owner group requires.
6. **Pressure-test the thesis**, then write the recommendation.

Use outside experts who know the work: practitioners (clinicians in healthcare, engineers in manufacturing), customers and buyers of the product, and experienced operators.

## Write the thesis in one sentence
"We believe X will happen because Y, even though Z." Z names the main risk out loud, which keeps confirmation bias in check.

## The conclusions a deal must reach
- The leader is honest and capable.
- The market is large and wants the product.
- The company does something better than anyone else, it lasts, and it shows up across the business.
- The economics can work and the target return is reachable.
- The return comes from execution you control, not luck.

## A seven-factor scorecard (rate each very weak to very strong)
1. Macro economy: cycle position and long-term trend.
2. Industry: structure and attractiveness, cycles, secular trends.
3. Company position: competitive position, bargaining power with suppliers and customers, unit economics and return on capital.
4. Current momentum: recent quarters, visibility over the next 12 to 18 months.
5. Changes the new owner will make: strategic and operational.
6. Management: inherited versus newly placed.
7. Price and structure: versus current comparables, versus history, in absolute terms, and downside protection from structure.

## Frame the committee memo
- **Clear positives, clear negatives, and "what really matters":** two or three judgements that will decide the outcome. Update the "what really matters" list at each committee meeting.
- **Return drivers:** earnings growth, debt paydown or cash yield, and buying cheaper than you sell. Two of the three make a good deal; all three a great one.
- **Hold yourself to the year-one plan.** Projections beyond about 18 months are guesswork; years four and five are false precision. A few big drivers decide the result.
- **Yes or no matters more than 100 versus 110:** in a leveraged, illiquid hold of four to six years, getting the decision right outweighs a 10% difference in price.
- **Price on a continuum:** before deal fever sets in, write down the price you would love, the price you would like, and the most you would pay. Respect the ceiling.
- **Absolute value over relative value:** comparables reflect the market's mood; cross-check with cash-flow value or replacement cost.

## Investment memo outline
Diligence summary; thesis; highlights; risks with how each will be mitigated and tracked; return profile. After closing, compare the first board meeting and first year against the memo: surprises are lessons for the next diligence.

## Lessons from failed buyouts (costly mistakes)
- Timing just before a financial crisis with heavy leverage (a casino buyout).
- Over-exposure to one commodity price (a power utility buyout hurt by falling natural gas prices).
- Scale economies from a roll-up that never came, after a key partner dropped out (a cinema roll-up).
- Exposure to a cyclical end market (auto sales), saved only by outside support.
- A category leader losing share to bigger general retailers and online sellers while carrying LBO debt, leaving nothing to invest in the change (a toy retailer).

Each was findable in diligence: stress-test the deal against the cycle, the main commodity, the synergy case and the secular trend.
