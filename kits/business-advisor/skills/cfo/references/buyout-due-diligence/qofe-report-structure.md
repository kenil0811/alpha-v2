# Quality of earnings: report structure and working file

How a buyer-side financial and tax due diligence (QofE) report is laid out, and the working file behind it. Based on a typical report for a small owner-run business, and the question workbook that goes with it. Use it to scope a QofE, or to run a light one yourself on a small deal.

## Report outline
1. **Restricted-use notice and engagement letter:** agreed-upon procedures only, not an audit or review; relies on management's information; for the buyer's internal use. (Lenders usually need their own reliance letter; standard practice.)
2. **Definitions:** periods covered (two full years plus the interim period, and LTM), units.
3. **Executive summary:** background; scope and the proposed transaction; **key observations** (one page or two, each with a recommendation); **open items** (documents still not received).
4. **Financial due diligence**
   - QofE, management's adjustments: reported revenue and EBITDA by period, each add-back the seller proposes, the firm's view on each.
   - QofE, diligence adjustments: further adjustments the firm makes, up or down, with non-quantified ("NQ") ones flagged where data is too poor to measure.
   - Proof of cash: revenue traced to bank deposits.
   - Reconciliations: internal P&L to tax returns, and to reviewed or audited statements.
   - Income statement: monthly revenue and gross margin; top customers by year; revenue by category; cost of sales (recast where the seller's definition is wrong); operating expenses; monthly reported versus adjusted EBITDA.
   - Balance sheet: net working capital by month, adjusted (this sets the peg); LTM detail; receivables ageing; fixed-asset additions (a capex history).
   - Accounting and other: who keeps the books, close timetable, revenue recognition policy, payroll, bonuses, benefits, paid time off, staffing and subcontractors, facilities and leases, contingent liabilities (warranties, customer deposits).
5. **Tax due diligence:** returns reviewed, audits and notices, entity status, book-to-tax differences, state nexus and sales tax exposure.
6. **Appendices:** support for each large adjustment; monthly recast gross profit.

## Adjustment types to expect
**Management's (usually upward):** owner pay, charitable gifts and other personal costs.

**Diligence (either way):**
- Owner pay replaced by a market-rate manager rather than removed entirely.
- Inventory first put on the balance sheet at year end, which flatters that year's margin; rebuild balances with a turns analysis.
- Cash-basis payables: bills recorded when paid, so the books show no payables and expenses lag a month.
- Revenue billed on invoice (including deposits) instead of percentage of completion on fixed-bid jobs; materials expensed up front; over and under billings.
- Bonus and payroll accruals missing from monthly numbers.
- Accrued vacation never recorded.
- Related-party rent below market (an owner's building): normalise to market.
- Professional fees normalised for the new owner's reporting needs.
- Pro forma effects of a recent expansion, shown separately and treated with care.

**Working-capital adjustments:** remove cash and undeposited funds, rebuild inventory, add unrecorded payables and payroll and bonus accruals, so the peg is set on true accrual working capital.

## Key observations worth looking for in any small company
- Departures from GAAP that mismatch revenue and cost (job businesses especially).
- No job-cost or work-in-progress schedule, so job profitability and loss jobs cannot be seen. The sample report recommended requiring a list of open jobs before closing and a purchase-agreement mechanism against loss-making jobs.
- Cost of sales that leaves out direct labour, overstating gross margin.
- Customer concentration (in the sample, two customers made about half of revenue).
- Tax status problems. In the sample, unequal distributions to two equal owners could be treated as a second class of stock, which voids S-corporation status; because the deal was an asset purchase, the buyer did not inherit that exposure. In a stock deal it would be a price or indemnity issue.

## The working file (question log)
Set it up as a workbook with:
- Monthly P&L and balance sheet trended across all periods, with a percent-of-revenue view and year-over-year change columns.
- A "questions" column and a "management response" column beside every line that moves unusually.
- Adjusted EBITDA bridge.
- Customer revenue by year, with questions on each big swing (new customer? lost customer? one-off project?).
- Project and project-cost questions (largest jobs, margin per job, loss jobs).
- Receivables listing and ageing with questions on old balances.
- Raw "provided by client" (PBC) data tabs kept separate from analysis tabs.

## How to use the result
- Price the deal on diligence-adjusted EBITDA, not management-adjusted.
- Set the working-capital peg on the adjusted monthly average.
- Turn each key observation into a price change, a specific indemnity or escrow, a closing condition, or a 100-day-plan item.
- Chase open items before signing; an open item at signing is a risk the buyer has accepted.
