# Inventory, PPE, R&D and restructuring: quality tests and adjustments

Depth for `cfo/references/earnings-quality.md` (cases I and II). From standard textbook material on revenue and operating income, and on operating assets, restated; examples
in our own numbers.

## 1. Inventory cost flow

- FIFO, LIFO and average cost are all allowed under US GAAP; IFRS forbids LIFO. The method need
  not match the physical flow of goods, and one company may use different methods for different
  inventories or countries.
- With rising costs, LIFO gives higher cost of sales, lower profit, lower inventory and lower tax.
  The tax saving is real cash; the LIFO conformity rule makes a firm using LIFO for tax use it in
  its accounts too.
- **LIFO reserve** (disclosed) = FIFO inventory − LIFO inventory. Convert before comparing with a
  FIFO peer or with the firm's own history:
  - Inventory: + reserve.
  - Tax liability: + reserve × tax rate (cumulative tax deferred so far).
  - Retained earnings: + reserve × (1 − tax rate).
  - FIFO cost of sales = LIFO cost of sales − increase in the reserve (+ a decrease).
- **LIFO liquidation:** when quantities fall, old cheap layers flow into cost of sales and lift
  gross margin for one period. The notes disclose the effect; remove it before forecasting
  margins. A shrinking reserve with falling quantities is the signal.
- **Lower of cost or market:** write-downs go into cost of sales (not SG&A). GAAP never reverses
  them; IFRS can. A big write-down followed by unusually high margins can be a "big bath".
- Retail inventory method: ending inventory at retail × cost-to-retail ratio. Watch the ratio.

Example: LIFO inventory 400, reserve 120 (was 100), cost of sales 2,000, tax rate 25%. FIFO
inventory 520; tax liability + 30; retained earnings + 90; FIFO cost of sales 1,980, so pre-tax
profit + 20.

## 2. Gross margin and days

- Gross margin falls when: the mix shifts to lower-margin products; new products launch at low
  prices; volume drops so fixed overhead spreads over fewer units; supply-chain, freight or
  material costs rise and cannot be passed on; discounts and return policies loosen; stock goes
  obsolete; the economy weakens; regulation adds costs. Find which, from the MD&A.
- Separate what management controls (mix, supply chain, shrink) from what it does not
  (accounting changes, an extra week, currency).
- Days inventory = 365 / (cost of sales / average inventory). The ending-inventory version
  (365 × ending inventory / cost of sales) answers a different question: how long the current
  stock will last. Pick the one that fits the question.
- More days can mean slow-moving or obsolete stock, or a deliberate move to higher-margin,
  slower-selling lines (often after an acquisition). Fewer days can come from just-in-time
  supply, demand-pull production and better forecasting, which are good, or from starving the
  shelves, which is not.
- Cash freed = change in days × cost of sales per day.
- 53-week years: scale cost of sales and sales by 52/53 before computing turnovers.

## 3. Property, plant and equipment

- Capitalise everything needed to put the asset in service (price, freight, duties, installation,
  testing). Depreciation rests on three estimates: life, salvage value and method.
- **Average useful life** ≈ average depreciable cost / depreciation expense, where depreciable
  cost = gross PPE − land − construction in progress. Longer lives than peers mean lower expense
  and higher profit.
- **Percent used up** = accumulated depreciation / average depreciable cost. About 50% is normal
  for steady replacement; well above it signals a coming wave of capital spending, higher repair
  costs and less efficient assets. Feed this into the capex forecast.
- **PPE turnover** = sales / average net PPE. Raise it by selling idle assets, sharing facilities
  through alliances or joint ventures, outsourcing production, or sale-and-leaseback; each is a
  strategic step, not an accounting one.
- **Gains and losses on disposal** are transitory and also say past depreciation was wrong (a gain
  means too much was charged). Optionally spread the gain back over the years the asset was held:
  a 100 gain on an asset held 5 years means pre-tax profit was about 20 too low each year.
- **Impairment (US GAAP):** if the undiscounted expected cash flows fall below book value, write
  down to fair value. IFRS tests against discounted cash flows in one step (so it impairs more
  often) and allows reversals. An impairment is catch-up depreciation: earlier profits were too
  high and this year's charge is too large. Many analysts leave it, but note it qualitatively.

## 4. Research and development

- US GAAP expenses R&D as incurred, except facilities and equipment with other uses. IFRS
  capitalises development (not research) once technical feasibility, intent and ability to
  finish, a market and reliable cost measurement are shown; IFRS peers then show more intangibles
  and higher early profit.
- Track R&D in dollars and as a % of sales against history and peers. A falling percentage can be
  a revenue jump or a cut to prop up margins; a cut in R&D or advertising buys short-term profit at
  long-term cost.
- Because R&D is expensed while its payoff is not recorded, R&D-heavy firms trade well above book
  value.

## 5. Restructuring

- Three parts: severance and relocation (accrued when a board-approved plan names the affected
  employees; under IFRS when the employees expect it), asset write-downs (no cash), and other exit
  costs (lease and contract termination, fees).
- The note rolls the liability forward: beginning + new charges − cash paid − write-downs used −
  reversals = ending. **Reversals of earlier charges flow back into profit**, and research finds
  they cluster in years when profit would otherwise have fallen. Repeated reversals or top-ups
  cost management credibility.
- Restructuring is usually excluded from "adjusted" earnings as one-time; a firm that restructures
  every year has a recurring cost.
- Analyst option: reverse the charge and spread it over the years that benefited (severance over
  the employees' recent years, write-downs over the assets' recent lives), adjusting tax and
  retained earnings. Example: a 90 severance charge spread over 3 years raises this year's pre-tax
  profit by 60 and lowers each of the two prior years by 30.
