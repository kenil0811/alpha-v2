# Non-GAAP earnings, transitory items and red flags

Depth for `cfo/references/earnings-quality.md`. From standard textbook material on
revenue and operating income, including a survey of chief financial officers on
earnings quality (Dichev, Graham, Harvey and Rajgopal, 2013), restated in our own words.

## 1. Transitory items

- **Discontinued operations** (a sold or held-for-sale business that marks a strategic shift) show
  the unit's results and the gain or loss on its sale below continuing income, and its assets and
  liabilities apart. Use **income from continuing operations** for ROE, trends and forecasts.
- Gains on asset sales, impairments, restructuring and litigation charges are usually transitory.
  Remove them for forecasting, but keep them in view: write-downs and accruals often record the
  results of poor past decisions.
- Tax expense distorted by a law change or one-off settlements: forecast with the rate the
  company guides to, or the statutory rate, not the year's effective rate.

## 2. Reading "adjusted" (non-GAAP) earnings

Companies may publish their own profit measures if they reconcile them to GAAP. The measure shows
performance as management would like it read. Before using one:

1. Read the auditor's report for anything other than a clean opinion.
2. Read the accounting policies note and compare with peers; unusual policies are a warning.
3. Check policy and estimate changes: what would the old policy have reported? Did the change
   avoid a loss or a covenant breach?
4. Compare key ratios over time and with peers; follow up big changes and also ratios that stay
   flat when conditions changed (an expense ratio held constant through a downturn can mean
   expenses were hidden).
5. List each excluded item. Is it really one-time? Do peers exclude the same things? Recurring
   "one-time" charges, excluded stock compensation, or excluded acquisition amortisation where
   acquisitions are the strategy all overstate sustainable profit.
6. Be most sceptical of invented metrics that turn a loss into a profit (forms of contribution
   margin or "adjusted EBITDA" that leave out real operating costs, billings or backlog counted as
   if earned).
7. Recast the GAAP statements to a policy closer to peers or to the economics, and use that.

## 3. Red flags for misreported earnings

From the survey, grouped. Executives estimated that around a fifth of public firms use the
latitude in the rules to misstate earnings, and that a third of misstatements reduce profit
(saving it for later). The main motives were the share price, hitting benchmarks and pay.

- **Cash versus earnings:** earnings and operating cash flow moving in opposite directions for
  six to eight quarters; accruals, assets or working capital growing much faster or slower than
  revenue; large changes in accruals or capitalised costs without explanation.
- **Benchmarks and smoothness:** always meeting or just beating targets; earnings smoother than
  the economy or the industry; wide swings with no change in the business; sudden breaks from the
  historical pattern.
- **Deviations from peers:** cash cycle, margins, growth, impairment frequency or disclosure
  level out of line with the industry; weak sales growth or slipping position against peers.
- **One-time items:** large or frequent restructuring, write-downs, or gains and losses.
- **Estimates and policies:** frequent policy changes; aggressive long-term estimates with little
  explanation; repeated restatements and prior-period adjustments.
- **Balance sheet:** inventory building up faster than cost of sales; receivables building up and
  DSO lengthening; rising debt and liabilities.
- **People and disclosure:** high executive turnover or a sudden change at the top; big bonuses
  followed by departures; filings and MD&A becoming less clear, notes more complex.

Use the list as a screen: two or three flags together justify the full adjustment work in
`cfo/references/earnings-quality.md`; one alone usually has an innocent explanation.
