# Revenue, allowances and receivables: quality tests

Depth for `cfo/references/earnings-quality.md` (cases III and IV). From standard textbook material on revenue recognition and operating income, restated; examples in our own
numbers.

## 1. When revenue is earned

Revenue is recognised when control of the good or service passes to the customer; cash need not
have arrived. Five steps: identify the contract; identify each distinct promise (performance
obligation); set the price (estimate any variable part); allocate it to the promises by
stand-alone selling prices; recognise each part when (or as) it is delivered.

Arrangements that need care, and where the aggressive error usually lies:

| Arrangement | Correct treatment | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Non-refundable up-front fee (setup, joining, activation) | Usually a prepayment for later service: defer and spread | Whole fee taken at signing |
| Bill-and-hold | Revenue when control passes, often at later shipment | Billing goods still in the seller's warehouse |
| Agent (marketplace, consignment) | Commission only (net) | Gross sales booked when the seller bears no inventory, credit or price risk |
| Licence of IP | Right to use: at the start. Right to access over time: spread | Access-type licence booked up front |
| Bundle (product plus service) | Split by stand-alone prices; service part spread | Discount loaded onto the service so more lands up front |
| Variable consideration (rebates, royalties, volume discounts) | Estimate the expected amount | Optimistic estimates of what will be kept |
| Right of return | Deduct expected returns from sales | Returns reserve shrinking relative to sales |
| Gift cards, deposits, extended warranties sold separately | Liability until used or over the cover period | Breakage (unused balances) taken early |
| Long-term contract | Over time, by costs incurred / total expected costs | Total cost estimate too low, so percentage complete and profit run ahead |

**Long-term contracts (cost-to-cost).** Percentage complete = costs to date / current estimate of
total costs. A contract priced 2,000 with expected costs 1,600 and costs to date 400 is 25%
complete: revenue 500, gross profit 100. If the cost estimate later rises to 1,800, earlier
profit was overstated and the catch-up hits the current year. Costs beyond billings sit as a
contract asset; billings or advances beyond revenue sit as a contract liability. Remaining-cost
estimates are hard for auditors to test: treat steady margins on troubled projects with doubt.

**New standards break comparability.** When a revenue (or lease) standard is adopted without
restating prior years, growth rates across the change mix two methods. Use the note that sizes
the effect and the cumulative adjustment to retained earnings.

## 2. Sales returns and discount allowances

- Roll the allowance forward: beginning + additions charged against sales − returns and credits
  taken = ending.
- Test: additions / gross sales, year by year. A ratio that falls while the business and its
  channels are unchanged lifts net sales.
- Adjust: apply the multi-year average ratio to each year's gross sales; the difference moves net
  sales and pre-tax profit; tax it; carry the cumulative after-tax effect into retained earnings
  (the latest balance sheet already reflects all years).
- Context: allowances are heaviest in commoditised industries with strong retail buyers.
  Shifting to direct-to-consumer sales raises gross margin and changes the allowance mix.

## 3. Receivables and the allowance for doubtful accounts

Magnitude:

- Receivable turnover = sales / average receivables; **DSO** = 365 / turnover.
- Compare DSO with the stated credit terms, with history and with peers.
- DSO rising means looser credit to chase sales, weaker customers, or a mix shift to slow payers.
  Read the MD&A before concluding.
- Cash effect of a change = change in days × sales per day (sales / 365). Ten days faster on 3,650
  of sales frees 100 once.
- Ways to collect faster: screen customers better, take deposits or progress payments, use
  automatic debits, ship exactly what was ordered (fewer disputes), chase overdue accounts
  sooner, and use payment-history data to set terms by customer.

Quality:

- Gross receivables = net + allowance. Allowance / gross receivables is the reserve ratio.
- Aging method: bucket balances by days past due and apply loss rates that rise with age.
- Bad-debt expense comes only from adding to the allowance; write-offs use up the allowance and
  do not touch profit. So the lever for profit is the size of the addition.
- Roll the allowance forward: beginning + additions (expense) − write-offs = ending. If write-offs
  exceed additions for several years and the reserve ratio falls while gross receivables do not,
  either credit quality improved or the reserve is being run down to support profit. Compare the
  ratio with peers and with the economy to decide.
- Adjust: reset the allowance each year to the multi-year average reserve ratio; the yearly
  change moves bad-debt expense; flow the after-tax total to retained earnings.

Example: gross receivables 1,000 then 1,100; allowance 60 then 45 (6.0% then 4.1%). Additions 20,
write-offs 35. If peers hold 6% and customers have not improved, the ending allowance should be
66, so this year's expense is understated by 21 before tax (about 16 after 25% tax), and net
receivables and equity are overstated by the same.

Under IFRS receivables are financial assets carried at discounted value where discounting is
material; check the rate when comparing turnover with US firms.

## 4. Deferred (unearned) revenue

- Roll forward: beginning + cash received in advance − revenue recognised from the balance =
  ending. Cash collected in advance = ending − beginning + revenue recognised from it.
- A falling balance means this year's revenue came from earlier prepayments and fewer new ones
  arrived: a leading signal of slower revenue. A rising balance supports future revenue.
- Common in subscriptions, cloud software, gift cards, memberships, extended protection plans and
  customer deposits.

## 5. Foreign currency

- A weaker home currency inflates translated revenue, costs and profit of foreign subsidiaries;
  a stronger one shrinks them. Most of this is translation, not cash.
- Realised gains and losses arise on foreign-currency sales, purchases, borrowings and
  repatriated cash; companies often hedge them.
- Use the MD&A's currency effect to compute **constant-currency growth** = (revenue − currency
  effect) / prior revenue − 1, and compute key ratios with and without it. Large currency effects
  year after year suggest weak hedging; ask why.
