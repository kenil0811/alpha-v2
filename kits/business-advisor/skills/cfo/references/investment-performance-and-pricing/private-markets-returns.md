# Private markets returns: judging PE and VC as a fund investor

From practitioner views on how a limited partner (LP) builds a private equity programme (2019, 2021),
analysis of forward PE returns against public markets (2021), and a foundation's published review of its
VC fund portfolio (2012). Figures are dated; recheck before quoting.

## How the money moves
- **Vehicles:** direct deals, a fund (blind pool), a fund of funds, co-investment beside a fund, or buying
  an existing fund stake second-hand (secondary).
- **Strategies:** venture, growth, buyout, mezzanine, distressed, real estate, natural resources, infrastructure.
- **Fund life:** fundraising takes 6 to 18 months; investing runs about years 0 to 5; realising runs to year
  10 and often 12 to 15. Capital is called as deals happen, not on day one.
- **J-curve:** early years show negative returns (fees and costs before any exits), then rise as exits come.
  Be wary when interim returns peak early, around the time the manager raises the next fund.
- **Terms:** management fee about 1.5 to 2% a year on committed capital; carry about 20% of profit; GP
  commitment often only about 1%. No liquidity, little transparency, hard to benchmark.
- **Waterfall:** LPs get capital back, then a preferred return, then the GP catches up, then profits split 80/20.

## Measuring returns
- **IRR** is time-weighted by when cash moves, so early quick wins inflate it and the GP controls timing.
  **TVPI** (total value / paid-in) and **DPI** (distributions / paid-in) show the multiple; DPI is cash
  actually returned, TVPI includes unrealised marks (standard practice for the
  TVPI/DPI definitions). Report both an IRR and a multiple.
- **PME (public market equivalent):** put the fund's calls and distributions into a comparable public index
  on the same dates and compare the end values. Above 1 (or a positive spread) means the fund beat the index
  after fees. Choose the index to match risk: small-cap for VC and buyout, not only the S&P 500.
- Pooled IRRs from data providers (e.g. Cambridge Associates) are the common benchmark against the S&P 500
  and Russell 2000.
- **Stale marks:** private holdings are valued quarterly by the manager, audited yearly, and lean toward cost,
  so they understate volatility and lag public falls. Compare correlation, not reported volatility.

## Is PE really better? (the 2021 debate)
- Long windows (15 to 25 years) show PE well ahead of PME; recent windows show the edge shrinking to about
  one percentage point. Which window to trust is the real argument:
  - Shorter-run case: more money chasing fewer deals, record multiples, rising rates.
  - Longer-run case: PE multiples still below the S&P, public return expectations also low (high CAPE), diversification.
- **Forward public returns:** the cyclically adjusted P/E (CAPE, 10-year earnings) has some predictive power;
  very high readings preceded weak real returns over the next 10 to 20 years. So judge PE against low
  public expectations, not historic averages.
- Outperformance comes mostly from **top-quartile funds**. Persistence of top-quartile performance into the
  next fund was strong in older studies but has faded for funds raised after 2000, so past quartile alone
  is a weak selection signal.
- **Where excess return may come from:** small-company premium, value premium, illiquidity premium,
  leverage and deleveraging, sector and company selection, information gaps, active ownership. Sceptics
  argue much of it (small size, value, leverage) can be replicated with public stocks more cheaply, and that
  evidence for operational improvement is the weakest. Test a manager's claim against that cheaper replica.

## Choosing managers and cutting cost (LP tools)
- The spread between good and bad managers is far wider than in public equity (top versus bottom quartile
  roughly +6% versus -5% a year in PE, against under 1% either way for large-cap stock managers). Manager
  selection is the main decision.
- Four levers, each with its own new risk:
  1. **Vintage-year spread:** commit steadily every year rather than timing the cycle.
  2. **Mix of strategies** across fund types.
  3. **Manager selection:** sector focus, concentration, emerging versus established, niche; team, culture,
     process and alignment (GP commitment, fee terms). Specialists have beaten generalists in their area
     (one 2014 study: about 2.2x versus 1.9x multiple). Ask for net PME, fund life to date, the manager's
     own economics.
  4. **Fee reduction:** cut out intermediaries, co-invest or go direct, negotiate on size, take first-close
     or first-time-fund discounts.
- Fund strategy should hang together: the business model, investment thesis, team make-up and fundraising
  should all point the same way (a regional fund should be based in that region).

## The denominator effect
Institutional investors hold a target share in private markets with a band (say 20%, range 17.5 to 22.5%).
When public markets fall, total assets shrink while private marks lag, so the private share jumps above the
band. Example in our own numbers: $1,000M split 800 public / 200 PE; public falls 20% to 640, PE still marked
200, so PE is 200/840 = 24%, over the cap.
- Remedies are poor: selling fund stakes on the secondary market goes at deep discounts in a crisis; waiting
  for distributions fails because exits slow while capital calls continue; defaulting on calls is penalised.
  The usable lever is to stop committing to new funds, which makes PE fundraising cyclical and favours
  managers with capital at the start of a downturn.
- The reverse (public markets surge) leaves investors under-allocated; they catch up slowly. Fast private
  write-ups (the numerator effect, often VC) push the share up too; most investors tolerate that drift.
