# Small-deal LOI, price mechanics and diligence lists

For a self-funded buyer of a business worth roughly $1M to $10M. Built from an acquisition teaching case (a searcher buying a thin-margin regional distributor with a bank loan, a personal guarantee and an earn-out), a published search-fund primer's evaluation chapter, and practitioner guidance on financial due diligence. The case questions are not answered here; these are the lessons that generalise.

## Questions the case trains you to ask

1. **Where does the growth come from?** A plan that assumes recent growth continues needs a named source: new accounts, new lines, price, territory. Past growth on a small base is weak evidence.
2. **Can margins really rise?** Separate savings you control (an owner salary or a general manager the new owner replaces) from hopes (dropping low-margin lines, renegotiating supplier terms).
3. **What multiple am I really paying?** State it four ways and pick one consistently: cash at close or cash plus expected earn-out, over actual EBITDA or over pro forma EBITDA after your planned savings. Paying a multiple of savings you have not yet made hands the seller your value creation.
4. **Who has power over this business?** One supplier providing most volume, non-exclusive distribution rights, or a few salespeople holding the accounts are risks that a 1% to 2% net margin cannot absorb.
5. **Would I buy it, at this price, with my own guarantee?** Personal guarantees on bank debt make downside cases personal.

## LOI checklist for a self-funded buyer

Write it with a lawyer's template, but do not drop protections to save fees or time.
- Price and form: cash at close, seller note (rate, term, interest-only period, subordination to the bank), earn-out (metric, period, cap, how it is measured, accounting rules).
- Asset or stock purchase; excluded assets and liabilities.
- **Working capital peg:** a target level (receivables plus inventory less payables, often a trailing average) with a dollar-for-dollar true-up at close, because a seller can collect early, run down stock or stretch payables before closing. A peg protects quantity, not quality: also get reps on collectability of receivables and saleability of inventory, or a reserve.
- **Exclusivity (no-shop)** for a stated period, even when closing looks close.
- **Escrow or holdback,** or a right to offset claims against the seller note or earn-out. An earn-out alone is weak security if the business underperforms.
- Seller transition role, hours and pay; non-compete and non-solicit.
- Conditions: financing, satisfactory diligence, key contracts and leases assigned, landlord and lender consents.
- Deposit or break fee only if the seller insists (often none in small deals).
- Timetable and access to staff, customers and suppliers.

## Financing notes

- Bank term loan sized off trailing cash flow, often with a separate revolving line for working capital. In a distributor that pays suppliers on delivery and collects later, the revolver matters as much as the term loan.
- Pass-through entities (S corporations, LLCs) pay no entity tax, but the owner owes tax on the profit: model those distributions as a cash outflow before debt service.
- Sources and uses must show any existing debt refinanced at close.

## Starter document request list (small company)

Financial: three years of statements and tax returns; monthly P&L and balance sheet; general ledger; AR and AP ageing; inventory listing and count method; fixed-asset list and recent capex; debt agreements and payment schedules; payroll reports; owner's personal expenses run through the business.
Commercial: sales by customer and by supplier or product line; price lists and markups; sample invoices; contracts and distribution agreements; marketing materials.
Operations and legal: leases (property, vehicles, equipment); insurance policies and claims; employee handbook, org chart, key employee pay; permits; litigation; related-party arrangements.

## Testing the seller's add-backs (financial diligence)

Purchase price and loan size both hang on adjusted EBITDA, and the price is usually trued up for net working capital against a target. Before paying for a quality-of-earnings report, do a first pass yourself:
- Tie the starting EBITDA to the seller's marketing document, then demand invoices or other support for every proposed adjustment. Check that the amounts actually hit the P&L.
- Read the monthly P&L and balance sheet for spikes, negative expenses or balances, margin drift, and whether equity rolls forward month to month.
- Reconcile internal P&L to tax returns and to any reviewed or audited statements; unexplained gaps often become adjustments.
- Receivables and payables: items over 90 days, credit balances in AR or debit balances in AP, rebates given or received, terms.
- Inventory: costing method, whether labour, overhead and freight are capitalised, how often it is counted, slow-moving and obsolete stock.
- Fixed assets: age, upcoming capex, repairs capitalised or purchases expensed.
- Customers: top accounts, recent or pending losses, related-party sales and whether they continue after close, locations that create tax exposure.
- Common adjustments: owner pay to market; personal and related-party costs; deal fees; genuinely one-time items; audit adjustments never booked; reserve and accrual catch-ups; capitalised costs; payroll accruals. Run-rate adjustments (for example, a new lease) should be applied both ways, costs as well as savings.
- Be sceptical, ask until answers make sense, and keep materiality in view.
