# Incentives and information

> **Area:** `incentives-and-information` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Fix problems that come from one side knowing more than the other - bad customers or suppliers self-selecting in, people slacking or taking risks once covered, and employees or managers pursuing their own goals - with screening, signals, monitoring and pay-for-performance design. Use when designing a compensation plan, a warranty or guarantee, a supplier contract, a customer offer that attracts the wrong buyers, or when asking "why do we keep getting the worst customers / hires / vendors?"

**Outcome:** a diagnosis of which information problem is at work, and a contract, signal, screen or pay
plan that aligns behaviour, with its side effects named.

**When to use**
- A price change or offer draws in exactly the customers you didn't want.
- Choosing suppliers whose quality you can't see in advance.
- Writing a sales commission plan, bonus, or owner-manager incentive.
- Selling something whose quality buyers doubt (used equipment, a new service, a small brand).
- A partner, contractor or insured party behaves differently once the deal is signed.

## Method

### 1. Name the problem
| Problem | When | What goes wrong | Typical fixes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Adverse selection** | Before the deal | The side with private information self-selects; the bad types are most eager | Screening, signalling, warranties, reputation, pooling |
| **Moral hazard** | After the deal | The covered party takes more risk or less care because someone else bears the cost | Monitoring, deductibles and co-pays, sharing of losses |
| **Principal-agent** | Ongoing | Someone acting for you (employee, manager, agent) wants different things | Pay for performance, ownership, governance, culture |

### 2. Adverse selection: the lemons logic
If buyers can't tell good from bad, they offer an average price. Owners of good items refuse it; only bad
items trade; the market can collapse. Same logic when you raise insurance premiums or prices and your
best (low-risk, low-cost-to-serve) customers leave first, or when a lenient sales policy fills the pipeline with
customers who take long to close or crowd out better ones.
Fixes:
- **Signals** from the informed side must cost the bad types more than the good types, or they are not believed: warranties and guarantees (expensive to honour on bad products), reputation that the good type would lose more by burning, selling through a certified dealer.
  Test a signal with numbers: it separates only if, for the bad type, its cost exceeds the price premium it would win by copying the good type, while for the good type its cost is below that premium. If a 1-year warranty costs a good seller $2,000 and a bad one $7,000, and the premium is $5,000, only good sellers offer it.
- **Costly customers are adverse selection too:** a B2B account that sends every order out to bid, or a shopper who takes much shelf or page space for little spend, crowds out better customers. Price or serve them accordingly.
- **Screens** by the uninformed side: offer a menu that makes types reveal themselves (deductible levels, trial periods, deposits, volume tiers).
- **Pooling:** a large mandatory pool reduces the cost per member but means good types subsidise bad ones.

### 3. Moral hazard: design for the behaviour after signing
- Perfect monitoring is impossible and costly. Accept some risk; share the rest.
- Make the covered party bear part of the loss (deductibles, co-pays, penalties for late delivery, holdbacks).
- Where possible, make the counterparty pay part of the cost of monitoring (audits at the supplier's expense, sensors they install).
- Choosing suppliers is also adverse selection: test before committing (trial orders, references, scorecards).

### 4. Principal-agent: find the source of misalignment
Three sources: different **objectives** (growth vs profit, empire building), different **risk appetite**
(the agent is usually more risk-averse, having more to lose personally), different **effort** preferences.
Every manager-report relationship is one; so is owner-CEO.

### 5. Design pay for performance
Pay = fixed salary + variable portion tied to a measure.
- Salary only attracts risk-averse people (adverse selection) and gives no reason for extra effort or risk (moral hazard).
- Pure commission attracts risk-lovers and can produce too much risk-taking or effort aimed at the measure, not the goal.
Raise the variable share when: the agent is less risk-averse; the agent's effort strongly drives the result; the measure is precise (little noise from luck).
Lower it when results depend on teamwork, quality is hard to see, or the measure is noisy.
Don't pay commission to "order takers" whose customers would buy anyway; equal team bonuses invite free riding.
Implementation traps: output hard to measure; team work makes individual credit unclear; quality suffers when only quantity is paid; people game whatever is counted.
Where client relationships sit with individual staff (agents, advisers, account managers), commission
without equity invites them to leave and take clients; add a stake in the firm or deferred pay, and keep a
second relationship with each key client (a lesson from a sports and talent agency; the second-relationship
fix is standard practice).
For owner-managers and executives: stock grants tie wealth to value with modest risk; options reward only upside, so they push toward riskier bets. Vesting periods keep focus long term. (Tailoring to a small private firm, e.g. profit-share or phantom equity, is standard practice.)

### 6. Check behavioural biases in the agent and in you
- Empire building: leaders growing what they control rather than value.
- Egocentric bias / illusion of control: crediting results to skill and ignoring luck; it inflates what people think they have earned.
- Groupthink: going along to avoid personal cost; invite dissent before signing off a plan.

## Worked pattern
A home-services company pays technicians a flat $28/hour. Owners see slow jobs and few upsells; they consider pure commission at 30% of job revenue.
- Diagnosis: principal-agent (effort) plus a moral-hazard risk under commission (rushed work, unneeded upsells, which a customer can't easily verify, i.e. the customer faces adverse selection too).
- Effort strongly drives output, but quality is hard to see and callbacks show up weeks later. So use a mix, not pure commission.
- Plan: base $22/hour + 12% of job revenue + a quality gate: commission on any job with a callback within 30 days is clawed back; quarterly bonus of $500 if customer rating >= 4.7.
- Check the numbers: a tech billing $160k a year (2,000 hours) earns 44,000 + 19,200 = $63,200 vs $56,000 flat. A slow tech billing $120k earns $58,400. The pay spread now tracks output.
- Side effects to watch: techs avoiding hard jobs (assign by rotation), and recruiting changes (the plan will attract people confident in their output, which is the point).
- Customer side: offer a 1-year workmanship guarantee. It is cheap for a careful firm to honour and costly for a sloppy one, so it is a credible signal of quality.

## Common mistakes
- Treating adverse selection and moral hazard the same: one is fixed before the deal (screens, signals), the other after (monitoring, sharing losses).
- Raising prices or premiums across the board and losing the best customers first.
- Offering a guarantee you can't afford to honour; it signals nothing and costs a lot.
- Paying only on a measurable quantity when quality matters.
- High-powered incentives on noisy measures; people get paid for luck and punished for it.
