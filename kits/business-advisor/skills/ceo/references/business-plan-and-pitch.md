# Business plan and pitch

> **Area:** `business-plan-and-pitch` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Write the documents a young venture uses to win money, partners or a competition: the one-page executive summary, the pitch deck, the short elevator pitch and the ask. Use when someone needs to explain a venture to investors, lenders, judges or partners, or wants an existing summary or deck reviewed against what evaluators score.

**Outcome:** a clear, honest, evidence-backed story of the venture (pain, solution, market, edge, model, traction, team, financials, ask) in the formats evaluators expect, ready to survive questions.

## When to use

- Preparing a pitch deck, a one-page executive summary or a 2-minute video pitch.
- Entering a business plan competition or applying to an accelerator or grant.
- Reviewing a founder's deck or summary before it goes out.
- Preparing for investor Q&A.

Do the opportunity-validation and market-sizing-and-unit-economics work first; the pitch only communicates it.

## Method

### 1. Know what evaluators score

Across typical competition rubrics, judges and investors score roughly the same things, usually 1 to 5 each:
1. Problem: clear, concise, real.
2. Solution: feasible, and the team can deliver it.
3. Target customer: you have spoken to them and keep doing so.
4. Value proposition: original, differentiated from established competitors.
5. Market and trends: real demand, significant and growing market.
6. Minimum viable product: a plan to build a working version.
7. Financial model: pricing, marketing, sales, revenue and investment needed are realistic.
8. Traction: milestones met, paying customers or partners, a plan for growth.
9. Team: knows its capabilities and how it will fill gaps.
10. Resource support: other funding pursued (grants, competitions, angels).
11. Use of funds: how much, and exactly what it buys.
12. Delivery: confident, answers questions well, motivated.

Investors' own shorthand is the **five Ts**: TAM (market), Thesis (what you do and why it wins), Team, Traction, Tech (what is built and the moat). Some add Timing (why now). Answer the first-tier question of each clearly in the deck; the Q&A will then be about second-tier questions (competition, adoption timing, team gaps, stickiness, defensibility).

**Pitch right for the funder's type.** A venture investor needs to believe the company can be one of the rare outsized winners, so show upside and leave out safe side businesses (they dilute the story). A bank or other lender gets nothing from upside and loses on failure, so show stable cash flow, collateral and the downside plan; a moonshot story scares a lender off. Same venture, two different pitches (see `cfo/references/raising-startup-capital.md` for which funder fits).

A venture investor's five questions, in the order they usually come: (1) can the market be big enough to return my fund, framed as the market the product can create, not only today's market it replaces; (2) why this founder, with founder-market fit and a plan to build the team; (3) how the product idea was reached (the evidence trail) and whether it is a must-have that is many times better or cheaper than the alternative; (4) how customers will be acquired, and whether the price supports that channel (a direct sales force needs a high average sale); (5) whether this round reaches milestones that justify a next round at roughly double the price, because the investor does not want to be the only one funding the next round.

### 2. Pitch deck structure (about 10 to 12 slides, 7 minutes)

1. **Title:** company name and one sentence on what it does ("the X of Y" analogies work).
2. **Pain:** the real pain, quantified, with a relatable customer story. Lead with pain.
3. **Solution:** how it relieves the pain; simple, visual.
4. **Market (who cares?):** customers and dollars, built bottom-up, with the math in the appendix.
5. **Competition:** your unfair advantage; a positioning chart on the two dimensions customers care most about; why existing solutions do not solve the pain.
6. **Business model:** segments now and later; how customers buy; how you grow the base; pricing; key revenue features.
7. **Traction:** sales and growth if launched; otherwise pilots, letters of intent, pre-orders, expert support. Evidence, not adjectives.
8. **Team:** why these people win; who you will add to fill gaps.
9. **Pro forma revenue (3 to 5 years):** the curve, what drives the bend, and the one or two drivers the forecast is most sensitive to.
10. **The ask:** amount, for what equity (or instrument), and the main uses of the money.
11. **Close:** contact.
12. **Appendix:** market-size math, pricing versus competitors, marketing plan by segment, detailed financials, anything an investor may ask.

Pitch-making rules (from investor practice):
- First slide states the value proposition in one or two sentences; do not bury it.
- Aim to be completely clear, even if not exhaustively accurate; pick one illustrative example.
- Show the team early, with titles and relevant accomplishments, not life stories.
- Traction needs a time axis; use a graph only if it is impressive; avoid vanity numbers (followers, survey counts).
- Pitch the investor, not the customer: they want return potential, not product features.
- If weak on one T, double down on a non-obvious insight that explains why you will win.

### 3. One-page executive summary

One page, scannable, same order as the deck: headline, problem (with a data point), solution and first product, customer segments with sizes, customer-discovery evidence, value proposition versus alternatives, market (TAM/SAM/SOM) and trends, MVP and roadmap phases, unit economics and a 5-year summary P&L, traction timeline, team and advisors, resource support, use of funds. Revise it as evidence arrives; a revised version adds financial projections.

### 4. Elevator and short video pitch

- The goal is the next step (a meeting, a referral, permission to send more), not the money or the deal. Research the listener first and lead with what is in it for them.
- Build it by answering, in order: the problem or opportunity and why now; the first product and how it differs from substitutes; who benefits and how (money saved, time saved, better performance, better information, access to a community); the launch plan and who does what; the money needed and from whom; what investors, partners and employees get (market, share, terminal value, return); who supports it and who will oppose it; the main risks and how each is handled. Then compress the answers into one paragraph and cut it for each audience.
- 2 minutes maximum (viewers stop at the limit); built from the executive summary.
- Cover pain, solution, target customer, how you differ; end with the ask or next step.
- Rough split in thirds: problem, solution and proof, why us and ask. Show energy and personal connection to the pain.

### 5. The ask and use of funds

- State the amount and the terms ("$400K for 25%" or "$400K on a SAFE with a $4M cap").
- Make sure the implied valuation is one you can defend (see startup-valuation-and-deal-math).
- Tie the money to milestones that make the next raise easier: what will be true when it is spent, and how many months of runway it buys.
- Break down the biggest uses (hires, production run, pilots).

### 6. Product roadmap (if asked)

Strategy is the what and why; the roadmap is the how and when. Show phases with milestones and checkpoints, at the altitude the audience needs (investors and owners want a high-level release view, not a task list). Treat it as a communication tool that will change.

### 7. Honesty

Pitches invite exaggeration. Do not overstate traction, customers, partnerships or IP. Due diligence will find it, and a broken trust kills deals and reputations. Present projections as projections, with assumptions.
- Enthusiasm slides into exaggeration, then falsehood, then fraud; the slope is gradual, so set the line early.
- Match disclosure to context: a chance elevator ride carries only the vision; a formal pitch, a hiring conversation or a diligence meeting needs the evidence and the assumptions.
- Use a "conclusion sandwich": state the claim ("about $2M revenue next year"), show the evidence and the assumptions behind it, then restate the claim. The listener gets the takeaway and can judge it.
- Choose co-founders, board members and investors who push you toward honesty, not toward bigger claims.

### 8. Prepare for Q&A

Write the twenty hardest questions (see the investor checklist in vc-deal-screening) and rehearse answers. Typical probes: how the SOM was calculated; why customers pay your price; current customers, CAC, churn; pre- or post-money terms; runway from this raise; cap table; how gaps in the team will be filled; exit path.

When an investor says the plan is wrong (say, the go-to-market), do not drop it on the spot: explain how you reached it, listen, and say what evidence would change your mind. Strong beliefs, loosely held, reads as a fit CEO; pivoting mid-meeting reads as an unprepared one.

### 9. The written business plan

A plan is a weak predictor of success, and an elaborate one can be a warning sign. Its job is to show the team has asked the right questions, not to forecast month by month. Organise it around four factors that all interact:
1. **People:** what the team knows, whom it knows, and how well it is known by suppliers, customers and hires. Investors read this section first.
2. **Opportunity:** a large or fast-growing market in a structurally attractive industry; who buys, how they decide, price, cost to acquire, produce, serve and keep a customer; how the opportunity grows; how competitors will respond.
3. **Context:** regulation, macroeconomy, technology, financing climate; how they help or hurt now, how they will change, and what management will do if they turn.
4. **Risk and reward:** what can go wrong and right, what management will do about each, and how investors eventually get money out.

Numbers belong near the back, as a model of the few drivers of success, the break-even point and when cash turns positive. Section-by-section outline, the question lists, the model layout and the plain-words glossary of plan clichés are in `ceo/references/business-plan-and-pitch/plan-sections-and-financial-model.md`.

### 10. Founder decisions the plan must settle

Before raising, settle the decisions that are cheap now and costly later: legal form, a founders' agreement with vesting and buy-out terms, clearance of the name and protection of IP, which resources to own versus rent or contract, the minimum resource set and a reserve for the plan going wrong, the checkpoints that lower risk, and how the venture could be harvested. Method in `ceo/references/business-plan-and-pitch/founder-decisions.md`.

## Worked pattern

A healthy breakfast-food startup's one-pager, outlined: headline on what it replaces; pain stat (most consumers too busy for a healthy breakfast); product and price; two segments with sizes; 100+ interviews and their top findings; positioning chart on nutrition and taste; $44B TAM → $1.7B SAM → $50M SOM; MVP phase 1 (hand-packed, break-even) and phase 2 (co-packer run of 12,000 units for $25K); unit economics by channel (direct 84% gross margin, wholesale 59%); 5-year revenue and EBITDA; dated traction milestones; team and advisors; use of funds itemised (ingredients, packaging, co-packer). Every claim has a number or a source.

## Common mistakes

- Burying what the company does; leading with history or technology.
- Pain that is conceptual rather than felt; no customer voice.
- Market slide that quotes a report and claims a percentage.
- Competition slide that says "no competitors" or ignores the status quo.
- Team at the end with no titles or relevant achievements.
- Hockey-stick revenue with no driver; no sensitivity.
- An ask with no valuation logic or no link between money and milestones.
- Overclaiming; inconsistent numbers between deck, summary and model.
- The stock lines investors discount on sight: "our projections are conservative"; an analyst's huge market figure; a big partner "about to sign"; key hires who "join once we are funded" (if they will not join before the money, they will not after); "no competition"; asking investors to sign a non-disclosure agreement; dismissing incumbents as slow; patents as the whole moat outside life sciences; "we only need 1% of the market".
- A detailed month-by-month forecast in place of a model of the few drivers that decide success.

## Generalised case lessons

- A founder who pitched one well-known investor a single sentence on why his company fit that investor's criteria sold the company a year later: know the listener and ask only for the next step.
- A payments idea won its series A only after the better-known co-founder took the CEO seat; investors who could not judge the first CEO passed. If your founder story is weak on a T, change the team or bring evidence for it before pitching.
- A games company with months of cash left pivoted to the internal tool its team had built, with the board's blessing, and that tool became the business. Pitch a team that learns, not a plan that cannot change.
