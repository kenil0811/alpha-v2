# Acquisitions, integration and the portfolio

Depth for `corporate-strategy` steps 4, 7 and 8. Two teaching cases inform it: a
cross-border buy of a founder-led start-up by a large retailer (people and culture), and a serial acquirer
that adds value through its management system (the playbook). Both are generalised below; the synergy
arithmetic, integration typology and portfolio review are standard practice.

## Before the deal: is there value to share?
1. **Strategic logic.** Which VRIO resource of the buyer reaches the target, or of the target reaches the buyer?
   If neither, the deal is a financial bet; compare it with what a fund would pay.
2. **Three tests (Porter):** attractive industry; entry cost does not already price in all the profit; better off together.
3. **Synergy arithmetic:**
   - Value to buyer = standalone value + PV(cost synergies) + PV(revenue synergies) − PV(integration costs) − PV(dis-synergies).
   - Premium = price − standalone value. The buyer's gain = synergies net of costs − premium.
   - Count cost synergies at about full value, revenue synergies at a haircut (often half), dated by year.
   - Dis-synergies: customers who leave because they compete with the buyer, key staff who leave, lost suppliers.
4. **Why acquire rather than ally or build:** the target is unique; a contract would expose you to hold-up;
   speed matters; preempting a rival matters (`competitive-moves`). If the target's edge rests on its people's
   strong incentives, an alliance or minority stake may keep more of it (`corporate-strategy` step 6).
5. **Walk-away price** before talks start; pricing mechanics with the CFO (`valuation-multiples-and-deals`).

## Integration: where the value is won or lost
Choose the integration level first (Haspeslagh and Jemison's typology):
| Approach | When | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Absorb | Value comes from combining operations and cutting duplicate cost | Destroys the target's capability if that was the point |
| Preserve | Value is in the target's independent capability or culture | Synergies never arrive |
| Symbiosis | Both: keep the edge, share selected resources | Hardest to manage; needs clear rules |

People checklist:
- **Key people:** name the 10-20 who carry the value; retention terms, roles and reporting lines before close.
- **Founders and leaders:** what decisions stay theirs, for how long, and what happens when that ends.
- **Culture and pay:** pay scales, equity, titles, decision speed; where they differ, say which wins and when.
- **Decision rights:** a written list of what the parent approves and what stays local.
- **Owner per synergy:** each synergy has one named owner, a date and a measure.
- **Communication:** customers, staff and suppliers hear the plan from you, early and in the same words.
- **First 100 days:** stabilise (no lost customers, no lost key staff), then capture the quick cost synergies,
  then the slower revenue ones.
Post-deal value creation and exit planning: `value-creation-and-exit` (CFO).

## Culture: choose the end state (from the cross-border case)
Four end states, chosen before signing and said out loud to both sides:
| End state | What happens | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Coexist | Each keeps its culture; separate brands and operations | The target's edge is its own way of working (a start-up bought for speed and tech) |
| Blend | The best of both, agreed explicitly | Both bring capabilities the other lacks |
| Absorb | The target adopts the buyer's culture | Value is in cost and scale; the target's culture was not the asset |
| Transform | Both drop key habits for a new set | A merger meant to change both firms |
What derails it: one side assuming its culture is superior. Common clash lines: entrepreneurial speed versus
process and hierarchy; generous pay and perks versus austerity; national and local cultures on top.

People work across three stages:
- **Before the deal:** define the end state; say what happens to founders (role, authority, how long); have
  the target's own leaders explain why the deal is good for staff.
- **During:** build cross-cultural learning while executives are busy with legal and financial work; find
  common ground in shared values.
- **After:** reinforce the chosen culture through named change agents; watch morale and departures monthly.
Traps the case shows:
- A founder who leaves because he was refused a real role takes morale with him.
- Staff equity bought back on terms that treat former staff worse than current staff reads as a broken
  promise and hurts hiring across the whole local market.
- Rumours of job cuts fill any silence: say early who is affected and who is not.
- A buyer with a record of failed foreign deals needs a stated reason this one differs.

## The management-system acquirer (from the serial-acquirer case)
How a parent turns an operating system into repeated acquisition value. Use it to test a claim that "we
can improve what we buy", or to build the capability in a smaller firm.
1. **Choose markets first.** A standing, board-reviewed list of attractive markets (criteria in
   `corporate-strategy` step 7). Prepared buyers act fast and decisively when a target appears.
2. **Platforms, then bolt-ons.** Enter through a lead firm with a strong position; bolt-on deals are run by
   the operating unit with central deal, legal and pricing help, and folded into the platform.
3. **Assess people in diligence and after.** Decide quickly who stays (from none leaving to half the top
   team); judge people by working with them, not by interview. Have a replacement ready for known gaps.
4. **A 100-day strategic plan** written with the acquired team once the deal closes: what game are we
   playing, how do we win. Challenge comfortable assumptions (a leader with 40% share should ask why 60%
   of buyers choose others: the growth is in rivals' customers).
5. **Teach the system by doing.** A short training week, then a week-long improvement event on the shop
   floor with an aggressive target (halve the space, halve the lead time). Leaders take part in person.
6. **Policy deployment.** Three to five breakthrough goals, cascaded into annual goals, process changes and
   one or two measures each, reviewed monthly on one page (red, yellow, green). Root-cause each red; check
   that greens are real. A business with all greens is not stretching.
7. **Keep the centre small.** A few dozen people at head office; few reporting lines; an internal team of
   former operators who coach units on the system for a few years each, by invitation where possible.
8. **Grow the talent pool.** Most senior roles filled from inside, a firm-wide talent review, outside
   hires immersed in the system for weeks before starting the job. Talent is the binding constraint.
Limits: organic growth is often modest (most growth bought); bigger targets with set cultures are slower to
convert; funds compete for the same deals, so the edge must be synergies a fund cannot claim.

## Portfolio review
For each business, three questions:
| Question | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Attractive industry? | `industry-analysis` |
| Advantage in it? | VRIO of its resources (`competitive-strategy`) |
| Does this parent add more than any other owner would (parenting advantage)? | Shared resources used, management system applied, capital it could not raise alone |
- All three yes: invest and grow.
- Industry and advantage yes, parent adds nothing: hold loosely (holding-company style) or sell to a better parent.
- Parent can add value but the business lacks advantage: a fix plan with a deadline.
- Weak on all three: sell or close.

## Divestiture
- **Sale** to a trade buyer (who may pay for synergies) or a fund; **spin-off** to shareholders; **carve-out**
  (partial listing or partial sale); **wind-down**.
- Sell when another owner values it above its worth to you, when it drains management attention or
  capital, or when it no longer shares resources with the rest.
- Untangle shared services (IT, sales force, plant) early; a transition services agreement covers the gap.
- An exit from a declining core can be gradual: build the new business first, then sell or run down the old.
