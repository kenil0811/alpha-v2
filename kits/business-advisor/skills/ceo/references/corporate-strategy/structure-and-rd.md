# Structure and R&D placement for a multi-business firm

Depth for `corporate-strategy` step 9. Strategy and structure go together: the businesses a firm holds
decide how it should be organised, and no structure suits every strategy or removes every conflict.

## The five generic structures
| Structure | How it groups work | Strengths | Weaknesses | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | By expertise (production, sales, finance) under one head | Specialisation; no duplicated oversight; one central decision-maker | Breaks down with many lines; functions guard their turf; no objective measure per unit; daily operations crowd out strategy | Start-ups, single-business firms |
| Multidivisional | Divisions by product or region, each with its own functions; a small centre | Strong financial incentives for division heads; centre free for strategy; easy to buy and sell divisions | Extra hierarchy; divisions hide information and game the centre; short-term focus; rivalry instead of cooperation; transfer-pricing fights | Diversified firms growing by acquisition |
| Centralised multidivisional | As above, but the centre steps in to exploit links between divisions (cross-division committees, shared R&D) | Captures economies of scope; integrates acquisitions; R&D used across divisions; longer view | More bureaucracy; performance harder to measure; less benefit from internal competition | Related businesses |
| Holding company | Divisions run almost as separate firms | Very sharp financial incentives (frequent reporting); a near-pure internal capital market | Little or no sharing of resources | Unrelated businesses |
| Matrix | People report to a function and to a product, project or region | Coordinates across both axes; shares functional skills; flexible for projects; clean project accounting | Role ambiguity; weak accountability; function versus product conflict | Project businesses, firms that must be both global and local |
Many real firms are hybrids.

Why the incentive gap: a division's results can be measured on their own, so its head can be paid on them;
in a functional firm no function's contribution to profit can be isolated. A matrix shares more because
every person answers to both a resource owner and a business owner, so resources are pooled instead of
duplicated in each division. The core trade-off throughout: **cooperation between units versus strong
incentives for each unit's managers**. A firm moves to a matrix when coordination across markets becomes
worth more than the sharp accountability it gives up (for example, when national divisions duplicate
products that a region could share).

## Where to put R&D
| Placement | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Central lab | Research useful to several divisions; sustained long-term projects; can transform the firm; draws researchers who want big problems; closer ties to universities | Far from customers; hard to redirect quickly; weaker incentive to produce products; contribution hard to measure |
| In each division | Close to customers; quick, product-focused | Incremental; duplicated; little cross-division use |
| Hybrid (most common in large US firms) | Centre explores, divisions exploit | Must set the size of the centre and who holds the budget |

Budget authority is a second lever: a central lab funded by the divisions is pushed toward customer needs;
divisional labs funded by the centre are pushed toward uses in other divisions.

Published research on large firms (patent citations as the measure):
- Centralised R&D, in reporting or in budget, produces innovations with wider impact on later innovation;
  wider impact is not the same as more profit.
- Centralised firms lean on internal research; decentralised ones source more knowledge through alliances
  and acquisitions. R&D-intensive firms tend to centralise and aim at major breakthroughs.
- How inventors are connected matters too. Firms whose inventors work in tight clusters linked by strong
  bridges produce wider-impact work and build on their own inventions faster than rivals; centralising R&D
  raised that connectedness after about five years.

## Lessons from the cases on organising
**A front-back reorganisation (a consumer-goods firm).** A geographic matrix with over a hundred profit
centres made accountability fuzzy and decisions slow. It was replaced by three linked parts:
| Part | Owns | Paid on |
|---|---|---|
| Global product units | Product development, brands, strategy, supply | Profit |
| Market units by region | Local selling, retailers, consumer knowledge | Sales growth |
| Shared business services | Accounting, payroll, IT, facilities on one system, run from a few service centres | Cost |
What it gains: global standards for products and plants, faster roll-outs, scale in back-office work.
What went wrong at first, and the general lesson:
- **Too much at once.** Structure, processes, pay (a much larger variable share), management layers and
  thousands of job cuts all changed together, and early results were poor while focused rivals took share.
  Morale fell before any gain showed. Sequence large changes and protect the customer-facing work.
- **Measures that pull apart.** A unit paid on sales and a unit paid on profit will disagree on every price
  and launch; give them a shared measure or a clear tie-breaker.
- **Strategy and structure together.** Many categories with no shared edge make any structure strain; ask
  whether some businesses would be worth more standing alone before redrawing the chart.

**Small autonomous profit centres (a sports and talent agency).** Splitting a growing firm into a dozen small
profit centres, each also responsible for helping the others, kept a small-firm feel and avoided layers.
The cost: managers start saying "this deal is mine" instead of "this deal is ours", and talented people
leave to start rivals when they have no equity. Pair profit centres with a firm-wide share of reward, and
deliberately move people across units so the structure does not wall off new ideas.

**A small centre over many units (a serial acquirer).** Dozens of profit-and-loss units, very few
people reporting to the chief executive, small units merged rather than split, and a centre limited to
finance, legal, tax, people and deals. Works when a shared management system, not shared operations, is
what the parent adds.

## Practical checks for a growing firm (standard practice)
- Second business line added: keep shared functions only where the VRIO resource is shared; give the new
  line its own profit measure.
- Acquisition-led growth: a multidivisional or holding form keeps targets separable; plan which central
  services they will use.
- Innovation that matters to several lines: a small central team with a budget the lines co-fund.
- Pay: weight unit results for unrelated portfolios; add a whole-firm component where units must cooperate.
