# Boundaries: make, buy or ally

Depth for `corporate-strategy` step 5. Use it to decide whether an activity (a component, a service, a
channel, a technology) should be owned, bought on contract, or done with a partner.

## The four corners

### 1. Asset specificity and incomplete contracts (transaction cost)
Transaction costs are the costs of negotiating, writing, monitoring and enforcing a contract. They rise when:
- **Contracts are incomplete:** no one can foresee every contingency, and courts are slow and costly.
- **Assets are specific:** one side's investment is worth little outside this relationship.
- **Uncertainty is high:** more unforeseen events, more chances to reopen terms.
Together these create **hold-up**: after the deal is signed, the less dependent side demands better terms,
and the dependent side usually gives in because the court route is too slow.

Five kinds of specificity, with a small-business test for each:
| Kind | Example | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Site | Your plant next to the supplier's in a remote place | Could either move without a large loss? |
| Physical | Tooling or moulds built for one buyer | Could the tooling serve another customer? |
| Dedicated | Capacity reserved for one buyer | Would the capacity sit idle if the buyer left? |
| Human | Engineers' custom design know-how | Would the knowledge be worth anything to another firm? |
| Timing | Late delivery stalls everything (construction, events) | What does a one-day delay cost? |

Owning the step lowers hold-up: units of one firm have less reason to squeeze each other, the chief
executive settles disputes in the firm's interest, and no court is needed. The price is bureaucracy and
weaker incentives than a supplier facing a market.

Scale cuts the other way: an outside supplier serving many buyers reaches larger scale, but only for
generic inputs. The more specific the input, the less scale the supplier gains from other buyers.

### 2. Knowledge leakage
- You cannot price know-how without showing it, and once shown the buyer need not pay.
- In joint development, the IP that emerges cannot be divided in a contract written beforehand.
- Patents are often easy to design around and weakly enforced in some countries.
High leakage risk pushes toward owning or toward tight, staged deals with clear IP ownership.

### 3. Comparative capability
Firms differ in what they do well for reasons beyond scale: learning curves and tacit know-how that take
years to build. Buy where the supplier is clearly better; make where you are. A firm may integrate without
the capability on purpose, to build it for the future; then budget for the learning period.

### 4. Market power
- Backward: offset a powerful supplier (a brewer making its own cans), raise rivals' input costs, lock up a scarce input.
- Forward: offset powerful buyers, close a distribution channel to rivals, raise entry barriers.
- Limits: antitrust law (restraint of trade), and high returns draw new suppliers and channels, so power fades.

## Questionable reasons
- "Capture the supplier's profit": the price you pay to buy or the cost to build already includes it.
- "Coordinate better": ask what a contract cannot do. If there is a real answer, it is a corner-1 or corner-2 reason.

## Scoring sheet (standard practice)
Score each corner 1 (low) to 5 (high) for the activity, then read:
| Corner | High score pushes toward |
|---|---|
| Asset specificity × uncertainty | Own |
| Leakage risk | Own, or ally with clear IP terms |
| Supplier's capability edge over yours | Buy |
| Power gain, after antitrust and new-entry check | Own, as a competitive move |
Two or more "own" signals and no supplier edge: integrate. A supplier edge with low specificity: buy.
Mixed: ally (joint venture, long-term contract with shared investment, minority stake), or taper
integration (make part, buy part, which also gives a benchmark on the supplier's cost).

## Middle forms (standard practice)
- **Long-term contract with shared tooling:** the buyer owns the specific asset, the supplier runs it.
- **Dual sourcing:** keeps a second supplier warm to cap hold-up.
- **Minority stake or joint venture:** aligns incentives, keeps the partner's independent edge.
- **Licence:** sells use of IP, keeps ownership; see `ceo/references/corporate-strategy/staged-options-and-licensing.md`.

## Why internal ventures often fail, and when to ally instead
New businesses need longer payback, stronger entrepreneurial pay and freedom from corporate rules on hiring,
sourcing and customers. Established units resist: they complain of favoured treatment, withhold help, or
lobby to take the venture over once it is profitable. If the parent does absorb it, it breaks the deal made
with the venture's managers and makes its next promise less believable. Since a parent cannot credibly
commit to leave a unit alone for ever, an alliance with an independent firm is often the better home for
innovation that depends on strong personal incentives (the way large drug firms rely on alliances with
biotech firms).

## Contracting out a new process: lessons from a biotech start-up
When the process is new and much of the know-how is tacit (scale-up that works in a flask but not in a
tank), contracting it out carries costs a price quote misses:
- **Disclosure to get a quote.** A contractor cannot price time and cost without seeing proprietary detail,
  so leakage starts before any deal is signed.
- **Time.** Negotiating, transferring and validating the process at a contractor can take about as long as
  building your own small facility. Compare dates, not only costs.
- **Quantity commitments.** Committing to large volumes is risky if the product or its specification may
  change; short contracts cost more per unit because the contractor must recover set-up.
- **Industry capacity.** Spare capacity at other firms (built for products that stalled) makes contracting
  cheaper and easier; check whether that glut will last.
- **Control and learning.** Owning even a small pilot step keeps process and quality in your hands and
  builds a team before the large, high-stakes step. The counter-argument: a small firm should put every
  scarce dollar where it is best (often research); ask honestly whether that edge alone still differentiates.
- **Process risk.** A facility built for one technology may not serve the next one already in the pipeline.

## Integrating into your supplier's business
When you are a large customer of a supplier (a carrier, a platform, a distributor) and start doing the work
yourself, the supplier becomes a rival in that work while still serving you elsewhere. Integrate in stages
(start with your own overflow), keep a second supplier, and expect prices and service from the incumbent
to change.
