# Case lessons, generalised

Depth for `corporate-strategy`. Nine teaching cases, each reduced to the decision it poses and the
lessons that carry to other firms. Own words; no case text. The step numbers refer to the area file.

## Lessons on modes (step 6)
- **Buying a partner whose power has grown** (an animation-studio partnership case): justified when your other
  businesses earn more from its output than any rival could; protect its culture and leaders as deal terms.
  Detail: `platform-and-technology-strategy`, step 6.
- **Stakes in partners** look cheap but depend on the partner surviving; a downturn can wipe out the stake
  and the supply deal at once. Size them as bets, not as secured channels.
- **Build a capability before the stakes peak.** A small, early in-house step (a pilot plant, a first
  service team) lets people learn while mistakes are cheap; waiting means a green team at the moment it matters.
  Weigh this against the investors' focus argument: a small firm's scarce money and attention may earn more
  in its core.
- **An exploration unit inside a parent** needs its own milestones, not revenue targets: selling an immature
  product early pulls the team off the technical path and disappoints buyers. More money speeds it only up
  to what the team can absorb.

## Online retailer
- A shared base (fulfilment, payments, customer accounts, data) lowers the cost of each new category; the
  breadth is related through that base, not through the products.
- A third-party marketplace on the same base earns far higher margins than stocking goods yourself, but
  puts you in competition with your own sellers, which invites complaints and regulators.
- A capability built for internal use (computing infrastructure) became the most profitable business.
- Minority stakes and supply deals with young partners lost their value when those partners failed.
- Pruning unprofitable items and processes matters as much as adding categories.
- Doing your own shipping turns your carriers into rivals; scale also draws political and antitrust attack.

## Device maker
- Opening a product to a much larger installed base (another platform's users) can multiply sales; a closed
  stance protects margin but caps reach.
- Accessories and services tied to the core device grow fast but rise and fall with it: not diversification.
- A large cash pile tempts bets far from the core; a failed high-profile bet costs brand, not only money.
- Ecosystem bundling and self-preferencing draw regulators; plan for forced openness.
- Long-horizon projects lose momentum when key people leave; continuity of leadership is a resource.

## Retailer buys an e-commerce start-up
- A strategic fit (a market you could not enter alone, technology you lack) does not secure the value.
- Decide the cultural end state up front; respect for the target's speed must be shown, not just promised.
- Founders' roles, staff equity buy-backs and job-cut rumours decide whether the talent stays.
- A record of failed foreign deals is a warning to answer explicitly.
Detail: `ceo/references/corporate-strategy/acquisitions-and-portfolio.md`.

## Industrial serial acquirer
- Markets first, then companies; a standing list of approved markets lets you move fast.
- The value is a codified, taught, monthly-reviewed operating system plus a deep talent pool; time spent
  using it is hard for a rival to buy.
- Limits: most growth bought, bigger targets harder to convert, funds bidding for the same firms.
Detail: the playbook in `ceo/references/corporate-strategy/acquisitions-and-portfolio.md`.

## Sports and talent agency
- Moving from representing talent into owning events, media and training is related diversification
  through relationships, and it creates conflicts of interest with the clients you represent.
- Client relationships sit with individual agents; agents without equity leave and take clients.
- Rival roll-ups that bought agencies paid for relationships the deal could not bind.
- Founder-centred firms need a succession plan as part of the strategy.

## Big-tech quantum computing lab
- When a technology is immature, the business-model options are: sell whole systems with service
  contracts, sell access as a service, or sell joint exploration and consulting. Each fits a different stage.
- Early paying customers pull the team off the technical roadmap and, if over-sold, damage the whole field.
- Whether to hedge across rival technologies depends on how far ahead your chosen path is and how much the
  others share with it.
- More money accelerates only what the team can absorb; inputs (materials, people) may be the real limit.

## Biotech start-up
- Contracting out a new process costs disclosure, time and flexibility, not only the fee.
- Building a small capability early prepares the team for the high-stakes step; licensing now trades
  upside for cash and focus.
Detail: `ceo/references/corporate-strategy/boundaries-make-buy-ally.md` and
`ceo/references/corporate-strategy/staged-options-and-licensing.md`.

## Consumer-goods reorganisation
- A front-back structure (product units, market units, shared services) can fix a slow matrix.
- Changing everything at once, with measures that pit units against each other, can make a sound design fail.
- Restructuring does not answer whether the portfolio itself still shares an edge.
Detail: `ceo/references/corporate-strategy/structure-and-rd.md`.
