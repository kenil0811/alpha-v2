# Running a client consulting engagement

> **Area:** `consulting-engagement` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Run a client consulting project end to end: kick-off and probing questions, goal and problem definition, a statement of work with scope, deliverables, milestones and communication plan, a research plan built on what must be true, a mid-point check-in, and a final deliverable that ends in a decision. Use when advising a business or nonprofit on a defined project, or scoping any piece of work for a client.

**Outcome:** a project that solves the client's real problem, on an agreed scope and timeline,
and ends with a recommendation the client can act on.

## When to use
- A client (business, nonprofit, internal team) asks for help on a problem.
- Writing or reviewing a statement of work (SOW).
- The project feels vague, the scope keeps growing, or the team does not know what to research.
- Preparing a mid-point review or the final presentation.

## Method

### The arc (five phases)
1. Immersion and planning (scope). 2. Research and analysis (learn). 3. Solution development.
4. Solution refinement. 5. Final deliverable and hand-off. A typical project runs about
15-17 weeks; it goes faster than it feels, so early momentum matters.

### Phase 1: immersion and planning
**Kick-off meeting.** Prepare a pre-meeting document (what you know, what you need to learn,
questions) and debrief right after. Act like an investigative reporter: curious, objective,
empathetic, flexible, discreet. Ask open questions:
How long has this been an issue? What is the financial impact or risk? How does it affect you
and others? What have you tried, what worked, what got in the way? What are your top
priorities? What would the ideal outcome be?

**Clarify the goal.** Stakeholders rarely give SMART goals; they give aspirations ("be the
leader"), vague directions ("grow share") or functional priorities ("build awareness").
Curate and translate them into specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound goals.
When stakeholders' goals conflict: re-anchor everyone on the shared objective; diagnose which
uncertainty each one is addressing; surface other needs; decide when each goal matters most;
build a sequenced roadmap connecting them. Make success explicit.

**Define the real problem** (spend most of the time here, not on solutions). Write a problem
and desired-outcome statement:
- When trying to [task: verb + object] in [circumstance] for [segment],
- they strive to [desired outcome], measured by [success metrics],
- but [problem: barriers], because [causes],
- which results in [negative impact: cost, time, risk], makes them feel [emotional], and
  fear [social].
Then check where the problem sits: talent, tool, process, context or culture. Ask: is the
problem understood, and has it been validated?

**Order:** goal, then problem, then the true need (from insight), and only then the approach.
Diverge while exploring; converge deliberately.

**Statement of work.** Sections (template in `ceo/references/consulting-engagement/sow-template.md`):
executive summary; key objectives (end-state decisions or abilities, not tasks); phases,
tasks and subtasks with outputs and dependencies; term, timeline and milestones;
deliverables; roles and responsibilities; communication plan; change-management process;
acceptance and signatures. Get client approval before work starts. Anything not in the SOW
is out of scope and needs a revision.

### Phase 2: research as a learning system
1. Start from the client's objective; define what success looks like.
2. **What must be true** for the objective to be reached? List the assumptions about customers,
   competitors, operations and the organisation.
3. **Knowns and unknowns:** mark which assumptions are already validated and which are
   uncertain and risky. Focus on the unknowns that matter.
4. **Questions:** turn each important unknown into a question whose answer would change the
   recommendation. Not what is interesting, what is necessary to decide.
5. **Data:** first-party (the client's own: high quality, small scale), second-party (a partner's
   first-party data), third-party (collected by others: most scale, least quality).
6. **Methods** matched to the question: exploration (desk research), observation (what people
   do), interaction (interviews, workshops), response (surveys, tests that measure).
Expect scope changes and data delays; record them and update the SOW.

### Phase 3: solution development
- Aim at five answers: **What** should the client do (direction), **Why** this over alternatives
  (conviction), **How** to execute (implementation), **When**, in what order (sequence), **Who**
  owns it (ownership). Not every project needs all five equally; say which ones yours must nail.
- Test the solution against **desirability** (does it meet the objective and give unique
  value?), **viability** (what must be true for the business to sustain it?) and **feasibility**
  (can people, processes and technology deliver it over time?).
- **Mid-point check-in** (a working session, not a finished presentation; about 30 minutes plus
  30 of discussion): purpose and objective; approach and method; hurdles and assumptions;
  vision for the final deliverable; next steps; key learnings. Ask the client: What have we done
  well? What should we improve? Should the scope expand or narrow? What questions do you have?

### Phases 4-5: refine and deliver
- A great deliverable gives **clarity** (what to do, why this path, what changes), **certainty**
  (why this over alternatives, risks acknowledged, assumptions explicit) and **direction** (who
  owns it, what must change, what happens first).
- Build it as a story (see `business-storytelling`): know the audience, structured narrative,
  one win theme, credible method, a framework that fits the decision, evidence, insights tied to actions.
- End with a decision the client can say yes to, plus a hand-off: documentation, templates,
  next steps, and ideas for a follow-on project.

### Running the relationship
- Agree communication rhythm: kick-off, regular check-ins, mid-point, draft review, final.
  No surprises at the end.
- Get out there: site visits and front-line conversations reveal what documents do not.
- Keep internal disagreements internal; present one team view to the client.
- Keep client information confidential; anonymise it in anything shared outside.
- Send short written status updates: what changed since the SOW, next steps, what access or
  resources you still need.
- Close with a reflection (describe, interpret, evaluate, plan) on what to repeat or change.

## Worked pattern
A pet-food brand asks to "connect with younger pet owners". Goal, made SMART: raise share among
owners aged 25-40 from 9 to 12 percent in 18 months within the current budget. What must be
true: younger owners choose differently from older ones; the brand can be positioned
credibly to them; competitors have not locked them in; retail execution can move share.
Known: the brand skews older (client data). Unknown and risky: whether younger owners value
what the brand offers. Question: "Which three purchase criteria rank highest for owners 25-40,
and how does the brand score on them?" Methods: 15 interviews (interaction), then a survey of
400 (response). Deliverable: positioning, channel plan, 3-phase roadmap with owners and KPIs.

## Common mistakes
- Jumping to solutions before the goal and problem are agreed.
- Objectives written as tasks ("conduct interviews") rather than outcomes ("decide whether to enter").
- A to-do list instead of 3-5 phases; tasks with no output or no link to an objective.
- No dependencies listed (client data, approvals), then blaming delays on the client.
- Researching what is interesting rather than what changes the decision.
- Treating the SOW as a legal weapon rather than a shared, adjustable roadmap.
- A final deck that shows work done instead of a decision.
