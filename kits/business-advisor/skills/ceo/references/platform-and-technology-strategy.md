# Platform and technology strategy

> **Area:** `platform-and-technology-strategy` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Work out how a new technology or platform shifts value and bargaining power along a value chain, and choose a response - build, partner, acquire, transform the model, or run an innovation program. Use when a business asks "what does AI, a marketplace or a digital rival do to us?", "should we buy this tech company or contract with it?", "how do we go from selling products to selling solutions?", "when do we start selling a technology that is not ready?" or "how do we roll out and govern AI tools for staff?"

**Outcome:** a map of who creates and who captures value in the business's value chain before and after a
technology shift, a decision on how to respond (and how to own or access the technology), and an
implementation check that the organisation can actually make the change.

**When to use**
- A new technology lets a new entrant (or a customer) do part of your job.
- You run, join or compete with a platform (marketplace, network, app store, model ecosystem).
- Deciding whether to acquire a technology partner, keep a contract, or form a joint venture.
- Planning a digital transformation, or moving from product sales to solution sales.
- Setting up an innovation pipeline (internal ideas, accelerators, contests, venture investing).

## Method

### 1. Map the value chain and what flows along it
List each stage from producer to end user (for music: artist, label, publisher, distributor, platform,
listener). For every link, write what moves each way: product, money, data, attention, services, risk.
Mark which players have bargaining power and why (few of them, crucial input, switching costs, control of
the customer relationship). Re-use the five-force tests from `industry-analysis` on each link.

### 2. Describe the technology shift
Separate shifts in how the product is **made**, **distributed** and **consumed**. For each, ask:
- Which stage becomes cheaper, unnecessary, or newly possible?
- Who now owns the customer relationship and the data?
- Which barriers to entry fall (for example, a new delivery channel lowers the cost of becoming a network)?
Then redraw the chain and re-rate each player's power. Value created by the new technology often goes to
someone other than the inventor.

### 3. Know which kind of platform you are dealing with
| Type | What it does | Value comes from | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exchange platform** | Matches sides who trade (buyers and sellers, viewers and advertisers, farmers and equipment owners) | Cross-side network effects, lower search and transaction costs, trust | Chicken-and-egg launch; multi-homing; disintermediation once sides meet |
| **Innovation platform** | A base others build on (operating system, AI model, developer tools) | Complementors' products; developer lock-in; ecosystem breadth | Open-source alternatives commoditising the base; dependence on upstream suppliers (chips) |

**Content community turning to commerce** (a social-commerce teaching case): users trust the community
because it is not selling; each step toward selling (sponsored creators, live selling, own store, curated
buyers) earns more and risks that trust. Watch three signals: users who research on you but buy elsewhere
(leakage), dependence on a few star creators, and fake reviews or counterfeits from third-party sellers.
Choose the commerce model on purpose: commission on creators' sales, an open marketplace, a store you run
yourself (needs supply chain and range), or curators paid by commission. Detail: `ceo/references/platform-and-technology-strategy/playbooks.md`, section 3.

For a multi-product firm, treat its brand, distribution and customer access as an internal exchange
platform that each product rides on; synergy is real only if products actually use the shared asset.

### 4. Work out bargaining power layer by layer (the stack)
For layered technologies (chips, models, tools, applications, users), ask at each boundary:
- Which layer is scarcer and harder to substitute right now?
- How could the weaker layer gain power: multi-source suppliers, own a scarce input, build switching costs
  into users, aggregate demand, specialise on a use case the stronger layer won't serve?
- Where can money actually be made: selling the "shovels" (infrastructure), the "gold" (the core model),
  or the "jewellery" (applications)? Moats are usually thinnest where entry is easiest and knowledge spreads fastest.

### 4a. When your suppliers own your cost of goods (a music-streaming teaching case)
A platform that licenses its core content from a few concentrated suppliers, and pays them a share of
revenue, cannot widen its gross margin by growing: the cost rises with every sale. Check for this pattern
(few suppliers, short licences renegotiated every few years, cost set as a percentage of revenue, a free
tier that costs more per pound of revenue than the paid one). Ways out, roughly in order of risk:
- **Raise price once user growth slows** in a market; grow users first while the value-for-money gap is wide.
- **Sell to the supply side:** promotion, data and campaign tools for the creators or sellers who need the
  audience. That revenue carries no licence cost.
- **Add content you own or license on better terms** (commissioned shows, new formats) and steer listening to
  it. Costly exclusives raise engagement but must be judged on churn and margin, not hype.
- **Change the payout rules** (thresholds, per-user pools) to cut waste; expect the small suppliers to read it
  as being squeezed.
Two outside threats: rivals inside large ecosystems who can run the same service as a loss leader for their
devices or memberships, and white-label tools that make launching a rival service cheap. Neither can be
fought on price; fight on the supply-side services and the data that the ecosystem rivals do not build.

### 4b. Earn money from a technology you give away (an open-source AI teaching case)
Open release wins adoption fast and pressures closed rivals' prices, but leaves no built-in revenue while
the costs of the next version stay high, and lets others (cloud hosts) resell it. Three paths, often combined:
| Path | How money comes | Gives | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial or dual licence | Free licence for community use, paid licence (support, updates, service levels) for firms or hosts | Direct, scalable revenue; stops free riding by cloud hosts | Community may feel betrayed; legal and support load |
| Partnerships | Partners host, tune or embed it and share revenue | Reach, partner handles delivery and compliance | Less control and customer contact; partner can switch to a rival |
| Value-added services | Free core; paid hosting, fine-tuning, tools, enterprise service levels | Loyalty and switching costs grow with use | Slowest; revenue lags adoption |
Decide what stays free for good and say so early; changing the deal later costs more trust than charging
from the start. Where the firm's origin draws regulators' suspicion, partner-hosted delivery can open doors.

### 5. For innovations needing many pieces, map the innovation stack
A radical model (equipment sharing, electric vehicles, telehealth) works only when every enabling piece
exists: financing, maintenance, trust and ratings, payments, logistics, training, regulation. List every
step that must happen for both sides to transact profitably. Each missing piece is either a service the
platform must supply itself or a reason adoption will stall.
In low-infrastructure markets (a farm-equipment marketplace, for example) the platform typically has to supply:
local agents paid per booking for users without smartphones; sensors on the asset so owners can see and
stop misuse, which is what makes renting to strangers safe; job verification that ends disputes; usage data
that lenders accept in place of collateral (pay-as-you-go ownership); and physical hubs for attachments,
repairs and inputs. Test before scaling. Once it becomes essential infrastructure, expect governments and
communities to treat it partly as a utility: data governance and quality across a dispersed network become
part of the strategy.

### 6. Decide how to get the technology: contract, partner, or acquire
Default (strategy consensus): buy on the open market what the market supplies well;
integrate only in limited cases. Lean toward owning (acquisition or controlling stake) when:
- The asset is specific to you and hard to re-deploy, so the partner can hold you up at renewal.
- The capability is core to your future advantage and you must control its direction.
- Contracts can't specify what you need (fast-moving technology, tacit know-how, talent).
- The partner could become a rival or be bought by one.
Lean toward contracts or joint ventures when the market is competitive, needs are specifiable, the
partner's culture or talent would leave after a buy-out, or the price paid would capture the whole gain.
(Factor list is standard transaction-cost practice.)
The full make, buy or ally test (the vertical-integration diamond, licensing as a staged decision, why internal
ventures stall) is in `corporate-strategy`.

**When a partner's power rises at renewal** (an animation-studio partnership case): a partner that now
supplies most of your profit in a line will ask for ownership of its output and a smaller distribution fee,
and can take the deal to your rivals. List three options: renew on its terms, replace it (build or find
another), or acquire it. Acquisition can make sense when your other businesses (merchandise, venues, media,
sequels) earn far more from the partner's output than any rival could, so you can pay a premium no one else
will. The price buys a culture and a few people; protect them as deal terms: keep the creative leaders in
charge, keep the unit's way of working, and give them influence over your own weaker unit rather than the
reverse. Bitter, drawn-out renewal talks are themselves a cost that pushes the partner away.

### 7. Plan a transformation (product to solution, store to digital)
1. Reconstruct the customer's journey end to end, then the digital version, and list which technologies
   make the difference (data, logistics, personalisation, connectivity).
2. Sort initiatives by whether they raise customer WTP or cut cost (see `competitive-strategy`).
3. Check readiness: resources (money, data, systems), capabilities (skills, how people think about the
   business), and cultural fit (incentives, sales comp, what gets celebrated).
4. Check partners: channel partners and suppliers can carry the change or block it. A distributor paid on
   hardware volume will not push a service contract.
5. Choose where the next dollar goes (for example R&D vs. solution sales people) by asking which constraint
   binds first: a product customers can't use, or a product no one can sell.
6. Track evidence in the financials: mix of service/recurring revenue, gross margin, SG&A and R&D as
   percent of sales. Solution businesses usually show higher margins and higher fixed selling cost.

Two worked playbooks, sections 1 and 2 of `ceo/references/platform-and-technology-strategy/playbooks.md`: **product to
solution selling** (size verticals, sell by vertical, new roles, pay, channel rules when a partner finds the
lead but cannot deliver) and **digital retail on thin margins** (cost per online order, loyalty data sold as
supplier media, build or partner by capability, staged spend).

### 8. Design the innovation program
- Pull innovation: the business states problems; teams or startups bring solutions. Push innovation:
  ideas or technologies arrive and look for a home.
- Push programs often fail because no business unit owns the result. Fix that first: a sponsor with
  budget, a defined pilot, and a path to scale before any startup is selected.
- Tools: internal idea programs (bottom-up), accelerators, contests, corporate venture capital. Match the
  tool to the goal: learning, sourcing a specific solution, or financial return. Their typical failure
  points (as seen at a large food-services firm): corporate accelerators often close within two years; a contest can
  produce a winner too costly to integrate; corporate venture capital serves two masters (strategy and
  return), creates board conflicts, and only reaches founders willing to sell equity.
- Large firms are often good at finding ideas and weak at deploying and scaling them. Test the gap: how
  many pilots became standard service? If few, fix the hand-off (a business-unit owner, a set pilot period
  with agreed measures, a budget to roll out) before scouting more. Tool table: `ceo/references/platform-and-technology-strategy/playbooks.md`, section 4.

### 8a. Commercialise a technology that is not ready yet (a quantum-computing teaching case)
Three models, each suited to a stage:
| Model | Fits | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Sell whole systems with a service contract | A few expert buyers who can run them | Sold units freeze at today's capability; heavy service burden |
| Sell access as a service (pay per use) | Once buyers know what the resource is and how it is priced | Little demand while classical alternatives do better |
| Joint exploration or consulting with lead customers | Very early, when expertise is scarce | Pulls scarce experts off the roadmap |
- Paying customers bring their problems with them; selling before the technology works pulls the team off
  its next milestone, and over-promising by any seller can sour the whole market.
- Give early access first to expert partners (universities, specialist start-ups) who can work with an
  unfinished product; keep a small high-touch commercial team so the skill exists when the product is ready.
- Price for a share of the value created only when you can show the value.
- Before adding budget, ask what the team can absorb; materials, people or physics may be the limit.

### 8b. Adopt AI inside the firm (a biotech AI-adoption case)
- **Fix the process before digitising it, and digitise before automating:** a poor manual process becomes
  a poor digital one. Connected systems that capture data once create the data AI needs.
- **Spread tools widely, with a secure enterprise version;** most useful tools come from staff, not the
  central team. Frame it as doing more and better work, not as cutting jobs, or people resist.
- **Govern by criticality:** rate each tool on impact if it fails (low to critical) and audience
  (one person, a team, the whole firm). Low ratings need only the code of conduct; high ratings need an
  owner, quality checks, monitoring, maintenance and documentation, with periodic review.
- **Build AI rules on existing values** rather than a new ethics framework, plus a living do-and-don't policy.
- **Watch for duplicates and orphans:** several tools for one task, none built by the team that owns the
  policy, and stale tools no one maintains. Assign an owning function for each process.
- **Decide which work stays human** (for example, parts of performance reviews), and decide deliberately,
  process by process, what people, systems, robots and AI will each do in a year or two.
- **Training must end in a use case** the trainee applies to their own job; otherwise it does not stick, and
  a central team cannot build every idea.

### 8c. Build a product roadmap for a fast-moving technology (an AI-product teaching case)
1. **Surface every candidate** feature: customer requests, known weaknesses (accuracy, bias, integration),
   rivals' strengths, and new capabilities the technology now allows.
2. **Group them into four or five themes** that state strategic objectives (for example reliability,
   customisation, integration, new modes of input), not features.
3. **Prioritise themes** by customer value, competitive gap, cost to serve and risk; drop the rest openly.
4. **Lay themes across releases** on a steady cadence: a major release every year or so, smaller releases
   between that refine performance and cost. Detail only the first release down to sprints.
5. **Use AI or outside input as a first draft,** then add what it lacks: your own usage data, cost and capacity
   limits, safety and legal risk, and what rivals will ship. (Steps 3 and 5 are standard practice.)

### 9. Judge success with a definition
State what success means (growth, profit, share, strategic control) before calling a platform a success.
Many platforms win users and still lose money because suppliers (content owners, chipmakers) capture the margin.

## Worked pattern
A regional equipment dealer (sells and services forklifts) sees a startup launch an online rental marketplace.
- Chain before: manufacturer -> dealer -> customer. Dealer power: local service network, parts stock.
- Shift: the marketplace aggregates idle forklifts from owners and rents by the day. It owns the customer search; the dealer becomes one supplier among many.
- Innovation stack the marketplace needs: inspections, insurance, delivery, maintenance, payments. The dealer already owns three of five (inspections, delivery, maintenance).
- Options: (a) ignore, (b) list on the marketplace, (c) launch its own rental platform, (d) partner exclusively as the marketplace's service provider.
- Choice: (d) for 2 years with a clause on data access and a right of first offer if the startup sells. Rationale: the dealer supplies the scarce pieces, keeps the service margin (about 35% gross vs 12% on equipment sales), and avoids building a two-sided network it cannot fill. Revisit if the marketplace passes 30% of local rental volume.

## Common mistakes
- Analysing one company instead of the whole chain; the profit often moves to a different layer.
- Calling any app a platform; check for real network effects and complementors.
- Assuming the inventor of a technology will capture its value.
- Buying a technology company to "get" talent that leaves after the deal.
- Starting a transformation without changing sales incentives and partner economics.
- Running accelerators with no business owner for what comes out.
- Vague or jargon answers instead of precise language and facts.
- Growing a platform whose suppliers take a fixed share of revenue and expecting margins to widen with scale.
- Letting a partner that drives most of your profit reach renewal with no alternative prepared.
- Open-sourcing first and deciding later what to charge for.
- Commercialising a community so fast that the trust that drew users is lost.
- Launching solution selling without new roles, pay and channel rules; the old system sells the old product.
