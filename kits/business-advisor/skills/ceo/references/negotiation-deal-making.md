# Negotiation: preparing and making the deal

> **Area:** `negotiation-deal-making` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Prepare and run a business negotiation (supplier, customer, lease, hire, salary, partnership) by setting a BATNA, reservation, target and aspiration, sizing the ZOPA, making and answering offers, creating value through trades across issues, and protecting the relationship and your ethics. Use whenever an owner or agent is about to negotiate price or terms with one counterpart.

## Outcome
A deal that beats your walk-away, captures a fair share of the value, finds the trades that make both sides better off, and leaves the counterpart willing to deliver and deal again.

## When to use
- Buying from or selling to one party: suppliers, customers, landlords, contractors.
- Job offers, salaries, contractor rates, partnership terms.
- "What should I open with?", "Should I go first?", "They said X, how do I respond?"
- Before any negotiation worth more than an hour of preparation.

## Method

### 1. Prepare (most of the result is decided here)
Write a one-page preparation sheet (template in `ceo/references/negotiation-deal-making/prep-sheet.md`).

**BATNA** (best alternative to a negotiated agreement): the concrete action plan you would take if this deal fails. Value it, including short and long-term costs, risk and hassle. Not a vague cloud of options: pick the single best one.
- Improve it before you negotiate (get a second quote, another candidate).
- Estimate *their* BATNA with the same care; it bounds what they will accept.

**Three numbers**:
- **Reservation point** (walk-away, bottom line): the worst deal you accept; anything worse and you take the BATNA. It can sit slightly below your BATNA's value because of transaction costs or uncertainty about the BATNA.
- **Target**: what you realistically expect, based on comparable deals and objective data.
- **Aspiration**: the best you could plausibly justify; use it to set your opening. It must be defensible by a standard or fairness principle.

**ZOPA** (zone of possible agreement): the overlap between your reservation point and theirs.
- Positive ZOPA: a deal is possible; impasse would be a mistake.
- Negative ZOPA (no overlap): no deal is the right outcome; agreeing would be a mistake.
- **Bargaining surplus** = size of the overlap. **Your surplus** = how far the final deal beats your reservation point.

**Also map**: the context; each side's interests (why they want what they want) behind their positions; all the issues, not just price; risk appetite, patience and deadlines; who they are and what they really need. For multi-issue deals build a scoring system (step 6).

### 2. Share information strategically
- Goal one: learn their interests, options and breaking points. Goal two: shape their view of the ZOPA (which depends on how each side sees its BATNA).
- Build a minimal personal connection first; information flows more easily. But stay sceptical; check facts independently.
- Mentioning that you have alternatives adds credibility. Never reveal your exact BATNA or reservation point: it removes any reason for them to beat it by more than a token, and may help them undermine your options.
- Rehearse your answer to "what's your other offer?"

### 3. Offers and concessions
- First offers strongly predict the final outcome; the deal often lands near the midpoint of the first offer and the first counteroffer inside the zone.
- **Go first** when you have good information about the zone (your offer anchors). **Let them go first** when they know much more than you (risk of winner's curse: you open too generously).
- Against their anchor: refocus on your target and their BATNA, counter quickly, don't accept "let's split the difference" unless the midpoint suits you.
- **Every offer tells a story**: give a reason (comparable prices, cost, fairness standard) so the counterpart can disagree without taking offence. Keep a public rationale and a private analysis; don't believe your own hype.
- Concessions: trade, don't give (quid pro quo); no larger than theirs; shrinking each round.
- Fairness rules people invoke: **equality** (same amount), **equity** (according to contribution), **need**. Pick the one that favours you and that the other side can accept. Few people notice when their own fairness view is self-serving.

### 4. Create value, then claim it
Negotiations are mixed-motive: cooperate to grow the pie, compete to split it.
- **Compatible issues**: both want the same thing; make sure you notice so you don't "concede" it.
- **Logrolling**: trade an issue you care less about for one you care more about. Differences create value: in preferences (taste, risk appetite, timing), in beliefs (forecasts), in abilities.
- **Contingent contracts**: when you disagree about the future, bet on it (earn-outs, volume bonuses, price adjusters). Needs continuing interaction, enforceability and transparency; can signal distrust or focus people on the wrong metric.
- Things that don't work: assuming a fixed pie; settling issues one at a time; splitting every difference; compromising before exploring trades; aiming only to feel good; aiming to beat them rather than maximise your own result.
- Practical moves: ask "why?" and "why not?"; package several issues into each offer; make two or three equivalent offers at once and see which they prefer.

### 5. Principled moves to get past deadlock (Fisher and Ury)
Separate the people from the problem; focus on interests, not positions; generate several options before choosing; insist on an objective standard and agree the principle before the number.

### 6. Build a scoring system for multi-issue deals
List every issue; weight issues by importance; set the possible outcomes on each; give points so a whole package can be compared to another and to your BATNA. A good system is complete, measurable on one scale, usable to compare packages, and true to your real preferences. Set reservation, target and aspiration in total points as well as per issue. Plan which trades you would make.

### 7. Style, emotions and satisfaction
- No single style wins. Know your default; be yourself, widen your repertoire. The most effective negotiators prepare, hold high but realistic aims, listen, communicate clearly, take initiative, keep integrity, and leave the counterpart willing to work again.
- Too tough: few concessions, walks out, gets a reputation. Too soft: concedes too much, reveals bottom line. Aim to grow the pie, take a good slice and stay comfortable.
- If you find asking hard: practise small asks ("what's your best discount?"), research and build a strong BATNA, and imagine you are negotiating for someone else (people, especially women, ask for more when advocating for others).
- Emotions spread. Positive emotion aids creative thinking; negative emotion can persuade but wears relationships. People remember how they felt at the end; finish warmly.
- **Subjective value**: how both sides feel about the outcome, the process (were they listened to, was it fair), themselves (did they keep face and principles) and the relationship. It predicts better results in later rounds and better job satisfaction and retention after salary deals. Don't make the counterpart feel they lost.
- Your own satisfaction: before, set an aggressive target; after, judge yourself against your reservation point, not the target.

### 8. Ethics
- Legal floor: fraud is knowingly misrepresenting a material fact the other party reasonably relies on, causing harm. Includes lying about intentions, and sometimes omission. Read the written contract; spoken claims often differ.
- Know your school: "it's a game" (poker), idealist (same ethics as private life), or pragmatist (avoid deception because it costs you later).
- Questionable tactics to avoid or watch for: bluffing about facts, fake authority limits, good-cop bad-cop, decoy issues, reneging, retracting offers, nibbling after agreement.
- Tests: Could you defend it publicly? Would you mind it done to you? Would you advise a younger sibling to do it?
- Defending against others: rely on relationships, check reputation, probe facts, be persistent, keep your standards, be ready to walk.
- "I'm not able to answer that, but tell me what concerns you and I'll see if I can address it" protects your integrity and keeps focus on interests.

### 9. Keep improving
Practice on low-stakes asks, debrief every real negotiation, peer coaching and a personal cheat sheet: `ceo/references/negotiation-deal-making/practice-and-debrief.md`.

## Worked pattern
A bakery renewing its flour supply contract (current price £540 a tonne, 40 tonnes a year).
- BATNA: second miller quoted £560 delivered, plus £1,200 one-off cost to switch: about £590 effective. Reservation: £585. Target: £530 (two other bakeries report £525 to £535). Aspiration: £505, justified by a 3-year commitment and wheat futures down 6%.
- Supplier's likely BATNA: losing a reliable mid-size account in a slow quarter; estimated reservation around £515.
- ZOPA £515 to £585. Bakery opens at £505 with the futures story and a 3-year term.
- Issues: price, term, delivery frequency, payment days. The bakery cares most about price; the miller about volume certainty and fewer deliveries. Trade: 3-year term and fortnightly instead of weekly deliveries for £522, with a price adjuster if futures move more than 10%.
- Result £522: £63 a tonne better than reservation, about £2,500 a year; the miller gets volume and lower logistics cost.

## Common mistakes
- Negotiating without a BATNA, or overrating a vague one.
- Revealing your bottom line; or believing theirs when they announce it.
- Splitting the difference on an anchor you never tested.
- Haggling only on price when other issues hold cheap trades.
- Agreement bias: closing a deal that is worse than walking away.
- Winning the terms but souring the relationship, so the deal is poorly carried out.
- Large, quick concessions; one-sided concessions.
