# Founder decisions before the plan and the raise

Depth for `ceo/references/business-plan-and-pitch.md` section 10.

## 1. Pre-start analysis: answer before committing

Aim: decide whether the opportunity is attractive, how big the effort is, and a route through its risks.
Work through every step before the first one, because each depends on the others.
1. **Opportunity window:** size, growth, how long it lasts, and what forces (technology, regulation,
   tastes) are creating it. Fit the window to your own goals, skills and time frame.
2. **Return:** adequate against the capital, time, risk and your other options. Long, capital-hungry,
   risky ventures make sense only if they create very large value, and repeated rounds can leave founders
   with little of it.
3. **Attractive traits:** steady growth in a defined niche for 5 to 7 years; recurring revenue;
   operating leverage; growth funded largely from internal cash; growing debt capacity (collateral or
   earnings); value built within 3 to 5 years; real harvest options; a high after-tax return (40% or more
   is a common bar).
4. **Options:** prefer opportunities that open further moves; avoid ones that use up resources, close off
   alliances or lock in a technology.
5. **Durability:** imitators, substitutes, technology and staff turnover will erode profit; know which
   threats are internal (manageable) and external (monitor).
6. **Business concept:** the concrete choices (own or franchise, location, central or local production,
   regional or national), barriers to entry you can build (cost, distribution, patents, secrets, focus),
   customers who are identifiable, reachable and willing to change, and whether powerful suppliers or
   buyers could take the profit.

## 2. Resources: the minimum set and how to control it

- List what the team already controls (skills, resources, relationships). The less it brings, the more
  fragile the venture.
- For the rest (money, sales, technology, production, development, people, management, systems), find
  who can supply it. Control does not require ownership: rent, license, subcontract, pay royalties.
- Own only what gives a lasting edge as scale grows, what rivals must be kept from controlling (often key
  people), and what needs day-to-day direction. Self-enforcing work (making to a spec) can be contracted.
- Quality need not be uniform: used equipment is fine where output quality is what matters.
- Choose a level for each resource between bare minimum (cheap, risky) and comfortable (costly, and not
  necessarily safer). More resources do not automatically mean less risk.
- Know what each provider wants beyond money (advancement, status, lower risk, fit with their own
  operations) and build incentives around it. Deals where nobody gets enough ("thin deals") break.
- Regulatory compliance: licences, operating rules, product approvals and testing, insurance and bonding.
  Some approvals take long and cost a lot; start early.

## 3. Checkpoints and reserves

- Break the long goal into milestones that each lower risk. Each one is when new investors, lenders or
  suppliers can be brought in on better terms; founders who plan around them keep more ownership.
- Hold a reserve: ventures run about three times as long and twice as costly as planned (or the reverse).
  Money raised in distress comes on harsh terms: new money rarely buys out old money, and often demands
  that old money step back in priority and control.

## 4. Legal form, founders' agreement, IP

Founders tend to make these choices quickly on cost alone; they deserve a deliberate decision and advice.
- **Entity:** a sole proprietorship or general partnership leaves personal assets exposed. A corporation
  or LLC limits liability. A C corporation suits venture investors (preferred stock, many owners); an S
  corporation passes income through but limits owners; an LLC passes income through with flexible profit
  splits but is awkward for many funds. Filing cost differences are small next to the cost of changing
  form later (standard practice for the fund point; check current law).
- **Founders' agreement:** with no agreement, the legal default may not match what founders intend. Agree
  early, while everyone is friendly: who owns what, vesting, what happens when a founder leaves (buy-out
  price and terms), decision rules, non-compete and confidentiality. Friends and family need it most.
- **IP:** clear the name before using it (a forced rename wastes money and brand); file what matters
  (trademarks, patents, copyrights) and protect unregistered IP by controlling access and use. IP is often
  among the most valuable assets; skimping on it to save fees can lose it.
- Use a lawyer who knows start-ups; responsibility for the outcome stays with the founders, never with the
  advisers.

## 5. Bootstrap or raise

Most fast-growing private companies started on the founders' own savings and credit, with modest sums,
and grew on retained earnings and debt. Raise outside equity only when the business truly needs capital
before revenue (heavy R&D, a race for scale) and the founder can meet investors' criteria honestly. Full
method in `cfo/references/raising-startup-capital.md` section 1a.

## 6. Plan the harvest from the start

- Mechanisms: sale to a larger company, public offering (new shares or founders' shares), sale to a third
  party or to management and staff, or liquidation. Personal-service businesses often cannot be sold and
  are run for cash: that is buying yourself a job, which is fine if chosen knowingly.
- Structure the entity and taxes years ahead to maximise after-tax proceeds.
- Triggers: a need for large new capital, profits peaking, tax or market changes, economic cycles,
  founders' age, health or interests. Blockers: market cycles, tax and legal changes, competitors,
  management problems, loss of trade secrets.
- Avoid moves that close exit options (for example, an alliance that scares off the natural buyer).
- Treat creditors, investors and staff fairly at exit if you want to work with them again.

## 7. The founder's changing role

Early hires are usually willing generalists. As the firm grows, the founder must delegate, become a
manager of managers, bring in seasoned people, and sometimes reverse the very habits (cheapness,
customisation, cash focus) that got the business started. Be candid with early staff that their growth
depends on performance.
