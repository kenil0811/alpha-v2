# The written plan: sections, questions and the financial model

Depth for `ceo/references/business-plan-and-pitch.md` section 9.

## Section order

| # | Section | What it must show | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Summary | What it is, for whom, why now, the ask | 1 page |
| 2 | People | Each member's knowledge of the product, process and market; whom they know; reputation; whether they have worked together (not just studied together); gaps and the plan to fill them | 2 to 3 pages |
| 3 | Opportunity | Market size and growth; industry structure; customer and buying process; price; cost to acquire, make, deliver, support and keep a customer; growth paths; competition | 4 to 6 pages |
| 4 | Context | Regulation, macroeconomy, technology, financing climate; how each may change; the response | 1 page |
| 5 | Risk and reward | What can go wrong and right; what management will do; the risk pictures; exit options | 1 to 2 pages |
| 6 | Model | Drivers, break-even, cash curve, capital needed by stage | 2 to 3 pages, near the back |
| 7 | The deal | Amount, stages, instrument, use of funds | 1 page |

Page counts are a guide (standard practice).

## Question lists

**People (ask of each founder):** origin, education, employers, past accomplishments, reputation, directly
relevant experience, skills, realism about the odds, who else is needed, willingness to recruit strong
people, response to adversity, capacity for hard choices, commitment, motives.

**Opportunity:** who the customer is; how they decide to buy; how compelling the purchase is; price; how
each segment will be reached; time and money to win a customer; cost to make and deliver; cost to support;
how easily the customer is kept.

**Cash cycle:** when resources must be bought and paid for; how long a customer takes to win; how long until
the customer pays; how much equipment each dollar of sales needs. The ideal is to buy low, sell high,
collect early and pay late; say honestly how close the venture comes.

**Competition:** current rivals and their resources, strengths and weaknesses; how they will respond to
entry and how you will answer; who else could see and chase the same gap; whether any rival can be turned
into an ally. A plan claiming an unassailable lead is, by that claim, naive.

**Opportunity traps to address:** an industry that is structurally poor (many similar rivals, powerful
buyers, short product lives, heavy ongoing investment); a "better mousetrap" built by an inventor who will
not spend on or share with the business side; an arbitrage (a price gap) that will close, so the plan must
say how its profits build a lasting business before it does.

## Two risk pictures

1. **The hole:** cumulative cash over time. Show the depth of the hole (money needed), when cash turns
   positive, and the slope of the payoff after. Optimists draw a shallow hole and a steep climb; investors
   correct for it.
2. **The return spread:** the chance of each outcome (total loss, modest return, good return, big hit).
   Most ventures either fail outright or return well; little mass sits at small losses. It tells the
   investor what class of bet this is.

## The financial model (the cash flow formula)

Model the few drivers rather than every line (the driver differs by industry: yield in manufacturing,
renewal rate in subscriptions, channel mix in software). Answer:
- Contribution margin per unit, and how it changes with scale.
- Fixed costs, the break-even volume and capacity use they imply, and break-even volume as a share of
  the addressable market (if break-even needs a large share, the model is in trouble).
- Working capital and equipment needed per dollar of revenue.
- How margin, fixed costs and investment per dollar of revenue change over time.
- The cash curve: how deep the trough is and when it is reached. That depth, plus a reserve, is the raise.
- Customer economics: lifetime value of contribution versus acquisition cost, by channel; conversion
  funnel; for viral products, the referral rate (see `cmo/references/market-sizing-and-unit-economics.md`).

Layout (standard practice): one sheet of assumptions with a source for each; monthly
for the first 18 to 24 months, then yearly to year 5; revenue built from drivers (customers × price ×
retention), not from a growth percentage; a cash sheet separate from profit; three cases and a table of
what each driver must be to break even.

Reserve: new ventures commonly take about three times as long and twice the money planned (or the reverse).
A plan without contingencies is not a plan.

## Plan clichés and what readers hear

| The plan says | The reader hears |
|---|---|
| Conservative projections | Numbers were worked back from a target |
| We need only a small share | So do the many other funded entrants |
| Customers love it | Nobody has been asked to pay, or the customers are friends |
| Low-cost producer | Nothing has been produced yet |
| A six-month lead | Nobody checked who else is working on it |
| The model is proven | One good week at the best site, extrapolated |
| Almost finished | The last part will take as long as the rest |

## Sensible deals

Simple, fair, built on trust more than legal ties, robust to results that differ a little from plan, free
of incentives that push either side to act destructively, and short on paper. Treat financing as a series
of funded experiments: raise enough for each major test (a prototype, a local roll-out), learn the true
economics, then raise for the next.
