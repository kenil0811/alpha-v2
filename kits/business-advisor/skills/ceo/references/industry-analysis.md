# Industry analysis

> **Area:** `industry-analysis` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Explain who captures the profit in a market and whether it is worth entering, expanding in, or investing in, using the five forces, substitutes from outside change, and the industry cost (supply) curve. Use before entering a market, building capacity, buying a business, or when asked "why is this business so hard to make money in?"

**Outcome:** a clear verdict on why the average business in a market earns what it does, which forces
take the profit, how that is changing, and what it means for a specific decision (enter, expand, invest, reposition).

**When to use**
- "Is this an attractive industry?" before entering, acquiring, or adding capacity.
- "Why are margins so thin here?" or "Why did prices fall after we expanded?"
- Preparing the external half of a strategy memo or an investment case.
- When a new rule, technology or shift in tastes may create substitutes.

## Method

### 1. Define the industry
Industry = product x geography (for example "full-service grocery, metro Denver"). Too broad and every
firm looks like a commodity; too narrow and every firm looks like a monopoly. Test the line with
substitution: if customers would switch to another product when your price rises, it belongs inside
(cross-price elasticity above zero; see `pricing-strategy`).

### 2. Name the players in each box
Rivals (industry members), buyers, suppliers, substitutes, potential entrants. Collect: how many in each,
their relative size, and their profitability. Industry guides (IBISWorld or similar) give cost structure as
percent of revenue; that one table usually shows where the money goes.

### 3. Rate each force
| Force | Strong (bad for incumbents) when |
|---|---|
| **Rivalry** (profit-destroying price competition) | Many members of similar size; high demand elasticity; slow growth; little differentiation; excess capacity; high exit barriers |
| **Buyer power** | Few buyers relative to sellers; buyers price-sensitive; low switching costs |
| **Supplier power** | Few suppliers relative to industry members; input is crucial and hard to source elsewhere; hard to switch suppliers |
| **Threat of substitutes** | Many substitutes; substitutes' price-performance is close or improving |
| **Threat of entry** | Incumbents lack advantages of time, size or system rigidity (see the moat table in `competitive-strategy`) |

Mark each force favourable, unfavourable or mixed and say why with a fact. One unfavourable force is
enough to drain profits. Then stand back: which force explains most of the industry's average return?

### 4. Look for substitutes from political, social and technological change (PST)
- Political: a new law or regulation legalises, bans, subsidises or taxes something.
- Social: new preferences or demographics (remote work substitutes for office space).
- Technological: new technology enables a new offer (ride-hailing for taxis).
Widen to a full macro scan when needed: political, economic, social, technological, environmental, legal.

### 5. Add the dynamics the static picture misses
- Every player responds to everyone else's responses. Ask what rivals, suppliers and buyers will do after your move.
- Commitments reshape the forces: long-term supplier contracts, visible spare capacity, reputation for retaliation.
- Organisations change slowly; incumbents tend to get better at what they already do and miss what is new.
- Explicit coordination with rivals to soften competition is illegal; do not recommend it.
- Check who can afford to earn nothing here: a rival that uses your product as a loss leader for its devices or
  memberships caps your price (as in a music-streaming business).
- Concentrated suppliers paid a share of your revenue take the gains of growth; scale alone will not widen
  your margin (method in `platform-and-technology-strategy`, step 4a).

### 6. For commodity or capacity-heavy industries, build the supply curve
When products are similar (metals, freight, basic materials, many trades), price is set by the
highest-cost producer still needed to meet demand.
1. List every plant (or competitor) with its capacity and cash cost per unit.
2. Sort from lowest to highest cost and plot cumulative capacity (x) against cost (y). That is the industry supply curve.
3. Mark expected demand. The cost of the plant at that point is the likely price floor in a downturn
   and a guide to the long-run price.
4. Forecast demand forward with a compound growth rate (CAGR). Use the demand that the industry actually
   serves (for example, total demand minus recycled supply).
5. Add any planned new capacity, including your own. New low-cost capacity pushes the highest-cost plants
   out and lowers the price. Your entry changes the price you planned on.
6. Compute the return at the post-entry price, and the price you would need for your target return:
   required price = (target return x capital per unit + cash cost per unit) adjusted for any costs that move with price.
7. Test how sensitive the decision is to demand growth, rivals' expansions, and input prices.

In a competitive market, positive profits draw entry until economic profit is zero; long-run price
settles near the average cost of an efficient-scale producer (see `cost-and-capacity-decisions`).

### 7. Turn the analysis into a decision
- Attractive industries still need a firm-level reason to win; unattractive ones can be won by a model that
  neutralises the bad force (private label weakens suppliers; a treasure-hunt assortment cuts buyers' price comparison).
- Industry analysis explains the average; it does not predict one firm's profit. Pair it with `competitive-strategy`.
- Write: the verdict, the one or two forces that matter most, the trend, and the implication for the decision.

## Worked pattern
A client wants to open a third commercial laundry plant serving hotels in one metro.
- Industry: commercial linen service, one metro. Rivals: 6 plants, two large. Buyers: hotel groups, a few control 40% of rooms and re-bid every 2 years (strong buyer power). Suppliers: linen and chemicals, many sources (weak). Substitutes: on-site hotel laundries (moderate). Entry: a plant costs $6M; route density favours incumbents (moderate barrier).
- Supply curve: total capacity 90M lbs/year; demand 80M. Cash costs per lb range from $0.28 (newest plant) to $0.41 (oldest). At 80M lbs the marginal plant costs about $0.37, so contracts clear near $0.38-0.40.
- The client's new plant: 15M lbs at $0.30 cash cost. Capacity jumps to 105M against 80M demand; the oldest plants run idle and price falls toward $0.33. Margin at $0.33 is $0.03 x 15M = $450k a year on $6M, 7.5%. Below a 12% hurdle.
- Required price for 12%: (0.12 x $6M / 15M lbs) + $0.30 = $0.348 per lb. Only if demand grows 3%+ a year for 4 years, or a rival closes.
- Verdict: wait, or buy and modernise an existing plant (adds no capacity, so price holds).

## Common mistakes
- Defining the industry so loosely that the forces can't be rated, or forgetting geography.
- Listing forces without facts or numbers; or rating every force "medium".
- Treating substitutes as only today's alternatives instead of asking what PST change will create.
- Planning a capacity investment at today's price, ignoring that your own capacity lowers it.
- Confusing total demand with the demand your producers serve (recycled or imported supply).
- Concluding "unattractive, so stay out" when the real question is whether a different model can win.
