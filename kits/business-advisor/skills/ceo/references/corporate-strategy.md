# Corporate strategy

> **Area:** `corporate-strategy` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Decide which businesses a firm should be in and how it should hold them - whether to diversify (related or unrelated), integrate up or down the value chain or buy from the market, grow by acquisition, alliance, licensing or internal venture, add or sell a business, and organise a multi-business firm. Use when an owner asks "should we get into X?", "should we make this ourselves or buy it?", "should we buy this company or partner with it?", "should we license this or develop it?" or "how should we organise now that we run several businesses?"

**Outcome:** a yes or no on a change of scope (a new business, a supplier or channel brought in-house, an
acquisition, an alliance or a licence, a sale), backed by the resource it rests on, the test it must pass,
the mode of entry that fits, and the structure that will run it.

**When to use**
- A new product line, market or industry is on the table, especially one far from the core.
- Make, buy or ally: bring a supplier, a component, a channel or a technology in-house, or keep contracting.
- An acquisition, a joint venture, a minority stake or a licence deal is being priced or negotiated.
- A firm holds several businesses and asks which to grow, fix, keep or sell, and how to organise them.
- A new technology or venture needs room to grow inside an established firm.
Use `competitive-strategy` for how one business wins; this area decides **where** the firm competes and
what the parent adds. Deal pricing sits with the CFO (`valuation-multiples-and-deals`), post-deal value
plans and exits with `value-creation-and-exit`, and the in-house vs outside cost sum with `relevant-cost-decisions`.

## Method

### 1. Separate the two levels
Business strategy answers how a firm competes in one business. Corporate strategy answers which set of
businesses it is in and how the centre makes them worth more together than apart. Every scope move must
pass one question: will the businesses be worth more inside this firm than on their own or in another owner's hands?

### 2. Find the resource the move rests on (resource-based view)
List what the firm has (tangible: money, plant, patents, data; intangible: skills, teams, reputation) and
what it can do (capabilities: routines that stay when people leave). Run each through **VRIO**:
| Test | Question | If no |
|---|---|---|
| Valuable | Does it exploit an opportunity or blunt a threat? | Disadvantage |
| Rare | Do only a few rivals hold it? | Parity |
| Inimitable | Is it costly to copy or substitute? | Temporary advantage |
| Organised | Is the firm set up to use it? | Advantage left on the table |
Diversify if, and only if, a resource that passes all four can be carried into the new business to **lower
its costs** (economies of scope: shared plant, data, sales force) or **raise what buyers will pay** there.
The synergy must itself be hard to copy: a link any rival can make earns nothing extra.

Look hardest at **bundles**: resources worth most together (characters plus park operation; a brand plus a
service network). A complementarity, where each asset raises the other's value, is hard to imitate because
a rival must copy the whole set. Name each complementarity and say which way the value flows.

Two patterns from teaching cases are worth testing for:
- **Infrastructure as a launch pad.** Shared fulfilment, payments, data and customer accounts let each new
  category start at a lower incremental cost and scale faster than a stand-alone entrant. The test: does
  each new line genuinely use the shared base, and does it add volume that makes the base cheaper for the rest?
- **An internal capability sold outside.** Something built to run your own business (an IT platform, a
  logistics network, a testing lab) can become a business if outsiders need it and you are already at scale.
  Check the conflict: outside customers may be your rivals, and they will ask who gets priority.

### 3. Place the move on the relatedness ladder
Core business → closely related → increasingly unrelated → entirely unrelated. Relatedness is about shared
**resources**, not shared customers or technology on paper. Default: the closer, the likelier to succeed;
performance usually falls once a firm spreads over too many industries.

### 4. Test the rationale, especially for an unrelated move
Weak reasons (reject unless something below applies):
- **Spreading risk.** Owners can diversify their own holdings more cheaply. Holds only where capital markets
  work badly (thin coverage, weak disclosure, insider dealing), which is why family conglomerates thrive in
  some developing economies and not in the US or Europe. A private owner with all wealth in one firm is a
  similar case (standard practice).
- **Empire and pay.** Managers gain size, pay, power; hubris says "I can run anything". Check incentives
  (see `incentives-and-information`).
- **Capturing the supplier's or buyer's profit.** That profit is already in the purchase price or the start-up cost.
- **Smoother coordination.** Ask why a contract cannot do it; if the answer is real, it is a transaction-cost reason (step 5).
Reasons that can hold:
- A **management system** that transfers across industries (a lean operating system, a pricing discipline,
  an acquisition-integration routine). This is VRIO at the corporate level: the parent's capability, not the
  products, is the shared resource. Test whether a private equity owner could do the same. A
  serial-acquirer case shows what makes it hold: the system is written down as tools, taught by people who
  ran businesses with it, reviewed monthly against a few cascaded measures, and carried by a deep internal
  talent pool. It weakens on very large targets with set cultures, on science-led businesses where the
  tools fit less well, and when deal flow dries up. Playbook: `ceo/references/corporate-strategy/acquisitions-and-portfolio.md`.
- **Well-timed buying** of assets others misprice. Compare with what a fund would pay and charge.
- A deliberate **exit** from a declining core by stepping into a new field.
- **Anticipating innovation shocks**: where demand for new products surges unpredictably, owning a spread of
  bets lowers the future cost of repositioning (adjustment cost). Preempt when the target is unique and a
  later contract would leave you open to hold-up. Judge **comparative** adjustment cost: yours vs rivals'.

Then check the hazards that come with relatedness itself:
- **Conflicts of interest.** A firm that sits on several sides of one deal (represents talent and owns the
  event; runs a marketplace and sells against its sellers; advises a client and sells it products) will be
  suspected of steering value to itself. Disclosure alone rarely ends it; clients leave or sue. Decide which
  side you serve, or wall the units off with separate people, pay and data.
- **Turning a partner into a rival.** Integrating into a supplier's or customer's business (shipping your own
  parcels while being the carrier's biggest client) changes how they treat you in every other dealing.
- **Regulators.** Bundling complements into an ecosystem, or favouring your own products on your own
  platform, draws antitrust action once you are large; price that into the move.
- **Focus.** A cash-rich firm can fund almost anything; that is not a reason to. New lines that sell only
  alongside the core product add revenue but not diversification: they fall when the core falls.

### 5. Draw the vertical boundary: make, buy or ally
Score the activity on the four corners of the vertical-integration diamond (depth and a scoring sheet:
`ceo/references/corporate-strategy/boundaries-make-buy-ally.md`):
1. **Asset specificity with incomplete contracts.** An investment with little value outside the deal
   (site, custom tooling, dedicated capacity, people's custom know-how, timing) invites **hold-up**: once
   committed, the less dependent side reopens terms. Worst under high uncertainty. Pushes toward owning.
2. **Knowledge leakage.** If you cannot price or share know-how without giving it away, or IP emerging in
   joint work cannot be split in advance, and patents are easy to design around or weakly enforced, own it.
3. **Comparative capability.** Who does it better (experience, tacit know-how, scale)? A generic input from
   a supplier serving many buyers is usually cheaper. Integrate without the capability only to build it
   deliberately for the future.
4. **Market power.** Backward to offset a strong supplier or lock up a scarce input; forward to offset buyer
   power or close a channel to rivals. Check antitrust first, and expect new suppliers or channels to appear.
   Treat these as competitive moves (`competitive-moves`, step 5).
Then the cost side of owning: more bureaucracy and weaker incentives than a supplier facing a market.
Decide first whether the aim is **efficiency** (corners 1-3) or **power** (corner 4); argue each on its own terms.

### 6. Choose the mode: organic, internal venture, alliance, licence or acquisition
| Mode | Fits when | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Build organically | The resource exists in-house and time is not critical | Slow; capabilities take years |
| Separate internal venture | Exploration needs other rules: longer payback, entrepreneurial pay, freedom on hiring and sourcing | Existing units resent, starve or capture it; folding it back breaks promises to its managers |
| Alliance or minority stake | The partner's edge depends on strong incentives (founders, scientists) and stays only if they stay independent | Leakage; who owns joint IP; exit terms |
| Licence out | You lack the downstream assets (manufacturing, sales, approvals) and others hold them | Selling too early gives away the upside the next milestone would prove |
| Acquire | The asset is unique, contracts would leave you exposed to hold-up, or speed matters | Premium paid, and people leaving in the integration |
Exploration and exploitation compete for the same money and attention. A large firm cannot credibly
promise a unit it owns autonomy for ever, so when high-powered incentives drive the innovation, an alliance
often beats an internal unit. A licence, venture or option should be valued as a staged decision tree:
`ceo/references/corporate-strategy/staged-options-and-licensing.md`.

Lessons from the cases on each mode (stakes in partners, buying a partner whose power has grown, building
a capability early, exploration units): `ceo/references/corporate-strategy/case-lessons.md`, first section.

### 7. Test any acquisition before the price is set
**Market first, company second** (from the serial-acquirer case): screen the market before any target.
Generalised: big enough to matter; steady growth without wild cycles; fragmented, with many small firms
to buy; no dominant, excellent rival; room for your management system to add value; a kind of business
you understand. Keep a standing list of approved markets so you can move fast when a target comes up.
Then separate **platform** deals (entry into a new market through a strong lead firm) from **bolt-on**
deals (smaller firms folded into a platform you own); bolt-ons are where most of the value is repeated.
A third type, the **adjacency**, is linked to a platform but run mostly standalone; set a return-on-capital
hurdle and payback period per type.
The one deal killer: no one to fill the management gaps you foresee. Walk away from far more than you close.

Before signing, check the base rate: if the firm has failed with past deals of this kind (foreign markets,
a very different culture), say what is different this time.

Three tests (Porter's, standard practice): **attractive** industry (`industry-analysis`);
**cost of entry** does not capitalise all future profit; **better off**: the target or the parent gains an
advantage it could not get alone. In a people business (agencies, advisers, talent), the assets can walk:
buying the firm does not bind its clients or staff, so price only what is tied down.
Then the arithmetic: maximum price = target's standalone value + present value of synergies − integration
costs. The premium must be smaller than the synergies, or the seller keeps the gain. Count cost synergies
fully, revenue synergies at a discount, and put dates on both. Pricing detail: `valuation-multiples-and-deals`.
The main danger is integration: people. Who leaves, whose systems and culture win, how decisions are made,
who owns each fix. Choose the cultural end state before signing (coexist, blend, absorb, or build a new
one) and treat founders' roles and staff equity as deal terms, not afterthoughts. Plan it:
`ceo/references/corporate-strategy/acquisitions-and-portfolio.md`.

### 8. Manage the portfolio and the parent (standard practice)
For each business ask: is it in an attractive industry, does it hold a VRIO advantage, and does this parent
add more value to it than any other owner would (**parenting advantage**)? Grow where all three hold; fix
where the parent can add value; sell where another owner would pay more than it is worth to you. A sale is a
scope decision, not a failure; run the exit through `value-creation-and-exit`.

### 9. Fit the structure to the strategy
| Structure | Fits | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Functional | One business, small firm | Can't handle many lines; no clean performance measure |
| Multidivisional | Several businesses, growth by acquisition and sale | Division rivalry, transfer-pricing fights, short-term focus |
| Centralised multidivisional | Related businesses sharing R&D or customers | More bureaucracy, softer measures |
| Holding company | Unrelated businesses, an internal capital market | Little sharing between units |
| Matrix | Need to coordinate function with product or region | Role ambiguity, weak accountability |
The constant trade-off: cooperation between units against strong incentives for unit managers. Unrelated
portfolios call for holding or multidivisional forms with sharp financial targets; related ones for more
centre, shared R&D and pay tied partly to the whole firm. Detail and R&D placement:
`ceo/references/corporate-strategy/structure-and-rd.md`.

From the reorganisation case: a front-back design (global product units own profit, market units own
sales growth, shared services run as a cost centre) gains scale and speed but sets units against each other
when their measures differ. Changing structure, processes, pay and headcount all at once, with no early
results, can sink a sound design. Sequence the change and give each unit a measure that rewards cooperation.

### 10. Recommend
State the scope change, the resource it rests on, the step 4 rationale, the boundary and mode chosen, the
value test (step 7) and the structure. Name the one assumption that, if wrong, reverses the call.

## Worked pattern
A specialty-coatings maker, $40M revenue, strong in water-based chemistry and a technical sales team that
reformulates for customers on site. Three proposals:
- **Enter industrial adhesives.** The chemistry and the on-site reformulation routine carry over (VRIO: both
  rare, years to build). Shared lab and sales force cut entry cost by about $1.2M a year. Related: proceed,
  organically or by a small acquisition.
- **Buy a regional distributor** for $9M. Standalone value $7.5M. Claimed synergies: "capture its margin"
  ($0.8M a year, already in the price, so zero) and freight savings with PV $0.9M. Value to us $8.4M < $9M.
  No asset specificity, no leakage, distributor is no worse at distribution. Keep contracting.
- **Develop a bio-based resin with a 12-person start-up.** Its edge is founders on equity; buying it would
  dull that. Take a 20% stake with first rights on supply and a joint-IP clause, and price the option to buy
  the rest after pilot data (staged tree in the licensing reference).
Structure: two related lines sharing a lab, so a centralised multidivisional form with one shared R&D unit.
Assumption that reverses it: if adhesives buyers buy on price alone, the reformulation routine adds no value.

## Common mistakes
- Diversifying because a market is attractive, without a VRIO resource that travels there.
- Counting "same customers" or "same technology" as relatedness when no resource is actually shared.
- Citing risk reduction for a public firm in a deep capital market.
- Integrating to "capture margin" or "coordinate better" without a hold-up, leakage or capability reason.
- Paying a premium larger than the synergies, or counting revenue synergies at full value.
- Treating the deal as done at signing; most value is lost in integration, mostly through people.
- Promising an internal venture autonomy the parent will not keep, or folding it back before it pays out.
- Licensing away all rights before the next milestone proves the value.
- Choosing a structure by fashion; each one moves the conflict, none removes it.
- Spreading into roles that put the firm on both sides of its clients' deals.
- Buying a people business and assuming its clients and staff come with it.
- Hunting targets before choosing markets; or entering a market with no one to fill the gaps.
- Rebuilding structure, pay and headcount in one move with nothing to show for a year.
