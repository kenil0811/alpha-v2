# Competitive strategy

> **Area:** `competitive-strategy` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Write down, test and sharpen how a business wins - who it serves, what it offers, the practices that deliver it, and why rivals cannot copy it. Use when an owner asks "what is our strategy", "why are we (not) more profitable than rivals", "can this be copied", or before a big, hard-to-reverse choice.

**Outcome:** a one-page statement of the business's strategy (six answers), a check that the answers fit
together and differ from rivals, and a named list of what protects the profits ("moats") and what threatens them.

**When to use**
- "What is our strategy?" or "Why do we make more (or less) than competitors?"
- Before a hard-to-reverse choice: a new location, a new segment, a big hire, a price repositioning.
- When a competitor copies you, or a new technology lets someone serve your customers differently.
- When writing a strategy memo, an investor pitch, or briefing a new manager.

## Method

### 1. Define strategy the useful way
A strategy is an explanation of why a small set of linked, hard-to-reverse choices will succeed.
Three tests follow from that definition:
- It says what the business will **not** do, and who it will disappoint.
- Its choices are few enough that they guide the smaller daily choices.
- It names the outside changes that would make it wrong.

### 2. Answer the six questions (fits on an index card)
| # | Question | Good answer looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Mission:** why do we exist? | Long-term, reflects a choice, short enough to remember. |
| 2 | **Vision:** what will we accomplish? | A number and a date, an implied focus (product or place), inspiring. |
| 3 | **Key stakeholder / customer scope:** who are we serving? | A defined user and use case, and who the buyer is if different. Name who is deliberately not served. |
| 4 | **Value proposition:** what are we offering? | The dimensions customers care about, marked better / at parity / worse than alternatives. |
| 5 | **Operating model:** which signature practices deliver it? | Activities that differ from the industry norm and reinforce each other. |
| 6 | **Advantage:** what makes us hard to copy? | Specific moats, each traceable to a cause (step 6). |

Two checks across all six: **consistent** (people inside give the same answers) and **different**
(the answers are not a rival's answers).

Mission and vision earn their place because they let staff act sensibly when the rulebook runs out,
and because a commitment made in advance lets you disappoint a stakeholder without losing their support.

### 3. Position with willingness to pay (WTP) and cost
Value created per unit = customer WTP minus cost. A business earns more than the average rival only by
widening that gap:
- **Differentiation:** raise WTP more than cost rises (premium price, higher margin per sale).
- **Low cost:** cut cost more than WTP falls (thin margin, high volume and asset use).
- Doing both at once is rare; when you see it, look for a structural reason (scale, technology, a new model).

The choice leaves a footprint in the financials. Use the DuPont identity to read it:
ROE = return on sales (net income / revenue) x asset turnover (revenue / assets) x leverage (assets / equity).
Differentiators tend to show higher return on sales; low-cost players higher asset turnover. Compare
against the industry average before believing anyone's story. (Ratio detail lives in the `ratio-analysis` skill.)

### 4. Build the value proposition with a value curve
1. Pick the user and the use case (the job, described in the user's words, not your product's).
2. List the 4-8 dimensions of value that user cares about (price counts as one; plot low price as high value).
3. Plot each alternative offer (rivals and substitutes) on those dimensions.
4. Choose where you will win, where you will be at parity, and where you will be worse on purpose.
5. Check that the "worse" dimensions matter little to the chosen customer and a lot to someone else.

A quick market-size sense check belongs here: customers who fit the scope x purchase frequency x price.
Do it back-of-envelope with stated assumptions; a beautiful position in a tiny market is still small.

### 5. Map the operating model and its fit
1. Lay out the value chain: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales,
   service; plus support activities (ownership/management, people, procurement, technology).
2. In each box, write the practices that differ from the industry norm (the "signature practices").
3. Draw the links: which practices make others more valuable or cheaper (complements)?
4. Tie each practice back to a dimension of the value proposition. A practice that supports nothing is cost.
5. Note which practices are tradeoffs a rival cannot adopt without hurting its own model. That is where fit
   becomes protection.

### 6. Name the moats (why you won't be copied)
If anyone can copy a profitable model, entry competes the profit away to a normal return. Look for the
cause behind any apparent advantage, then classify it:

| Family | Sources |
|---|---|
| **Time** | Experience curve; brand and reputation; culture; intellectual property |
| **Size** | Scale economies from large fixed investments; network effects from a large user base; presence in other markets (synergy) |
| **System rigidity** | Customer and supplier switching costs; regulatory standards; interconnected tradeoffs and counter-positioning (a rival can't follow without damaging its own business) |

Switching costs come in three kinds: **financial** (fees, re-tooling), **procedural** (time, effort,
risk of learning a new provider), **relational** (people, identity, trust). Most real ones mix all three.

Then run each claimed asset through **VRIDO**: Valuable, Rare, hard to Imitate, Durable, and worth more
inside this Organization than elsewhere. People's general skills fail the "O" test (they can leave and
take the value); only firm-specific know-how, routines and relationships count. Expect most strong businesses
to have several overlapping sources, since each one leaks. Using the same test to decide which other
businesses to enter (diversification, integration, acquisition) is `corporate-strategy`.

Network effects deserve a careful test:
- **Direct (same side):** users make it better for other users.
- **Indirect (cross side):** each side attracts the other (riders and drivers).
- It is only a network effect if users improve the offer for each other at low marginal cost to the firm.
  Scale economies, scope economies and word of mouth are not network effects.
- Ask how much one more participant adds. Value that plateaus early is a weak moat.

### 7. Check unit economics
Pick the unit that drives the business (seat-mile, square foot, customer-month, job) and compute revenue
and cost per unit. A strategy that loses money on each variable unit does not get better with growth.

### 8. Stress-test against change
List political, social and technological shifts that could create substitutes or remove a moat. For each:
which of the six answers breaks? A strategy built on an assumption ("customers will give up choice for
convenience") should name that assumption and how you would know it is failing.

### 9. Write it up (memo pattern)
Executive summary first (150 words or less, the recommendation). Then the analysis, with at least five
numbers in the body. Exhibits only if the text tells the reader what to look for in them. Be decisive.
Full case-analysis steps and the writing standard: `ceo/references/competitive-strategy/case-analysis-and-memo.md`.

### 10. Go further

## Worked pattern
A two-location independent bike shop, net margin 3%, losing share to online sellers.
- Customer scope: commuters and weekend riders within 5 miles who keep bikes 5+ years. Not served: price-first buyers of entry bikes.
- Value curve: better on fit, repair speed, and trust; parity on brands; worse on price and range.
- Signature practices: same-day repair guarantee; free fit with every sale; service plan at $120/year; staff
  paid partly on repair satisfaction. They reinforce each other: the plan fills the repair bench, the bench
  builds trust, trust sells the next bike.
- Moats: relational switching cost (the mechanic knows your bike), local reputation (time). No network effect.
- Unit economics per service-plan member: $120 fee + $180 parts margin a year against $150 of labour cost = $150 contribution.
  400 members = $60,000, about the shop's current net profit, so the plan is the strategy, not a side line.
- Risk to watch: a mobile repair start-up copies same-day repair. Check: would it also have the fit history and the parts on hand?

## Common mistakes
- A mission or vision that names no choice ("be the best") or has no number and date.
- A value proposition that is better on every dimension. Real ones are worse somewhere on purpose.
- Listing practices without showing how they reinforce each other; a list can be copied piece by piece.
- Calling any growth "network effects" or any loyalty a "moat" without naming the cause.
- Treating general talent as an advantage; it walks out the door.
- Reading profits without checking whether the industry or the firm explains them (use `industry-analysis`).
- Assuming the strategy's key assumption instead of stating it and watching it.
