# Nonprofit board governance

> **Area:** `nonprofit-board-governance` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Serve on, assess or strengthen a nonprofit board: a director's legal duties, the board's ten responsibilities, the board-chief executive split, committees, financial oversight, the three modes of governing, and a board self-assessment. Use when joining a board, reviewing a board's health, preparing for a funder's governance review, or deciding whether an issue is the board's or management's.

**Outcome:** a board (or a single director) that knows its duties, oversees money and mission
without micro-managing, and measures and improves its own performance.

## When to use
- Someone is joining a nonprofit board and needs to know what is expected.
- A board's role versus staff's role is unclear ("is this governance or operations?").
- A funder (for example a United Way) scores the agency's governance, administration and finance.
- A board wants to assess itself and set 1-2 year priorities.
- Reviewing a nonprofit's financial health before a vote.

## Method

### 1. Legal basics
- Nonprofits get tax exemption for public benefit; a 501(c)(3) can also receive tax-deductible
  gifts. Incorporation (state) comes before 501(c)(3) status (federal). A program run under
  another body (a church, a fiscal sponsor) may not be a separate legal entity, so liability
  and final authority sit with that body.
- The board holds all legal power and liability; it may delegate authority to the chief
  executive or committees, and take it back.
- Directors are normally unpaid (expenses may be reimbursed); paid staff should not be voting members.

### 2. A director's duties
- **Care:** make informed decisions. Attend, read the packet and minutes, approve the budget,
  understand the financial statements and audit, use independent judgment.
- **Loyalty:** act for the organisation, not yourself. Disclose conflicts, keep confidences,
  never use the seat for personal gain.
- **Obedience:** keep the organisation within its mission, its bylaws and the law.

### 3. The board's ten responsibilities
1. Set mission, vision and values. 2. Select, support and evaluate the chief executive (formal
annual review). 3. Set policy and strategic goals. 4. Monitor programs and improve
performance. 5. Provide financial oversight and ensure adequate resources. 6. Protect assets
and ensure legal and ethical integrity. 7. Link the organisation and community. 8. Help raise
money (most boards expect a personal gift from every member, sized to means, so the
organisation can report 100 percent board giving). 9. Act as ambassadors. 10. Recruit,
orient and evaluate the board itself.

### 4. Board versus management
- Rule of thumb: the board steers, staff rows. "Eyes in, fingers out."
- Board chair leads governance; chief executive leads management. Shared: mission, financial
  and social stewardship, resource development, succession, strategic planning and evaluation.
- No micro-managing by the board, no micro-governing by the executive. The chair-executive
  relationship is the single biggest success factor: define roles, talk openly and often,
  review and recalibrate.
- Test for any issue: Is it about direction, policy, money at risk, the executive, or legal
  exposure? Board. Is it how the work gets done day to day? Management.

### 5. Three modes of governing
- **Fiduciary (Type I):** stewardship of assets, compliance, accountability. Without it the board
  has no legitimacy.
- **Strategic (Type II):** set priorities and allocate resources with management.
- **Generative (Type III):** frame the problem and make sense of ambiguous situations before
  strategy is set ("what kind of organisation do we want to be?").
Good boards work in all three on big issues. Ask, for any major decision, a Type I, a Type II
and a Type III question. Generative work needs open, frank discussion (not formal motions),
learning at the organisation's boundaries (site visits, clients, partners) and reflection on
the past, not only plans for the future.

### 6. Design the board: structure, composition, process
- **Structure:** enough members (a common funder standard is at least 12), at least four meetings
  a year (one per quarter), bylaws reviewed periodically, staggered terms, officer and member
  rotation, attendance requirements, written role descriptions.
- **Committees:** each with a clear charge, at least one board member, minutes, regular reports
  and self-evaluation. Typical: finance and audit, governance and nominating, fundraising and
  development, program, executive; ad hoc committees for short projects.
- **Composition:** skills the strategy needs (finance, fundraising, program, HR, legal, PR, the
  field), diversity that reflects the community served (avoid tokenism), commitment and
  willingness to give and ask. Use a skills matrix to find gaps.
- **Process:** orientation for new members (history, programs, nonprofit accounting, how to read
  statements, roles), ongoing training, leadership succession, periodic self-evaluation.

### 7. Financial oversight
Use the checklist in `ceo/references/nonprofit-board-governance/financial-oversight.md`: reserves, budget process, quarterly
statements, audit, internal controls, revenue diversity and overhead ratio. Questions every
director should ask: How large is the budget and how many months of reserves? Are payroll
taxes current? Any lawsuits? Who holds the bank reconciliation? What share of revenue comes
from the top funder?

### 8. Risk the board watches
Financial (cash shortfall, one dominant funder, fraud), compliance (IRS filings, grant terms,
state registration), operational (program failure, safety, data breach), reputational
(scandal, poor service), succession (board turnover, volunteer burnout, executive departure).

### 9. Board self-assessment
1. Rate performance on each core responsibility 1-4 (poor, fair, good, distinctive):
   shape mission and strategy; select and develop the executive; ensure financial resources;
   provide expertise and access; build reputation; oversee finance and risk; monitor
   performance; improve the board itself.
2. Rate the enablers: size and structure, composition, leadership, processes (meeting quality,
   information, orientation).
3. Rate which responsibilities matter most in the next 1-2 years (low, medium, high).
4. Collect anonymously from board and senior staff; compare the two views; the gaps start
   the discussion. Pick 2-3 priorities and owners.

### 10. Joining a board
Clarify your motivation and time; research the organisation; ask: Who is on the board and
how did they join? What committees exist? Can I see all financial records and the strategic
plan? What is the giving expectation? How many votes take an action? Are taxes current, any
lawsuits? As a new member, listen for two meetings, ask "why do we do it this way?", master
the financials, own one committee niche, and support decisions publicly once voted ("one voice").

## Worked pattern
A senior-meals nonprofit's board reviews a $12.9M budget. Type I: reserves equal 4 months of
expenses (within a 25-75 percent target); audit opinion clean; overhead 18 percent. Type II:
56 percent of revenue is government money, so set a goal to cut that to 40 percent in three
years through catering and individual donors. Type III: "Are we a meal provider or a senior
nutrition partner?" That framing decides whether the capital campaign should fund a bigger
kitchen or a medically tailored meals line.

## Common mistakes
- Boards doing staff work, or rubber-stamping staff work.
- Approving budgets and audits nobody on the board understands.
- Recruiting friends instead of filling skill gaps; no orientation.
- Only fiduciary mode: endless reports, no strategy or framing.
- No annual review of the chief executive; no succession plan.
- Undisclosed conflicts; sharing client information.
