# Case lessons, generalised

to the decision it poses and the lessons that carry to other firms. Own words; no case text. Step numbers

## Music-streaming platform
- Growth did not fix margins: licence costs are a share of revenue, so gross margin stayed flat as revenue
  rose many times over. Losses came from operating spend, not from the core unit economics improving.
- A free tier wins users in markets where paying is a hard sell (prepaid day or week plans help), but its
  licence cost per unit of revenue is higher; free users are a funnel, not a business.
- Price rises came only after penetration was high in a market; the stated order was users first, then price,
  then new product lines.
- Moving into owned content (podcasts, audiobooks) aimed at engagement and lower churn, at great cost; much of
  that spend was later cut. Judge diversification by the margin it adds, not the engagement it buys.
- Selling promotion and data tools to artists monetises the supply side and makes the platform harder to leave.
- Ecosystem rivals bundle the same service into memberships and devices; a standalone firm competes on product
  and the supply-side tools, not on price.

## Media group and its animation partner
- One partner produced a small share of the studio's revenue but most of its operating profit; that
  dependence moved bargaining power to the partner at each renewal.
- The partner's edge was a proprietary technology plus a culture joining technical and creative staff and a
  peer-review habit for every film; the buyer's own unit had lost both.
- The buyer's downstream businesses (merchandise, parks, home video, sequels) made the partner's films worth
  more to it than to any other studio: the basis for paying a premium.
- Long, hostile renewal talks and personal friction almost lost the partner to rivals; leadership change on the
  buyer's side reopened the door.

## Industrial serial acquirer (adds to the `corporate-strategy` lessons)
- Three deal types, each with its own integration and payback: new platform (enter a market through a strong
  lead firm, slowest to earn its return), bolt-on (folded fully into a platform, fastest), adjacency (linked
  to a platform but run mostly standalone).
- A minimum return on capital within a few years, set per deal type, keeps the pace of buying honest.
- The operating system is taught by a small central office staffed by rotating line managers.
- Open question the case raises: does a system built for industrial products work in higher-technology,
  research-led businesses, and can a firm this size still find enough targets?

## AI chatbot product team
- A steady release rhythm (big release, then smaller refinements) sets expectations for customers and
  developers alike.
- The candidate list mixes capability (reasoning, multimodal input, agents), reliability (accuracy, bias),
  and business needs (enterprise controls, integration, customisation); a roadmap must choose among them.
- A model can draft its own roadmap, but the draft misses judgment: usage data, cost to serve, safety and
  what rivals will ship.

## Industrial networking maker
- Hardware was being commoditised while customers wanted their data connected end to end; selling the whole
  data-collection layer was the opening.
- Acquired brands kept separate sales teams and distributor rights, which blocked selling a combined solution;
  the fix was one P&L, sales by vertical, and pricing moved to sales.
- New solution roles were hard to hire for; a clear strategy helped retention more than pay.
- Channel partners that found leads but could not deliver services created a three-way conflict; the answer
  has to keep the lead finder paid.
- Early solution revenue was small against bookings; heavy R&D was still ahead. Self-funding a transformation
  from a temporary boom is fragile.

## Grocery chain
- A large digital programme was scaled back after profits fell under price competition, then rescued by a
  surge in demand that may not last.
- An in-house analytics unit built on loyalty data powered personalised offers and a media business sold to
  suppliers.
- Store picking for online orders was costly per order; automated warehouses cost tens of millions each and
  take years to turn a profit, so the chain partnered for them.
- Rivals with deeper pockets set the pace in digital; the grocer's edge was fresh food, stores near customers
  and the data, not technology spending.

## New broadcast network
- Old barriers fell at once: satellite links made national distribution cheap, and the number of independent
  stations had doubled, giving an entrant outlets to sign as affiliates.
- Earlier entrants had failed for want of advertisers and because transmission was costly; the new one
  anchored itself on owned stations in big cities and bought a studio for programming.
- Entry plan: a few nights a week, a younger audience the incumbents were not chasing, a lean cost base, ads
  priced below the incumbents per rating point, and a roll-out paced by ratings.
- Affiliates got more local advertising time than incumbents offered: pay the side you need most.
- Staying under the hours that legally define a network kept the entrant free of rules that bound the
  incumbents.
- Incumbents were busy cutting costs after takeovers, which softened their likely response.
- The plan left out uncertain studio profits from reruns; count speculative synergies only as upside.

## Social-commerce app
- The community began as shopping advice and became a lifestyle network; its high conversion came from trust
  in ordinary users' posts.
- Many users researched there and bought on bigger marketplaces; closing that leakage drove every commerce move.
- Each model (creators' commissions, star live-selling, own store, open marketplace, curated buyers) traded
  revenue against authenticity; over-commercialising risked the core users.
- Rivals copied the format, and fake content and counterfeits threatened the trust it sold.
- The core audience (young urban women in big cities) did not travel abroad easily.

## Equipment-sharing platform for farmers
- The founder framed the problem as coordination, not a shortage of machines: idle tractors sat near farmers
  who could not get one.
- The platform supplied what was missing: booking agents, SMS and app channels, sensors with remote cut-off
  that made renting to strangers safe, verified jobs, and data that lenders used for pay-as-you-go ownership.
- Physical hubs added implements, repairs and inputs; a digital link alone was not enough.
- It tested and refined before chasing growth, which drew development partners and investors.
- At scale it faced questions of a utility: data governance, quality across dispersed agents, and national policy.

## Open-source AI model developer
- An efficient open model matched closed leaders at a fraction of the cost and briefly shook rival and chip
  supplier valuations.
- Openness drove adoption but brought only modest API revenue; training the next model stayed very costly.
- Options were commercial or dual licensing, partnerships with cloud and software firms, and paid services
  around a free core, each trading speed of revenue against community trust and control.
- Others could resell the free model; several governments restricted the app over data and security concerns.

## Food-services group
- The group ran many innovation programmes and engaged thousands of staff, yet few ideas became standard
  service; the gap was deployment, not ideas.
- The culture of saying yes to every client request pulled innovation one client at a time.
- Options were a new internal programme, fixing an existing one, or an outside lab running short, measured
  pilots with start-ups against problems the business units named.
