# Playbooks for platform and technology moves

Depth for `platform-and-technology-strategy`. Each playbook is drawn from one teaching case, generalised in
own words. Step numbers refer to the area file.

## 1. Product to solution selling (step 7; industrial-networking case)
- **Size the target verticals first:** for each sub-industry, total addressable market and the part you can
  serve; pick the few where your products cover the most of the customer's problem.
- **Reorganise sales by vertical, not product line,** and move pricing from product managers to the people
  selling the solution. Add roles a product seller does not have: a consultant who audits the customer's
  site, and a specialist in the digital layer.
- **Sell to a different buyer:** a product sells to a maintenance or project manager on specification and
  price; a solution sells to a senior technology or operations executive on the outcome (uptime,
  productivity, quality). The sales process gets longer (more stages, a site audit, a proposal).
- **Change pay:** quotas on revenue booked at shipment reward boxes, not solutions; add margin and multi-period
  credit (standard practice).
- **Watch the adjacent layer:** firms that own the customer's data or cloud can move down toward your layer;
  owning the full layer you sit in is the window to move up first.
- **Decide channel roles explicitly.** Split demand creation (your solution team) from demand capture and
  fulfilment (distributors). When a partner finds a lead but cannot deliver the service, the options are: deliver
  through that partner (keeps the relationship, gives up margin), bring in a partner who can (risks the lead
  finder's goodwill), or deliver directly (full margin, angers the channel team). Pay the lead finder something
  in every option, or the leads stop. Help partners build service skills; they can block a change they cannot
  join.

## 2. Digital retail in a thin-margin business (step 7; grocery case)
- Online orders add picking and delivery cost per order that a store sale does not carry; work out the cost per
  order and the volume at which an automated warehouse beats store picking before committing capital.
- Customer data from a loyalty programme is an asset twice: it personalises offers, and suppliers will pay for
  targeted media and measurement (a second, high-margin revenue line).
- Decide build or partner capability by capability: build what differentiates (data, customer experience),
  partner for what specialists do better (warehouse automation, cloud, autonomous delivery).
- Price competition and deep-pocketed rivals can force a pause in transformation spending; plan the spend in
  stages tied to profit, so a pause does not strand half-built assets.

## 3. Turning a content community into a store (step 3; social-commerce case)
| Commerce model | Earns | Needs | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commission on creators' recommendations | A cut of sales the content drives | Many credible creators | Paid posts read as adverts; trust falls |
| Live selling with stars | Large one-off sales | Talent that can sell on camera | Dependence on a few personalities; sameness across shows |
| Open marketplace for brands | Fees and advertising | Seller vetting, anti-counterfeit checks | Bad sellers damage the platform's name |
| Store you run yourself | Retail margin | Sourcing, stock, logistics, wide range | Thin range sends users to big rivals to buy |
| Curated buyers paid on commission | A cut on curated picks | Buyers with taste the users trust | Slow to scale |
Steps: measure leakage (share of purchase research that ends in a purchase elsewhere); pick the model that
closes the biggest leak without crowding out ordinary users' posts; set rules that label paid content and
verify reviews; track a trust measure (share of non-commercial posts, review authenticity) beside sales.
Abroad, the core audience may not exist; localisation is more than translation (standard practice for the
last point).

## 4. Choosing an innovation tool (step 8; food-services case)
| Tool | Good for | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Internal programmes (training, idea schemes, communities) | Skills and bottom-up ideas | Ideas with no business owner |
| Corporate accelerator | Access to many start-ups | Often closes within two years; little reaches the business |
| Matchmaking accelerator or outside lab (fixed-length pilot with set measures) | Solving a stated problem fast | Pilot succeeds, nobody pays to roll it out |
| Innovation contest | A well-defined technical problem | Winning answer too costly to integrate |
| Corporate venture capital | Strategic window plus financial return | Two goals clash; board-seat conflicts; only founders willing to sell equity |
Start from pull: ask the business units and clients for specific pain points, then pick the tool. Count
success by pilots that became standard service, not by start-ups met or ideas logged.
