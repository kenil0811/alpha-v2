# Healthcare business economics

> **Area:** `healthcare-business-economics` · owned by the CEO skill. **Use when:** Run the money side of a healthcare provider (practice, clinic, hospital, care company) - understand payors and payer mix, find and fix revenue-cycle leakage, control supply costs, stay inside the fraud and abuse rules, and choose a strategy under scenarios. Use when a provider asks "why are we losing money?", "where is our cash stuck?", "which service lines or payors pay?", "is this arrangement legal?", or "which growth path should we bet on?"

**Outcome:** a clear picture of where a provider's revenue comes from and leaks, which services and
payors make or lose money, the compliance risks in how it earns, and a decisive strategic recommendation
tested against scenarios.

**When to use**
- Negative or thin operating margins; cash slower than volumes suggest.
- High denials, rising days in accounts receivable, unexplained underpayments.
- Deciding which service lines to grow, cut, or protect; payer-mix questions.
- Referral, physician-compensation or vendor arrangements that may touch fraud and abuse laws.
- Strategic planning for a practice, hospital or care model facing a reimbursement or technology shift.

## Method

### 1. Understand why healthcare is different
- Three parties: the patient (consumer), the payor (pays), the provider. Patients often choose with little
  information and are steered by payors and referring physicians. Payors are a second customer.
- Payor contracts set the price of most care; payment is delayed and contested.
- Payor types: Medicare, Medicaid, commercial insurers, self-pay/uninsured, risk or value-based contracts.
  Commercial usually pays most; government and uninsured often pay below cost.
- Insurance prices risk either by **community rating** (everyone pays the same) or **risk adjustment**
  (price varies with expected cost). Pooling invites adverse selection (see `incentives-and-information`).
- Ownership shapes goals: government (tax-funded, safety net), nonprofit (surplus reinvested, community benefit
  scrutiny), for-profit (investor returns). Physicians may be independent, hospital-employed, in medical groups, or PE/VC-backed; whoever employs them controls referrals, quality rules and incentives.
- Revenue sources: patient care (driven by payer mix, contract terms and revenue-cycle efficiency), grants and government funding, investment income, and non-patient services (parking, food, retail).
- Costs: labour is the largest and hardest lever; supplies are complex; workforce shortages and burnout raise both.

### 1a. Read the market's structure before choosing where to play
- **Map the care pathway** for the condition you serve: prevention and lifestyle, diagnosis (often first by the
  patient), then treatment that is palliative, stabilising or curative, with patients cycling back. Each step is
  a place a business can enter, and each has different payers and competitors.
- **The government payer's formula sets prices for everyone:** private insurers largely follow the public fee
  schedules and bundled per-diagnosis payments. What the formula pays well (procedures, specialists) gets
  supplied; what it pays poorly (office visits, prevention, coordination) is scarce. Ask how a coming change
  in the formula moves your lines.
- **Simple, profitable work leaves the hospital:** physician-owned clinics and retail clinics take routine,
  well-paid cases, leaving the hospital with fixed costs spread over sicker, poorer-paying patients. For a
  hospital this is a threat to defend; for a new entrant it is the opening.
- **Access substitutes:** retail clinics, workplace and school clinics, and the emergency department all
  stand in for a regular primary-care doctor; the emergency department is the most costly one.
- **Coordination is the gap:** patients with several conditions need care joined up across specialists, shifts
  and records; fragmentation causes errors and waste. Models that own coordination (care management, a
  doctor who manages the hospital stay, shared records) answer a real unmet need.
- **Spending is not quality:** regions and systems vary widely in cost and outcomes, more supply of doctors
  and beds tends to raise spending without better outcomes, and best practice spreads slowly. A provider
  managed explicitly for cost and quality together can beat a richer rival.
- **Suppliers are profitable and concentrated:** drug makers buy biotech pipelines, and devices, imaging
  equipment and procedure kits come from a few large firms; expect little price give unless volume is pooled
  (see step 5).

### 1b. Decide whether to invest in patients' social needs (social determinants of health)
Most of what drives health that can be changed sits outside medical care, about half of it in income,
housing, food, transport and similar conditions; these needs also predict which patients will cost most.
- **Who gains decides who pays.** Under fee-for-service, a provider that pays for housing or food rarely sees
  the saving. Under bundled, capitated or accountable-care contracts the saving comes back to the provider,
  so these contracts are what make social-needs programmes pay.
- **Time to payback varies:** healthy food for diabetic patients can move clinical markers within months;
  housing may take years. The longer the payback and the more patients switch insurer or move, the less the
  funder captures; prefer quick-payback programmes unless the contract or mission covers the rest.
- **Play your own role:** providers screen and refer; community organisations deliver the social service;
  payers fund and reward screening and referral; government sets mandates and funding; integrators link them.
  A provider running a food pantry itself works outside its role; partner first.
- **Target:** find high users by combining health, social and local data and rank needs by cost and
  productivity impact, or work locally with community groups to get enrolment. Programmes rarely scale
  unchanged across communities.
- **Measure** inputs, process and outcomes, including second-order effects on health and cost; without a
  standard measure, results are hard to attribute or compare. Write the partnership down: most rest on
  informal shared values, which blurs accountability.

### 2. Profit by service line and payor
For each service line: volume, cost per case (labour, supplies, implants, length of stay, space),
contracted rate by payor, actual cash collected by payor, write-offs. Answering "do we lose money on X?"
needs clinical, operational and billing data joined together. Expect a few profitable lines (often
procedural: orthopaedics, cardiology, surgery) to subsidise losing ones (emergency, primary care, behavioural health), which may still feed referrals into the profitable lines.

### 3. Run the revenue cycle as a process
Eight steps: pre-registration; data and eligibility verification (including prior authorisation);
charge capture; coding (ICD-10 diagnoses, CPT/HCPCS procedures, E&M visit levels); claim submission and
denial resolution; remittance processing (check payments against the contract fee schedule); patient
collections; process review.

KPIs and common benchmarks:
| KPI | Benchmark |
|---|---|
| Days to bill (charge lag) | 2 days or less |
| Clean claim rate (pass edits with no manual touch) | 95% or more |
| Denial rate | 5-10% typical; drive lower |
| Denial appeal rate | High; a low rate means money written off without a fight |
| Days in accounts receivable | 30 or less |
| AR older than 90 days | Under 15% of claims |
| Net collection rate | Above 95% |
| Coding accuracy / coding queue | Above 95% / under 5% of work stuck |
| Days to pay | Commercial ~12-20, Medicare ~18-30, workers' comp ~55-75 |
Providers commonly fail to collect 2-5% of net patient revenue through underpayments they don't dispute.
Fixes: verify eligibility and authorisation before every visit; documentation within 24 hours; scrub claims
before sending; follow up every claim at 21 days; work denials by reason code and fix the upstream cause;
reconcile every remittance to the contract; appeal underpayments (payors test whether you will). Automate
where possible, but adding coders won't fix a broken process.

### 4. Stay inside the rules
- **Privacy and security (HIPAA):** protect patient information; administrative, physical and technical safeguards; breach notification to regulators, affected individuals and, for large breaches, the media within 60 days of discovery.
- **Anti-Kickback Statute:** no payment or benefit in exchange for referrals of federally paid business.
- **Stark law:** limits physician referrals for designated health services to entities they have a financial relationship with, unless an exception fits.
- **False Claims Act:** billing for services not rendered, upcoding, or keeping known overpayments creates liability, including through whistleblowers; individuals can be held liable, not just the organisation.
- **Exclusion:** excluded owners, employees or contractors can't be paid with federal funds; screen them.
Build a compliance program into the culture and every process, while still optimising revenue and cost.
(This is orientation, not legal advice; have healthcare counsel review arrangements.)

### 4a. Buy and roll out clinical IT
- A practice's own medical record does not talk to others; a shared health record across providers is what
  gives the full picture. Ask any vendor how data moves out of its system.
- Barriers: cost per physician, years of learning curve and changed workflow, interfaces built without
  clinicians, security breaches, and vendors locking customers in with their own data formats. Hosted
  systems cost less up front and travel better; in-house systems allow more customisation.
- The buyer is often not the user: a hospital can impose a system that doctors then work around. Involve
  clinicians in choosing it.
- Government incentives tend to pay early adopters most and later turn into penalties; time the purchase
  against the schedule. Telehealth and remote monitoring cut visits and travel and open care at home.
(Incentive programmes change; check current ones.)

### 5. Manage the supply chain
Manufacturers -> distributors -> provider, often through a group purchasing organisation (GPO).
- Distributors: simpler procurement, inventory, stockout replacement, forecasting; relationships matter for pricing and access during shortages, but create critical dependency.
- GPOs: scale pricing, contract management, benchmarking; but may lock in vendors, carry unfavourable terms, have conflicts (suppliers fund them), and matter less to large systems.
- Formularies: choose specific items or drugs to standardise and negotiate; involve clinicians; expect unintended consequences.
- Risks: shortages, offshore concentration, regulation, cost-quality disputes, poor data. Stockouts are safety issues.

### 6. Choose a strategy with scenarios
1. Frame the questions: grow, stabilise or retrench? Diversify revenue? Improve cost structure? Which innovations give advantage? Test advantages with VRIO (see `competitive-strategy`).
2. Pick the two biggest uncertainties (for example, how care will be delivered, how reimbursement will change) and build a 2x2 of scenarios.
3. Test each option in each scenario: where does it win, where does it fail?
4. Recommend one option without hedging. Ground it in the facts (payer mix, volumes, margins, capital, competitors' wait times).
5. State what is given up and why that is acceptable; show how incentives and the operating model (physician pay, staffing, referral flows) must change or the plan breaks.
6. Give a feasible execution path with milestones and the metric that would trigger a pivot.
**Plan on a time cone, not a round-numbered timeline** (a futurist planning practice): years ending
in 0 or 5 give false control. Sort actions by certainty instead. Tactics (about 1-2 years, backed by data);
strategy (about 2-5 years: priorities, resources); vision (about 10-15 years, revised as evidence comes in:
research, investment, workforce); and the systems-level change in the industry you want to help shape.
Reset the cone to the present as evidence arrives. Organisations that loop only between tactics and strategy
end up always catching up.
Typical options: **scale/access** (more primary and urgent care sites feeding referrals, care management, throughput), **focus** (invest in two or three high-margin lines and chase commercial patients, at the cost of community access and a fight for specialists), **partner** (embed a model in a payor or system: faster, less control), **lighten** (bigger panels, more technology: scalable, risks diluting outcomes).

## Worked pattern
A 12-physician orthopaedic group: $24M net revenue, operating margin 4%.
- Revenue cycle check: clean claim rate 86%, denial rate 12% (mostly missing prior authorisations), days in AR 52, AR over 90 days 24%.
- Leakage estimate: lifting clean claims to 95% and denials to 6% recovers about 3% of revenue in cash previously written off = $720k a year; cutting AR from 52 to 35 days frees about $24M x 17/365 = $1.1M of cash once.
- Action: hard-stop scheduling of surgery until authorisation is confirmed; 21-day claim follow-up; monthly denial review by reason code; contract-variance audit of the top three commercial payors.
- Service line view: joint replacements earn $3,100 per case from commercial payors and lose $400 per Medicare case after implant cost. Formulary standardising on two implant vendors via the GPO is expected to cut implant cost $600/case, turning Medicare cases positive.
- Compliance flag: a proposed "marketing fee" to a referring physical-therapy practice per referred patient is a kickback risk; drop it.

## Common mistakes
- Treating payors as a back-office issue instead of a second customer whose contracts set profitability.
- Looking at charges instead of cash actually collected by payor.
- Accepting denials and underpayments as a cost of doing business.
- Cutting a money-losing service that feeds the profitable ones, or that the community and regulators will defend.
- Hedged strategy recommendations that blend options; ignoring how physician incentives must change.
- Revenue growth plans that create referral or billing arrangements regulators treat as fraud.
